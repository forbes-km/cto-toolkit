We Just Had a Major Incident

The response is probably over or nearly over. What happens in the next two weeks decides whether this becomes a story about a system that failed or a story about a leadership team that did not know what it was running. This page sequences the decisions, the questions, and the outputs, and links to the full pages for each.

Reading order

Where to start

Run the response first. The reflection and communication work matters just as much once the fire is out, and it is where most of the lasting damage is done or avoided.

  1. Incident ResponseDetection through postmortem follow-through, with the failure modes that turn a manageable incident chaotic.
  2. Regulatory MapWhich reporting clocks started when you became aware, and who owns each one.
  3. Data Governance & PrivacyIf any personal or regulated data was involved, which notification regimes apply and which clocks started the moment you became aware.
  4. Board ReportingCommunicating what happened upward without making it worse, and the out-of-cycle escalation path that should already exist.
  5. Epistemology & Research MethodsRoot cause versus triggering event, and the correlation-versus-causation trap that produces confident, wrong postmortems.
  6. Technology Risk AppetiteWas this a risk the organization meant to accept, or a gap in the stated appetite itself.
  7. Observability & SRE Operating ModelIf detection was slow or the same few people carried the response, the operating model is the finding.
  8. Reliability as an Economic DecisionTurn the incident's cost into the number that decides the next reliability investment.
  9. Disaster Recovery & Business ContinuityIf this was bigger than a single incident: recovery testing, RTO/RPO, and what the board will ask next.

Questions to ask

Questions to ask before the postmortem, not during it

Answers the CTO should have before sitting in the room. The postmortem is for the team to learn; these are for the CTO to understand what kind of organization had this incident.

  • How long between the failure beginning and someone knowing, and was that someone a customer
  • How many people could have run this response, and how many actually did
  • Was any part of the recovery dependent on a single person's knowledge, credentials, or access
  • Did the runbook exist, was it current, and was it actually used
  • Did any monitoring fire before the impact, and if so, why was it not acted on
  • Which contributing conditions were known and previously deprioritized, and where is that decision recorded
  • What did the response cost in engineering time, cloud spend, credits, and customer concessions, in dollars
  • Which customer contracts carry notification, service credit, or termination clauses that this incident triggered
  • Was the last restore or failover test for this class of system run in the past twelve months, and did it pass
The trap

Fixing the incident instead of the class of incident

The most common outcome of a major incident is a well-executed fix for the specific failure, with the conditions that produced it left in place.

What it looks like

The database failover that did not work gets tested and fixed. The fact that no failover in the estate has been tested in two years is not raised, because the postmortem's scope was the incident. Six months later a different failover fails, and this time the board asks why the first one did not prompt the question.

The counter-move

One question in every postmortem, asked by the CTO personally: where else in the estate does this same condition exist. The answer becomes a separate item with its own owner, funded from the reserve in Capital Allocation, and reported to the board as the real remediation. The specific fix is table stakes; the class-level answer is the leadership output.

Outputs

What should exist at 7, 30, and 90 days

The postmortem document is the least of it.

CheckpointWhat exists
Day 7A written timeline with detection, escalation, and recovery times stated in minutes, not adjectives. A completed regulatory and contractual notification log. A one-page summary for the board in the form the Board Reporting page describes. The postmortem scheduled with the right attendees, including someone from outside the owning team.
Day 30The postmortem published internally, with actions that have owners, dates, and funding decisions. The class-of-incident question answered with a list. A recorded decision on whether the risk appetite or DR posture changes. If detection or response depended on too few people, an operating model change proposed with a cost attached.
Day 90The funded actions closed or visibly progressing against their dates. A restore or failover test run on the class of system that failed. The incident's cost stated in dollars and used in the next reliability investment conversation. The board updated once more, unprompted, which is what turns a bad quarter into evidence of control.

Technical Hiring & Leveling

Leveling by scope and ambiguity rather than tenure, designing an interview loop that covers distinct signal instead of redundant algorithm questions, why independent scoring before group discussion matters, and the difference between culture fit and culture add.

A framework for structuring leveling and interview design. Specific rubrics, leveling calibration, and compensation banding depend on company stage and market and should be built out with a leveling committee rather than from this page alone.
Framework

Leveling by scope rather than tenure

The most common leveling mistake: anchoring on years of experience instead of the scope and ambiguity a person can independently handle.

LevelScope of impactAmbiguity handled
Mid-levelOwns a well-defined project or feature within a teamNeeds clear requirements; escalates ambiguity to a senior or manager
SeniorOwns a system or a significant cross-team initiativeResolves ambiguity within their domain independently; brings options along with the questions
StaffInfluences technical direction across multiple teams or the whole orgDefines the problem as well as the solution in ambiguous, unscoped situations
1

Why tenure-based leveling fails

Years of experience correlates loosely with scope of impact, and loosely is doing a lot of work in that sentence.

The failure

A candidate with eight years of experience doing the same well-scoped, individually assigned work for eight years hasn't necessarily developed staff-level judgment, while someone with four years who's had to define ambiguous problems from scratch may have. Tenure-anchored leveling systematically over-levels the former and under-levels the latter.

The fix

Level against specific, documented examples of scope and ambiguity handled, gathered from the interview loop and reference checks rather than from a resume's years-of-experience line. This takes more work per candidate and produces meaningfully more accurate leveling.

2

A worked example: leveling two candidates

What the fix above looks like applied to two people with very different resumes and very different scope.

Candidate A: eight years of experience, all at one company, owned a single well-defined subsystem the entire time. A product manager always supplied clear specs; ambiguity, when it came up, got escalated upward rather than resolved independently.

Candidate B: four years of experience. In year three, handed an open-ended "reduce infrastructure cost" mandate with no defined solution. Had to define the problem, evaluate three competing approaches, and drive cross-team adoption of the one that won.

The call: Candidate A levels as mid-level, well-scoped work, ambiguity escalated rather than resolved, despite twice the tenure. Candidate B levels as senior: ambiguity resolved independently, options brought along with the questions. Tenure-anchored leveling would have inverted this outcome entirely.

Interview loop design

The most common structural problem: five interviewers independently asking some version of the same algorithm question, leaving signal gaps elsewhere.

What a well-designed loop covers

Each interview stage tests something distinct and non-overlapping: coding ability, system design and tradeoff reasoning, past project depth and ownership, and collaboration or communication signal, each owned by a different interviewer with a defined rubric for that stage specifically.

What a poorly designed loop looks like

Multiple interviewers independently converging on the same type of question because nobody coordinated the loop beforehand, leaving system design or collaboration entirely untested while coding ability gets triple-checked.

4

Independent scoring before group discussion

The same multi-evaluator mechanic that makes a vendor scorecard defensible applies directly to hiring decisions, and for the same reason.

How it works

Every interviewer submits their score and written notes before the debrief conversation starts, and before hearing what others thought. This prevents the most senior or most vocal interviewer's opinion from anchoring everyone else's assessment before independent judgment gets a chance to register.

What it surfaces

A split score, one interviewer strongly positive, another strongly negative, is the most valuable outcome of the process rather than a problem to explain away. It means the loop caught something a single interview would have missed entirely.

5

Culture add rather than culture fit

A subtle distinction that changes what the interview process is optimizing for.

Culture fit

Screens for similarity to the existing team, which quietly narrows the team's collective perspective over time and is also where a lot of unconscious bias hides undetected, since "similar to us" is a very easy proxy for bias to disguise itself as.

Culture add

Screens for alignment with a small set of explicit, named values (how the team handles disagreement, how it treats mistakes) while actively looking for what a candidate would add that the team doesn't already have, rather than how well they'd blend in.

6

Calibration across loops, over time

A well-designed single loop still drifts against other loops without a mechanism forcing them to agree.

Practice

A standing leveling committee, distinct from any single interview loop, periodically reviews leveling decisions across multiple hiring managers and teams, checking whether the same demonstrated scope gets leveled consistently regardless of who ran the loop.

Common failure mode

Leveling drifts differently across teams with no central calibration, so a "senior" hired by one hiring manager and a "senior" hired by another represent different bars, discovered only once they land on the same team and the gap becomes visible to everyone.

What actually predicts job performance

The largest recent re-analysis of selection research, Sackett and colleagues (2022), corrected a long-standing statistical overcorrection and reordered the list. The practical conclusion for engineering hiring is clear.

MethodCorrected validity (Sackett et al., 2022)What it means for an engineering loop
Structured interviews0.42, the highest of the common methodsSame questions for every candidate at a level, scored against written, anchored rubrics
Job knowledge tests0.40Questions about the actual stack and domain, not trivia unrelated to the role
Work sample tests0.33A realistic, time-boxed task that looks like the job: debugging, extending, or reviewing real-looking code

A loop that covers distinct signal

Each stage owns one signal, with a rubric written before the first candidate. Anchored rubrics describe what a 1, 2, 3, and 4 look like at this level so interviewers score behavior, not impressions.

Recruiter screenRole fit, level range, logistics, compensation expectations30 minutes, structured questionsPass or hold, with notes

System designTradeoff reasoning at the right scale for the level60 minutes; problem scoped to the level being hiredRequirements clarified, options compared, failure modes, cost awareness
Project deep diveScope and ambiguity handled, which is the leveling evidence45 to 60 minutes on one project the candidate choseWho defined the problem, what they decided, what they would change
Collaboration and valuesHow they handle disagreement, feedback, and mistakesStructured behavioral questionsSpecific past examples, not hypotheticals

Checklist

Signs the hiring and leveling process isn't working

Patterns that show up when hiring feels rigorous but the underlying signal is weaker than the process implies.

  • Leveling is anchored to years of experience rather than documented scope and ambiguity handled
  • Interviewers ask overlapping questions, leaving signal gaps (design, collaboration) untested
  • Interviewers discuss impressions with each other before submitting independent scores
  • The interview process screens for culture fit (similarity) rather than culture add (what's missing)
  • No standing calibration mechanism exists to check whether "senior" means the same thing across different hiring managers or teams
  • Interview questions have no written, anchored rubric, so scores reflect each interviewer's personal bar
  • Postings go out without pay ranges in places that require them, or leveling bands do not exist to support the ranges
  • An AI screening or ranking tool is in use without the audit, notice, and human review that apply where you hire

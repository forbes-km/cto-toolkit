Working with the CFO

The relationship where every capital allocation debate gets litigated. Distinct from the CEO relationship in what it requires: the CEO extends trust to judgment, while the CFO needs numbers that hold up under scrutiny.

See Technology Capital Allocation for the underlying numbers this relationship is built on, and Working with the CEO for the sibling relationship this one is often confused with.

Different from what the CEO needs. The CEO wants judgment and a point of view; the CFO wants numbers that survive scrutiny.

1

Defending a number the CFO doesn't like

The moment that determines whether the relationship is adversarial or productive going forward.

How it works

Show the derivation along with the conclusion. Walk through the specific assumption that, if changed, would change the number. Disagreement then becomes a conversation about one assumption, which is productive, instead of a vague sense that the number feels wrong, which goes nowhere.

Where it goes wrong

Presenting a number as settled fact with no visible derivation. Any pushback then becomes a credibility fight rather than a disagreement about a specific input, and credibility fights are much harder to walk back than assumption disagreements.

2

When ROI framing doesn't fit, and saying so

Forcing every investment into ROI language is a common, well-intentioned mistake that costs credibility over time.

What's ROI-shaped

Infrastructure consolidation, a specific automation replacing manual work, a feature with a modeled revenue impact. These reduce cleanly to a number, and the CFO relationship is healthiest when that number is presented as exactly what it is.

What isn't, and shouldn't be forced

Security investment, reliability investment, and technical debt remediation reduce risk and avoid future cost; they rarely produce a clean, defensible ROI figure. Forcing one produces a number nobody believes, which costs more credibility than justifying the investment on risk grounds instead.

3

The budget conversation before the budget conversation

Where most CFO relationships go wrong: the timing of when the CFO first sees the numbers, more than the numbers themselves.

Practice

Bring the CFO the pre-ranked capital allocation priorities before the formal budget cycle starts, in a one-on-one setting. The CFO should never see the logic behind technology priorities for the first time in a room with other executives present.

Common failure mode

The CFO's first exposure to how priorities were ranked happens live in the budget meeting itself, which makes every question feel adversarial even when it's just first contact with the reasoning.

4

Capitalization and accounting realities worth understanding

A CFO thinks in capex versus opex and amortization schedules. A CTO who understands the basics speaks the CFO's language instead of translating everything through an interpreter.

Software development costs that qualify for capitalization, versus costs that must be expensed immediately, materially change how a project's cost looks in a given quarter, independent of the project's actual value. Understanding this distinction, even at a high level, prevents a CTO from being caught off guard when a CFO cares more about the timing of a cost than its total, or pushes back on a schedule change that seems purely technical but carries a real capitalization consequence on the books. The rules themselves changed in 2025 on both the book and the tax side; the next section sets out what applies now.

What to do with that as a CTO

The practical consequences sit in how engineering tracks its work, not in the accounting itself.

Practice

Track time by project or initiative in the tool the team already uses, so finance can support capitalization and tax positions without a quarterly scramble. Tell the CFO early when a capitalized project is likely to be cancelled or fundamentally rescoped, because that can mean an impairment charge that has to be planned for, not discovered.

Common failure mode

A capitalized project is quietly abandoned, and finance learns about it at quarter close. The write-off is a surprise, the CFO concludes engineering does not understand the numbers, and every future capitalization request gets more scrutiny.

8

Unit economics as the shared language

The single change that most improves a CTO-CFO relationship is agreeing one or two unit costs and reporting them every month.

Practice

Pick a unit the business already counts: cost per transaction, per active customer, per claim processed, per patient encounter. Report infrastructure and tooling cost against it monthly. The FinOps Framework treats this as a core capability because it turns a growing bill into a question about efficiency rather than a question about spend.

The failure mode

Reporting total cloud spend alone. Total spend rises with success, so the CFO sees a number going up and the CTO argues it is fine, and neither can show who is right without a unit to divide by.

Checklist

Signs the relationship is unhealthy

Patterns that show up when the CTO-CFO relationship has drifted into friction, regardless of how either person would describe it individually.

  • Technology budget numbers get revised after the CFO has already committed them upward, more than once
  • Every investment gets forced into ROI language regardless of whether it fits
  • The CFO's first exposure to a priority ranking happens in a contentious budget meeting rather than before it
  • The CTO can't explain, at a basic level, why a project's cost is phased the way it is on the books
  • Disagreements about a number become personal rather than staying focused on a specific stated assumption
  • The CTO cannot say which current projects are capitalized or what happens to the books if one is cancelled
  • No unit cost has been agreed with finance, so every budget discussion is about total spend

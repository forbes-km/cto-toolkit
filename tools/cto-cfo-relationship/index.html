Working with the CFO

The relationship where every capital allocation debate gets litigated. Distinct from the CEO relationship in what it requires: numbers that hold up under scrutiny, not just judgment that's already trusted.

See Technology Capital Allocation for the underlying numbers this relationship is built on, and Working with the CEO for the sibling relationship this one is often confused with.

What the CFO needs from a CTO

Different from what the CEO needs. The CEO wants judgment and a point of view; the CFO wants numbers that survive scrutiny.

1

Defending a number the CFO doesn't like

The moment that determines whether the relationship is adversarial or productive going forward.

How it works

Show the derivation, not just the conclusion. Walk through the specific assumption that, if changed, would change the number. Disagreement then becomes a conversation about one assumption, which is productive, instead of a vague sense that the number feels wrong, which isn't.

Where it goes wrong

Presenting a number as settled fact with no visible derivation. Any pushback then becomes a credibility fight rather than a disagreement about a specific input, and credibility fights are much harder to walk back than assumption disagreements.

2

When ROI framing doesn't fit, and saying so honestly

Forcing every investment into ROI language is a common, well-intentioned mistake that costs credibility over time.

What's genuinely ROI-shaped

Infrastructure consolidation, a specific automation replacing manual work, a feature with a modeled revenue impact. These reduce cleanly to a number, and the CFO relationship is healthiest when that number is presented as exactly what it is.

What isn't, and shouldn't be forced

Security investment, reliability investment, and technical debt remediation reduce risk and avoid future cost; they rarely produce a clean, defensible ROI figure. Forcing one produces a number nobody believes, which costs more credibility than justifying the investment honestly on risk grounds instead.

3

The budget conversation before the budget conversation

Where most CFO relationships go wrong: not in the numbers themselves, but in when the CFO first sees them.

Practice

Bring the CFO the pre-ranked capital allocation priorities before the formal budget cycle starts, in a one-on-one setting. The first time the CFO sees the logic behind technology priorities should not be in a room with other executives present.

Common failure mode

The CFO's first exposure to how priorities were ranked happens live in the budget meeting itself, which makes every question feel adversarial even when it's genuinely just first contact with the reasoning.

4

Capitalization and accounting realities worth understanding

A CFO thinks in capex versus opex and amortization schedules. A CTO who understands the basics speaks the CFO's language instead of translating everything through an interpreter.

Software development costs that qualify for capitalization under applicable accounting standards, versus costs that must be expensed immediately, materially change how a project's cost looks in a given quarter, independent of the project's actual value. Understanding this distinction, even at a high level, prevents a CTO from being caught off guard when a CFO cares more about the timing of a cost than its total, or pushes back on a schedule change that seems purely technical but carries a real capitalization consequence on the books.

Checklist

Signs the relationship is unhealthy

Patterns that show up when the CTO-CFO relationship has drifted into friction, regardless of how either person would describe it individually.

  • Technology budget numbers get revised after the CFO has already committed them upward, more than once
  • Every investment gets forced into ROI language regardless of whether it genuinely fits
  • The CFO's first exposure to a priority ranking happens in a contentious budget meeting, not before it
  • The CTO can't explain, at a basic level, why a project's cost is phased the way it is on the books
  • Disagreements about a number become personal rather than staying focused on a specific stated assumption
Reference only. Accounting treatment and capitalization rules vary by jurisdiction and applicable standard; confirm specifics with finance, not this page.

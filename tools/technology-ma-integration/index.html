Technology M&A Integration

What happens after the deal closes. Diligence tells you what you're buying; this is the discipline of combining it, sequenced across the first day, the first 100 days, and the first year.

Diligence-stage assessment lives on Technology Diligence. Scoring the combined application estate uses the same instrument as Application Portfolio Management. This page picks up where those leave off, at close, and covers the reasoning conceptually; Technology Diligence's own preview section lists the working-artifact layer beyond this page, a scheduled timeline tool, a decision tree, and a tracker, for readers who need more than the reasoning.
Reference

The five phases

Each phase has a different job, and doing a later phase's work during an earlier one is one of the most common ways integrations go wrong.

PhaseJobFocus
Day 0Don't break anythingIdentity, access, security continuity, communications, critical systems kept running exactly as they were
First 30 daysUnderstand what you haveTechnology inventory, people, vendors, contracts, architecture, security gaps, dependencies
100 daysDecide rather than defaultConverge, coexist, or replace decisions per system, made and assigned an owner, with execution still ahead
100 days to Year 1Execute the decisionsThe migration, consolidation, and platform convergence work, sequenced and tracked against what was decided at day 100; this is most of the calendar and most of the effort
Year 1Test the thesisMeasure realized synergies and convergence against what diligence promised, technical debt remediation, target architecture, operating model

The phases, with the TSA running underneath

Integration work and transition services run in parallel. The phases decide; the TSA exits are when the decisions become real.

Phase lengths follow the table above; the TSA services and exit months are illustrative. See the TSA card further down for how to structure them.

1

Day 0 is about keeping everything running

The most common integration mistake happens in the first week rather than the first year.

Practice

Identity and access continuity, security posture held exactly where it was, communications sent as planned, critical systems left untouched. The goal on day 0 is invisibility to customers and continuity for employees, nothing more ambitious than that.

Common failure mode

Attempting a quick consolidation win, merging two identity systems, migrating a customer-facing tool, before the estate is even fully understood. This creates a new outage layered on top of the transition itself, at the exact moment trust is most fragile.

2

The 100-day converge, coexist, or replace decision

Every inherited system needs an explicit call. The absence of a decision is itself a decision, and usually the worst one.

Practice

Score the combined estate the same way any application portfolio gets scored, then assign each system a named owner and an explicit converge, coexist, or replace call within the 100-day window, in place of a default assumption that everything eventually merges.

Common failure mode

Systems left in undecided limbo well past 100 days, with no owner for the call, while both organizations quietly keep maintaining duplicate infrastructure indefinitely because deciding felt harder than deferring.

3

Days 100 to 365: executing the decisions

The phase this framework most commonly loses, a decision made at day 100 with no visible forcing function behind it for the next nine months.

Practice

Each converge, coexist, or replace decision made at day 100 gets a named owner, a sequenced execution plan, and a specific target date within the year, tracked on the same standing cadence as everything else in this phase. Migration and consolidation work starts immediately after the decision rather than months later once someone remembers it was made.

Common failure mode

The decision gets made and recorded at the 100-day mark, then nobody schedules or resources the execution work. Six months later the system is still officially "decided to converge," with nothing having moved, which in practice is indistinguishable from never having decided at all.

4

The call, worked across common system categories

What the decision looks like against specific, common systems, rather than as an abstract framework.

SystemTypical callWhy
CRMConverge onto the acquirer's systemSales process consistency and pipeline visibility matter more than any feature gap the acquired system might have had
Identity & accessConverge immediately, within Day 0 to 30Security and access control can't safely run on two separate systems for long; this is the one category that shouldn't wait for the 100-day window
Core product data warehouseCoexist initially, converge during Year 1Migration risk is high; premature convergence risks corrupting the acquired product's own operational data before it's understood
Internal ticketing or ITSMReplace both with a new standard, or converge onto whichever is strongerInternal and lower stakes, which is why it often gets deferred the longest despite being one of the easiest calls to make
5

The diligence-to-integration handoff

Where institutional knowledge most commonly gets lost, right at the moment the deal closes and attention shifts.

What works

The people who ran diligence stay involved through at least the first 100 days, so findings, the security gap, the key-person risk, the technical debt hotspot, translate directly into the integration plan rather than getting rediscovered from scratch by a different team.

Common failure mode

Diligence findings live in a report that gets filed away once the deal closes, and the integration team, often entirely different people, rediscovers the same risks weeks into the process that diligence had already flagged in detail.

6

Cultural integration, in this specific window

See Engineering Culture for the general argument about whose culture wins in a merger. This is that argument, compressed into a much higher-stakes, much shorter timeframe.

Practice

Decide deliberately, before Day 0, what's worth preserving from the acquired team's culture and practices, and build that preservation explicitly into the integration plan, rather than assuming the acquirer's culture automatically and correctly wins by default.

Common failure mode

The acquired team's good practices and institutional knowledge get silently discarded as "not how we do things here," the same credibility-erosion pattern described on Engineering Culture, just compressed into a much shorter and higher-stakes window where the damage happens faster and is harder to reverse.

7

Year 1 is where the deal thesis gets tested

The numbers used to justify the deal don't disappear once it closes, even though attention usually does. This is the measurement point for the execution work from Stage 3 rather than a separate deliverable that appears on its own.

Practice

Track cost synergies and platform convergence against the specific figures used to justify the deal, on a standing cadence, with a named owner accountable for the gap between promised and realized.

Common failure mode

The synergies promised during diligence quietly stop being tracked once the deal has closed and organizational attention has moved to the next priority, so no one ever finds out whether the thesis was right.

8

Before close: clean teams and Day 1 readiness

Most Day 0 problems are decided in the weeks before signing, while the two companies still cannot share competitively sensitive information freely.

Practice

Use a clean team, a small group bound by a clean-team agreement and advised by counsel, to review sensitive items such as customer contracts, pricing, and detailed architecture before close. Build the Day 1 plan from its findings: identity bridging, email and collaboration access, finance system cutoffs, and the systems that must not be touched.

Common failure mode

Gun-jumping in reverse: teams avoid all joint planning out of caution, then discover on Day 1 that nobody has admin access to the target's identity provider and the only person who does has already resigned.

9

Transition service agreements (TSAs)

In a carve-out, the buyer usually depends on the seller's systems for months after close. The TSA is where most carve-out cost overruns come from.

TSA elementWhat good looks likeCommon mistake
Service scheduleEvery service listed with scope, service levels, and a named owner on each sideVague descriptions such as IT support that nobody can hold to a standard
DurationSized to the realistic exit plan, commonly 6 to 18 months, with extension terms priced in advanceA 6-month term for an ERP separation that takes 15
PricingCost-based, with step-ups that reward an early exitFlat monthly fees that remove any incentive to move faster
Exit planA dated migration plan per service, reviewed monthly by a joint steering groupTreating the TSA as the plan rather than as the bridge to one
Data and securityAccess boundaries, logging, and data segregation defined per serviceBuyer staff left with broad access to the seller's environment for months
Checklist

Signs integration is drifting

Patterns that show up when an integration has good intentions but no forcing function behind them.

  • No named owner exists for the converge, coexist, or replace decision on a given inherited system
  • Cost synergies promised during diligence aren't being tracked against actuals a year later
  • Identity and access consolidation is still incomplete well past the 100-day mark with no active plan
  • Key people from the acquired company have left without the succession plan that was supposed to prevent it
  • The acquired company's application portfolio has never been scored on the same terms as the acquirer's own
  • The diligence team's findings never made it into the integration plan in any traceable way
  • A carve-out closed with TSA services that have no dated exit plan
Reference only. Integration timelines and sequencing depend heavily on deal size, cultural fit, and regulatory context; adapt accordingly.

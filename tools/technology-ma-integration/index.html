Technology M&A Integration

What happens after the deal closes. Diligence tells you what you're buying. This is the discipline of combining it, sequenced across the first day, the first 100 days, and the first year.

Diligence-stage assessment lives on Technology Diligence. Scoring the combined application estate uses the same instrument as Application Portfolio Management. This page picks up where those leave off, at close, and covers the reasoning conceptually. Technology Diligence's own preview section lists the working-artifact layer beyond this page, a scheduled timeline tool, a decision tree, and a tracker, for readers who need more than the reasoning.
Reference

The five phases

Each phase has a different job, and doing a later phase's work too early is one of the usual ways integrations go wrong.

PhaseJobFocus
Day 0Don't break anythingIdentity, access, security continuity, communications, critical systems kept running exactly as they were
First 30 daysUnderstand what you haveTechnology inventory, people, vendors, contracts, architecture, security gaps, dependencies
100 daysDecide. Don't defaultConverge, coexist, or replace decisions per system, made and assigned an owner, with execution still ahead
100 days to Year 1Execute the decisionsThe migration, consolidation, and platform convergence work, sequenced and tracked against what was decided at day 100. This is most of the calendar and most of the effort
Year 1Test the thesisMeasure realized synergies and convergence against what diligence promised, technical debt remediation, target architecture, operating model

Before close

Clean teams and Day 1 readiness

Most Day 0 problems are decided in the weeks before signing, while the two companies still cannot share competitively sensitive information freely.

Use a clean team: a small group bound by a clean-team agreement and advised by counsel, allowed to review sensitive material such as customer contracts, pricing, and detailed architecture before close. Build the Day 1 plan from what it finds. The plan covers identity bridging, email and collaboration access, finance system cutoffs, and a list of systems nobody touches.

The opposite failure is common too. Out of caution about gun-jumping, the teams avoid joint planning altogether and discover on Day 1 that nobody has admin access to the target's identity provider, and the one person who did has already resigned.

Day 0

Keep everything running

The goal is that customers notice nothing and employees can still do their jobs. The most frequent integration mistake happens in the first week, not the first year: a quick consolidation win attempted before anyone understands the estate.

AreaDo on Day 0Leave alone until later
Identity and accessFederate or bridge so people can reach what they need. Remove access for anyone leaving at close. Review privileged accounts on both sidesMerging the two directories
Security operationsKeep both monitoring stacks running and connect them to one escalation pathReplacing the target's tools
Email and collaborationShared calendars and directory lookup, so the two companies can find each otherMailbox migration
Customer-facing systemsA change freeze, with an exception process that needs your sign-offAnything that changes a login, a URL, or an invoice
Finance and payrollConfirm cutoffs and that people get paid on scheduleSystem consolidation
Handoff

Carry the diligence findings into the plan

Knowledge gets lost at the moment the deal closes and attention moves. The security gap, the key-person risk, and the debt hotspot that diligence found get rediscovered weeks later by a different team.

  1. Keep the diligence leads on through day 100At least part time. They know which answers were evasive and which systems they never got to see.
  2. Turn every finding into a backlog item with an ownerA report filed in the deal room helps nobody. Each red flag from Technology Diligence becomes a tracked item in the integration plan, with a date.
  3. Hold a day-30 readoutDiligence leads and integration owners in one room, going through what was confirmed, what turned out worse, and what nobody has looked at yet.
Day 100

Converge, coexist, or replace, for every system

Every inherited system needs an explicit call. Not deciding is itself a decision, and usually the worst one.

Score the combined estate the same way any portfolio gets scored in Application Portfolio Management. Then give each system a named owner and an explicit call within the 100-day window, instead of assuming everything eventually merges. Systems left undecided past day 100 tend to stay that way, with both organizations paying for duplicate infrastructure because deferring felt easier than deciding.

SystemTypical callWhy
CRMConverge onto the acquirer's systemSales process consistency and pipeline visibility matter more than any feature gap the acquired system might have had
Identity & accessConverge early, within Day 0 to 30Security and access control can't safely run on two separate systems for long. This is the one category that shouldn't wait for the 100-day window
Core product data warehouseCoexist initially, converge during Year 1Migration risk is high, and converging too early risks corrupting the acquired product's own operational data before it's understood
Internal ticketing or ITSMReplace both with a new standard, or converge onto whichever is strongerInternal and lower stakes, which is why it often gets deferred the longest despite being one of the easiest calls to make
Days 100 to 365

Executing the decisions

The phase most integrations lose.

A decision made at day 100 with nothing forcing it forward for the next nine months looks, six months later, exactly like no decision at all. The system is officially "decided to converge" and nothing has moved. Each call needs an owner, a sequenced plan, a target date inside the year, and a slot on the same standing review as the rest of the integration. Migration work starts the week after the decision, not when someone remembers it was made.

Culture belongs in the same plan. Decide before Day 0 which of the acquired team's practices are worth keeping, and write them down. Otherwise good practices get thrown out as "not how we do things here," which is the pattern described on Engineering Culture, compressed into a window where the damage happens faster and the people you most need to keep are the ones with the most options.

Year 1

The deal thesis gets tested here

The numbers used to justify the deal don't go away at close, even though attention usually does.

Track cost synergies and platform convergence against the figures in the deal model, on a standing cadence, with one named person accountable for the gap between promised and realized. Track key-person retention the same way, because the people who understand the acquired systems are usually part of what was bought. If nobody tracks either, nobody ever learns whether the thesis was right, and the next deal gets modeled with the same assumptions.

Carve-outs

Transition service agreements (TSAs)

In a carve-out, the buyer usually depends on the seller's systems for months after close. The TSA is where most carve-out cost overruns come from.

TSA elementWhat good looks likeCommon mistake
Service scheduleEvery service listed with scope, service levels, and a named owner on each sideVague descriptions such as IT support that nobody can hold to a standard
DurationSized to the realistic exit plan, commonly 6 to 18 months, with extension terms priced in advanceA 6-month term for an ERP separation that takes 15
PricingCost-based, with step-ups that reward an early exitFlat monthly fees that remove any incentive to move faster
Exit planA dated migration plan per service, reviewed monthly by a joint steering groupTreating the TSA as the plan instead of the bridge to one
Data and securityAccess boundaries, logging, and data segregation defined per serviceBuyer staff left with broad access to the seller's environment for months

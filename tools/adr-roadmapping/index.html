Framework

What a good ADR contains

An Architecture Decision Record exists to answer one question well after the fact: why did we decide this, given what we knew at the time. Most ADR templates fail at exactly that.

SectionWhat it should contain
ContextThe situation and constraints as understood at decision time, not with the benefit of hindsight. This is the section that goes stale fastest and matters most.
DecisionWhat was decided, stated as a single clear sentence, not buried in a paragraph of qualifications.
Alternatives consideredWhat else was on the table and why it was rejected. Absence of this section is the most common ADR failure; it's what lets someone later ask "why didn't we just do X" and get a real answer instead of a re-litigation.
ConsequencesWhat this decision makes harder later, stated honestly. Every real architecture decision has a cost; an ADR that lists only benefits wasn't written honestly.
1

When a decision needs an ADR

Not every choice. Writing one for every minor decision produces the same fatigue problem as over-gating architecture review.

Write one

When the decision is expensive to reverse, when it affects more than one team, or when you can already predict someone will ask "why did we do it this way" in six months. What counts as expensive to reverse scales with the team, a change that's a quick fix for a three-person startup might be a multi-quarter migration for a five-hundred-engineer org, so weigh it against your own team's actual capacity, not a fixed unit of time.

Skip it

Reversible implementation choices scoped entirely within one team, where the cost of writing the record exceeds the cost of just trying something and changing it later if it's wrong.

Roadmaps for two different audiences

The single most common roadmap mistake: one document trying to serve both an engineering audience and a business audience, serving neither well.

Engineering roadmap

Organized by system or capability, includes technical sequencing dependencies, appropriately detailed about implementation approach, timeframes expressed as sprints or quarters.

Business roadmap

Organized by customer or business outcome, no implementation detail, timeframes expressed as calendar dates tied to business events (a renewal cycle, a fiscal year), directly reusable in the board reporting format described on the Board Reporting page.

Maintain one underlying plan and generate both views from it, rather than maintaining two documents that inevitably drift out of sync with each other within a quarter or two.

The business case, stripped to what gets funded

What separates a business case that gets approved from one that gets deferred indefinitely.

What gets funded

A specific, falsifiable claim (this reduces X by Y amount, measured how, by when) tied to a number the approver already tracks, with the cost of not doing it stated as explicitly as the cost of doing it.

What gets deferred

A business case built entirely around technical merit ("this reduces technical debt") with no connection to a business metric anyone outside engineering tracks. Technical merit is necessary justification, rarely sufficient justification on its own.

ADR conventions that keep the practice alive

The template is the easy part. These conventions are what turn a folder of documents into a resource people consult.

  • Numbers are never reused, even for deprecated or superseded ADRs. The number is an identifier, not a priority.
  • Once accepted, the decision is immutable. Circumstances change, a new ADR supersedes it. Editing the original to reflect current understanding destroys the record.
  • One decision per ADR. A compound ADR is a sign the team is avoiding the harder work of separating decisions.
  • One to two pages. Longer usually means design work leaking in that belongs elsewhere; shorter usually means the alternatives section is missing.
  • Author by role, not just name. "Lead Architect, Payments" carries context that survives staff changes.
Signals of a healthy practice: new engineers read old ADRs during onboarding and find them useful, debates reference specific ADRs by number, superseded ADRs exist and are traceable. Signals of a failing one: ADRs only get written when leadership asks to see one, the alternatives section always contains only the chosen path with strawman alternatives dismissed, no ADR has ever been superseded.

A worked roadmap example

A compressed real example showing the method end to end: strategic priority through capability gaps through sequenced initiatives.

Strategic priority: reduce customer acquisition cost 25 percent within 36 months while maintaining conversion on qualified leads.

What the example demonstrates: three initiatives, all traceable to one strategic priority, sequenced by genuine dependency rather than organizational convenience, each owned by a named business sponsor. The technology choices support the story; they don't constitute it.

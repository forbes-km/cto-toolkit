This calculator implements the same NPV, IRR, benefit-to-cost, and payback mechanics as the full business case workbook, condensed to year-level cost and benefit totals rather than line-item detail. The full workbook separates implementation from run costs, cost reduction from revenue benefits, and documents intangible benefits alongside the scored ones, available through direct engagement.
1

What the model produces

Roll-up numbers from year-by-year cost and benefit inputs, the same structure as the full business case workbook: net present value, benefit-to-cost ratio, internal rate of return, and payback period.

The five outputs
  • NPV: sum of discounted net cashflow across the analysis period
  • Benefit-to-cost ratio: total benefits divided by total costs
  • IRR: the discount rate at which NPV equals zero
  • Payback period: when cumulative after-tax cashflow turns positive
  • Verdict: a plain-language read on whether the case is strong, acceptable, or fragile
Reading it

NPV positive and B/C above roughly 1.5x reads as a strong case, NPV positive but B/C under 1.2x as worth funding but not a slam dunk, worth watching the sensitivity. These are illustrative reference points, not universal thresholds, your organization's actual bar depends on its cost of capital and risk appetite, and should be set deliberately rather than borrowed from this page. NPV negative at base case doesn't automatically kill an initiative with strategic value that doesn't show up in the numbers, but it does mean the strategic argument has to carry the case explicitly, not hide behind an inflated financial one.

What the pre-filled example represents

The default numbers loaded above are a representative modernization program, not a template to copy blindly.

Shape of the numbers

Front-loaded implementation cost in Years 1 and 2, tapering to steady-state run cost by Year 3. Benefits ramp in behind it, typically 30 percent of steady-state value in Year 1, 70 percent in Year 2, full value from Year 3 onward, since adoption and realization lag the investment.

Why that ramp matters

A business case that assumes full benefit realization starting Year 1 is either describing a trivial change or overstating the case. Reviewers who've seen enough of these will discount an unramped benefit projection on sight.

3

Sensitivity: what happens if you're wrong

The single most common gap between a business case that survives real scrutiny and one that only looks solid under one set of assumptions.

Practice

Run the same model at a pessimistic case, benefits 20 to 30 percent lower, costs 10 to 15 percent higher, before presenting the base case. If NPV still clears positive under the pessimistic scenario, the case is genuinely robust. If it only works at the base case, say so explicitly rather than letting the base case stand in for more confidence than the analysis supports.

Common failure mode

Presenting only the base case with no sensitivity shown. Either it was never tested, a real gap, or it was tested, the pessimistic case failed, and it quietly got left out of the deck, which a CFO who's seen enough of these will assume by default even without being told.

4

Choosing a discount rate

The single input most likely to be pulled from thin air, and the one most likely to quietly determine the verdict.

What it represents

The return the capital could earn elsewhere, the company's actual cost of capital if it tracks one, or a hurdle rate leadership has explicitly agreed represents good enough for a technology investment. 8 percent shows up often as an illustrative default for an established company, but it's not a universal correct answer, your own cost of capital is company-specific and should replace it rather than be assumed to match it. A deliberately higher rate, 15 percent or more, is often used for early-stage or higher-risk bets specifically because the higher bar forces only the strongest cases through.

The trap

Picking a lower discount rate because it makes more initiatives clear the bar, which quietly inflates the entire portfolio's apparent quality without anyone deciding to lower the standard. If the rate changes between two business cases with no stated reason, that's worth questioning before either number gets trusted.

5

How business cases get gamed, and how to catch it

A handful of specific patterns account for most inflated cases. Worth knowing what to look for, whether reviewing someone else's case or checking your own honestly.

  • Double-counted benefits. The same cost saving claimed by two different initiatives' business cases, each one looking stronger on its own than the two deliver combined.
  • A skipped ramp. Full benefit value assumed from Year 1, which is either describing a trivial change or quietly overstating the case, covered above.
  • Costs that stop at go-live. The build cost is modeled carefully; the run cost the initiative adds afterward, ongoing licensing, additional on-call load, incremental support burden, never makes it into the model at all.
  • A quietly adjusted discount rate. The rate shifts from the standard used elsewhere in the portfolio, with no stated reason, until the number clears the bar.

Business Case Calculator

A working NPV, IRR, and payback calculator for a multi-year technology investment, built on the same mechanics as the full business case workbook.

This calculator implements the same NPV, IRR, benefit-to-cost, and payback mechanics as the full business case workbook, condensed to yearly cost and benefit totals. The full workbook separates implementation from run costs, cost reduction from revenue benefits, and documents intangible benefits alongside the scored ones, available through direct engagement.
1

What the model produces

Roll-up numbers from year-by-year cost and benefit inputs, the same structure as the full business case workbook: net present value, benefit-to-cost ratio, internal rate of return, and payback period.

The five outputs
  • NPV: sum of discounted after-tax net cashflow across the analysis period. The tax rate is applied to each year's net cashflow, which assumes costs are deductible against other taxable income; capitalized costs are deducted on a different schedule, see Working with the CFO
  • Benefit-to-cost ratio: present value of benefits divided by present value of costs, on the same discounted basis as NPV
  • IRR: the discount rate at which NPV equals zero
  • Payback period: when cumulative after-tax cashflow turns positive
  • Verdict: a plain-language read on whether the case is strong, acceptable, or fragile
Reading it

NPV positive and B/C above roughly 1.5x reads as a strong case. NPV positive with B/C under 1.5x is worth funding, with the sensitivity checked before committing. These are illustrative reference points; your organization's bar depends on its cost of capital and risk appetite and should be set on purpose, not borrowed from this page. NPV negative at base case doesn't automatically kill an initiative with strategic value that doesn't show up in the numbers, but it does mean the strategic argument has to carry the case in the open, not hide behind inflated financials.

Interactive

Run the numbers

Enter costs and benefits as separate lines, named for what they are, over a horizon of 3 to 10 years. Add, rename, or remove lines freely. The tool runs the base case and a pessimistic case side by side (benefits cut and costs raised by the percentages you set), because a case that only works at the base numbers is a weaker case than it looks. Defaults are pre-filled with a representative example. Runs entirely in your browser; nothing is sent anywhere.

2

What the pre-filled example represents

The default numbers loaded above are a representative modernization program. They are there to show the shape, so replace them with your own.

Shape of the numbers

Front-loaded implementation cost in Years 1 and 2, tapering to steady-state run cost by Year 3. Benefits ramp in behind it, typically 30 percent of steady-state value in Year 1, 70 percent in Year 2, full value from Year 3 onward, since adoption and realization lag the investment.

Why that ramp matters

A business case that assumes full benefit realization starting Year 1 is either describing a trivial change or overstating the case. Reviewers who've seen enough of these will discount an unramped benefit projection on sight.

3

Sensitivity: what happens if you're wrong

This is usually what separates a business case that survives scrutiny from one that only looks solid under a single set of assumptions.

The approach

Run the same model at a pessimistic case, benefits 20 to 30 percent lower, costs 10 to 15 percent higher, before presenting the base case. If NPV still clears positive under the pessimistic scenario, the case is robust. If it only works at the base case, say so plainly. Don't let the base case imply more confidence than the analysis supports.

The usual mistake

Presenting only the base case with no sensitivity shown. Either it was never tested, or it was tested, the pessimistic case failed, and it got left out of the deck. An experienced CFO will assume the second.

4

Choosing a discount rate

The input most likely to be pulled from thin air, and the one most likely to decide the verdict without anyone noticing.

What it represents

The return the capital could earn elsewhere, the company's cost of capital if it tracks one, or a hurdle rate leadership has agreed represents good enough for a technology investment. 8 percent shows up often as an illustrative default for an established company, but it is only a placeholder. Your own cost of capital is company-specific and should replace it. A deliberately higher rate, 15 percent or more, is often used for early-stage or higher-risk bets specifically because the higher bar forces only the strongest cases through.

The trap

Picking a lower discount rate because it makes more initiatives clear the bar, which makes the whole portfolio look better than it is without anyone deciding to lower the standard. If the rate changes between two business cases with no stated reason, that's worth questioning before either number gets trusted.

5

How business cases get gamed, and how to catch it

A handful of specific patterns account for most inflated cases. Worth knowing what to look for, whether reviewing someone else's case or checking your own.

  • Double-counted benefits. The same cost saving claimed by two different initiatives' business cases, each one looking stronger on its own than the two deliver combined.
  • A skipped ramp. Full benefit value assumed from Year 1, which is either describing a trivial change or overstating the case, as covered above.
  • Costs that stop at go-live. The build cost is modeled carefully; the run cost the initiative adds afterward, ongoing licensing, additional on-call load, incremental support burden, never makes it into the model at all.
  • A discount rate that moved. The rate shifts from the standard used elsewhere in the portfolio, with no stated reason, until the number clears the bar.

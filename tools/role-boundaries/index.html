Role Boundaries: CTO, CIO, CISO, CPO

Where these roles typically split, and why the boundaries are organizational choices rather than universal truths, ones that still need to be stated explicitly so the same decision doesn't get fought over by two people who each assumed it was theirs.

This feeds directly into Technology Decision Rights, which formalizes the boundaries this page describes into an explicit decision model.

Typical scope by role

Illustrative, not universal. Many organizations don't have all four roles distinctly, and that's often the right call at their size.

RoleTypical scope
CTOTechnology strategy, engineering, architecture, technology-enabled competitive advantage
CIOInternal technology estate, enterprise systems, business technology, IT operations
CISOSecurity risk, cyber controls, security governance
CPOProduct strategy, product outcomes
1

Why these lines move by company type

The same role name can mean genuinely different jobs depending on what the business is.

Where the split makes sense

A hardware or deep-tech company often has the CTO own product direction fully, with no separate CPO, because the product and the technology are close to the same thing. A SaaS company usually splits CTO (how it's built) from CPO (what gets built) cleanly, because those really are separable questions there.

The mistake

Importing a role-boundary model from a different kind of company without checking whether the underlying business maps onto it, which produces two people with overlapping mandates and neither fully accountable for the outcome.

2

The CTO / CIO split specifically

The pairing most likely to exist on paper without a clear real-world distinction behind it.

When it's earned

At real scale, the CTO owns what makes the product differentiated and the CIO owns what makes the business run, ERP, internal tools, IT operations. The split lets each focus without the other's priorities crowding it out, and both jobs are genuinely full-time at that size.

When it isn't

Most companies below a certain size never need both roles. Forcing the split prematurely creates two half-empowered leaders instead of one fully accountable one, and the resulting turf questions cost more than the specialization is worth at that stage.

The CISO reporting line

See Security Program for the full argument: security reporting into a role whose other incentives directly compete with security priorities creates a structural conflict every time a security requirement would slow a release.

Signs the boundaries aren't working

A boundary can exist on an org chart and still not be functioning day to day.

  • Two of these roles both believe they own the same category of decision
  • The boundary was set once at founding and never revisited despite the company changing shape significantly since
  • The CISO reports into a role whose incentives conflict with security priorities
  • No one outside the four roles could explain where one's authority ends and another's begins
  • A new hire into one of these roles has to guess at their own scope because it's never been written down
Reference only. Role structure should reflect your organization's actual size, business model, and history, not a template borrowed from a different kind of company.

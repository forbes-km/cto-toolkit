Technical debt and code health signals

What's diagnosable in a compressed diligence timeline, versus what requires the seller's own honesty.

What's diagnosable externally

Test coverage trend over time (not a single snapshot), dependency freshness and known vulnerability count, build and deploy frequency as a proxy for how painful shipping is, and the ratio of time spent on new feature work versus maintenance in recent sprint history if available.

What requires asking directly

Which systems the engineering team itself is afraid to touch, and why. This question, asked plainly to individual engineers rather than through the seller's leadership, surfaces more real risk than most automated code analysis.

2

Key-person risk

The single most common surprise that surfaces after close rather than during diligence, because it's uncomfortable to investigate thoroughly before a deal is signed.

How to assess it

Map named individuals against system ownership: which systems have exactly one person who understands them deeply, and what's that person's stated intent post-acquisition. Check git history or equivalent for actual bus-factor, not org-chart assumptions about who owns what.

The trap

Assuming a documented ownership chart reflects reality. Documentation frequently lags reorgs and attrition; the org chart says one thing, the actual person who gets paged at 2am says another, and the second one is the real answer.

3

Security posture specific to acquisition context

Different from an ongoing vendor security review, since the diligence question is about accumulated, possibly undisclosed history, not current-state snapshot alone.

What to check

Whether a breach or significant incident occurred in the last 24 to 36 months and whether it was disclosed proactively versus discovered through diligence. Current access control hygiene, specifically whether departed employees' access was revoked, checked directly rather than taken on the seller's word.

The red flag

Any hesitation or vagueness answering the incident history question directly. A clean answer given confidently and a vague answer given confidently look identical in a single meeting; press for specifics (dates, what happened, what changed afterward) rather than accepting a general reassurance.

4

Regulatory and compliance exposure inherited at close

Certifications and audit history transfer with the deal whether or not the buying organization has fully absorbed what they mean.

What to check

Current certification status against the renewal calendar, not just whether a certification exists; an attestation eight months from lapsing with no remediation plan is a different risk than a freshly renewed one. Whether certifications were scoped to match actual current operations, or scoped years ago and never re-baselined.

The red flag

A compliance program that reads as a once-a-year attestation exercise rather than something embedded in day-to-day operations. Ask how a policy change reaches an engineer's day-to-day work; a vague answer here usually means the paperwork and the practice have drifted apart.

5

Integration complexity assessment

Estimating the actual cost and timeline of combining systems post-close, which drives the real economics of the deal far more than the initial purchase price.

What to model

Data model compatibility between the two organizations' core systems, authentication and identity system overlap or conflict, and which systems are redundant (safe to sunset) versus which have diverged enough that migration, not simple consolidation, is required.

The estimation trap

Integration timelines estimated by the acquiring team's own engineers, who are systematically optimistic about unfamiliar systems they haven't worked inside. Weight their estimate down, or better, get a few hours of hands-on access during diligence rather than relying on an outside-in guess.

6

IP and third-party exposure

A separate domain from security or compliance, and one of the most common ways a clean-looking deal turns expensive after signature.

What to check

Whether every current and former employee and contractor who touched the codebase has a signed, complete IP assignment agreement on file, not just a policy that says they should. An open-source inventory checked against actual license terms, specifically anything copyleft (GPL and similar) that could obligate source disclosure if triggered by the deal structure.

The red flag

A core dependency, commercial or open-source, whose license doesn't survive a change of control, or a contractor engagement from early in the company's history with no IP assignment paperwork at all. Both are cheap to check and expensive to discover after close.

7

Engineering organization and talent, beyond one or two named people

Key-person risk is the sharpest version of this question; the fuller version is whether the organization as a whole can execute the roadmap the deal assumes.

What to check

Voluntary attrition over the trailing 24 months against a reasonable industry benchmark, not just the current headcount. Compensation positioning relative to market, since a team paid meaningfully under market carries retention risk the org chart won't show. Whether there's a real bench below the CTO or VP Engineering, or whether departure of one or two people at the top empties the leadership layer.

The red flag

Attrition trending up over the trailing year rather than flat or down, especially concentrated among senior engineers, paired with exit interview themes that repeat. One departure is a data point; a pattern across several is the organization telling you something the management deck won't.

Structure

Where findings go

A finding without a deal-structure destination is an observation, not a diligence output. Every material item from the sections above lands in one of these.

  • Purchase price adjustment, for findings that warrant a quantified reduction in consideration
  • Representation and warranty requirement, for findings where a specific seller commitment protects the buyer
  • Indemnification requirement, for findings creating potential future liability the buyer needs covered
  • Escrow requirement, for findings where holding back part of consideration is warranted pending resolution
  • Post-close investment requirement, for findings that need spending after close to achieve the deal thesis, tracked rather than absorbed silently into "integration cost"
  • Integration planning input, for findings that inform how systems get combined without changing deal terms at all

Checklist

Red flags that should stop or reprice a deal

Not exhaustive, but the items that most consistently turn out to matter more than they appeared to during a compressed diligence window.

  • No test coverage on the systems that generate the majority of revenue, discovered rather than disclosed
  • A single named individual who is the only person capable of deploying or debugging a core system, with no stated transition plan
  • An undisclosed prior security incident surfacing during diligence rather than in the seller's initial disclosure
  • Licensing terms on a core dependency that don't survive a change of control, discovered late
  • Customer contracts with data residency or security commitments the acquiring organization's own infrastructure can't currently meet
  • A certification or attestation presented as current that has lapsed, or was never re-scoped to match how the business operates today
  • A core dependency whose license doesn't survive a change of control, or a contractor engagement with no IP assignment on file
  • Senior-engineer attrition trending up over the trailing year with no bench below the departing leaders

Running the Technology Budget

The operational mechanics of managing a standing budget across a year: variance, showback versus chargeback, and the review cadence that catches drift before it becomes a surprise. Distinct from deciding how to allocate capital in the first place.

See Technology Capital Allocation for the strategic allocation this page executes against, and Working with the CFO for capitalization and amortization basics, which this page does not repeat.
Reference

Vocabulary worth having cold

Terms that come up constantly in a budget conversation and are worth knowing precisely rather than only recognizing.

TermWhat it means
Run-rateWhat a cost annualizes to at its current pace, distinct from a one-time cost that won't repeat
VarianceThe gap between forecast and actual; favorable if actual cost came in lower or actual value came in higher than planned, unfavorable the other way
Accrual versus cash timingA cost gets recognized when it's incurred, which is often a different moment from when cash moves; a signed annual contract can show up as a full expense before a single payment has gone out
Burn rateNet cash spent per month; the number that determines runway at an early-stage company more than total budget size does
1

Forecast versus actual: what triggers a reforecast

Most gaps between plan and reality are noise. Knowing which gaps are normal and which are signal is the skill here.

Normal drift

Cloud costs fluctuating with real usage, a hire landing a month later than planned, ordinary seasonal patterns already built into the forecast. Small variance in either direction, within roughly 5 to 10 percent, is what a forecast is supposed to absorb.

Red flags

A category running more than 10 to 15 percent off forecast for two consecutive months, a single large unplanned commitment, or the same category missing in the same direction every quarter. The last pattern usually means the estimate method itself is wrong, whatever the noise in reality.

2

Showback versus chargeback

Two different mechanisms for making technology cost visible to the teams generating it, with different requirements and different politics.

Showback

Each team sees what it costs; no money moves between budgets. Builds awareness and light peer pressure without the political fight over whose budget absorbs what. The right starting point almost everywhere.

Chargeback

Team budgets are directly debited for what they consume. Creates ownership and an incentive to optimize, but it requires trustworthy, granular cost attribution first. Chargeback built on shaky tagging produces disputed bills nobody believes, which is worse than not having it.

3

The review cadence itself

See The CTO Operating System for where this sits in the broader monthly rhythm. This is what the review itself needs to contain.

What a working review covers

Actuals versus forecast by category, with the variance explained in one sentence per line item instead of a number sitting next to another number. Any category trending toward a reforecast gets flagged as it happens rather than discovered when the quarter closes.

What a review that's just theater looks like

A spreadsheet recited back without anyone asking why a variance happened. It produces the same conversation every month, and no decision ever comes out of it differently than it would have without the meeting.

4

Consolidating a budget across multiple business units

Everything above describes running one budget. Once the technology function serves several BUs with separate P&Ls, possibly on different fiscal calendars or in different currencies, the review cadence still applies but a roll-up layer has to sit on top of it.

What the roll-up needs

A common chart of accounts across every BU, so "cloud infrastructure" means the same category everywhere rather than each BU tracking spend under its own local taxonomy. A consolidation date that accommodates the slowest BU's close, or a documented estimate-then-true-up process for the ones that close later. Currency conversion locked to a single rate source and a single date per period, since a roll-up recalculated at spot rates on different days never reconciles cleanly. The funding model decided on Technology Capital Allocation (corporate-funded core, chargeback, showback, or federal) applied consistently, since a roll-up mixing funding models across BUs produces a total that doesn't mean anything.

Common failure mode

Each BU's finance team builds its own version of the technology budget using its own categories and its own judgment calls about what counts as technology spend at all, and the group-level total is assembled by a spreadsheet that reconciles those differences by hand every quarter. The reconciliation effort scales with the number of BUs and the roll-up becomes less trustworthy exactly as it becomes more important, since a larger, more complex group is precisely when leadership most needs one true consolidated number.

A newly acquired BU is the most common source of a broken roll-up: its categories, fiscal calendar, and currency rarely match on day one. Build the mapping into the acquisition's technology integration plan rather than discovering the mismatch at the first group-level close; see Technology & M&A Integration.

A year in the budget

A technology budget has a shape across the year that is predictable, and most of the surprises come from treating it as twelve identical months.

WhenWhat happensWhat the CTO does
Month 1Plan locked; actuals from last year still closingBaseline every category; confirm the reserve is recorded as reserve and not quietly pre-spent
Months 2 to 3First real actuals; early variance appears, usually in cloud and contractorsSet the variance thresholds per category; fix any tagging or attribution gap now, because showback later depends on it
Month 4First quarterly review with the CFOOne page: actuals, variance explained per line, categories trending to reforecast, decisions requested
Months 5 to 6Mid-year; the point at which a reforecast is still worth doingReforecast any category more than 10 to 15 percent off for two months; reconcile the vendor renewal calendar against the second-half budget
Month 7Second quarterly review; budget planning for next year begins in financeBring the pre-ranked priorities to the CFO one-on-one, per Working with the CFO, before the formal cycle
Months 8 to 9Next year's budget submissionBuild next year from this year's run-rate plus decisions rather than from this year's plan; every ask carries its derivation
Month 10Third quarterly review; year-end pressure beginsDecide what the reserve funds, if anything; resist the year-end spend-it-or-lose-it instinct unless the item was already on the ranked list
Months 11 to 12Close; accruals and timingConfirm which contracts hit which year; a renewal signed in month 12 for next year can land in either, and the CFO cares which
Worked example

A variance that was signal, and one that was not

Two categories drift in the same quarter. The skill is telling them apart quickly.

Cloud, up 18 percent for two months

The cloud category is 18 percent over forecast in months 3 and 4. The instinct is to escalate. The review asks why per line: a new analytics workload is running as designed, and a data pipeline was duplicated during a migration and both copies are still running. The first is a forecast error, corrected by reforecasting the category up and noting that the workload's business owner approved it. The second is waste, and the duplicate is turned off the same week. Net: one reforecast, one saving, no escalation, and the FinOps tagging gap that hid the duplicate becomes a fix with an owner.

Contractors, up 12 percent every quarter

The contractor line has been over forecast by roughly 12 percent in each of the last four quarters. Each quarter it was explained as a specific project overrun and absorbed. In the fourth review someone lays the four explanations side by side: the pattern is that the organization is running a permanent contractor bench it has never budgeted for. This is the finding. A reforecast would paper over it; the fix is a decision about whether those roles should be headcount, and it goes to the capital allocation conversation with a number attached.

Common pitfall

The reserve that becomes a slush fund

A budget with no reserve blows through its total the first time reality diverges from plan. A budget whose reserve is spent quietly on whatever came up last has the same problem with a worse audit trail.

What it looks like

Reserve is set at 8 percent. By month 5 it has funded a conference, an overrun on a project that was never reforecast, and a tool a team wanted. None of the three went through a decision. When the unplanned event that matters arrives in month 9, an unsupported database that has to be migrated after a vulnerability disclosure, the reserve is gone and the CTO is asking the CFO for money that was supposed to already exist.

The counter-move

Reserve is spent only by a recorded decision, against the same ranked list the capital allocation page describes, and it is reported in the monthly review as a line with a balance. Overruns are reforecast rather than absorbed. Anything that would have been declined at planning time is declined from the reserve too. The test of a reserve is that in month 9 it is still there.

Checklist

Signs the budget is being tracked rather than managed

Patterns that show up when a budget process exists on the calendar but isn't catching anything before it becomes a problem.

  • Variance gets discovered at quarter-end review rather than flagged as it's happening
  • No one can explain why a specific line item is off forecast, only that it is
  • Chargeback exists on paper but nobody trusts the underlying cost attribution
  • The same category misses in the same direction every quarter and the forecast method has never been revisited
  • Budget review meetings recite the numbers without any decision changing afterward
Reference only. Specific variance thresholds and review mechanics should reflect your organization's budget size and volatility rather than a fixed rule.

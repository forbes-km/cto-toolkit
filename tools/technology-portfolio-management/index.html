Technology Portfolio Management

The application estate, AI initiatives, innovation bets, and the budget each have their own page. This is the narrower piece missing between them: ranking and sequencing every initiative competing for the same engineering capacity, regardless of category, plus a working WSJF calculator that ranks a list you build yourself.

This page assumes you already know your available capacity and makes no attempt to estimate it. If capacity itself is the uncertain number, that's a separate, prior problem.

1

The default is a de facto portfolio rather than a deliberate one

Most organizations never decide their portfolio. It accumulates.

What a deliberate portfolio looks like

Every active initiative sits on one list, ranked against every other active initiative, regardless of which team or category proposed it. The list is re-ranked on a fixed cadence, whether or not something new has shown up. Items get removed as well as added.

The tell that it accumulated

The roadmap has never had an item removed, only added. Nobody can say, without checking three different documents, what the fourth-highest-priority initiative in the company is right now. Prioritization happens by whoever escalates loudest rather than by a comparable score.

2

WSJF: ranking across categories on one scale

Weighted Shortest Job First, from SAFe's Lean Portfolio Management, built on Don Reinertsen's Cost of Delay economics. The reason it works here: it doesn't care what type of work an item is.

WSJF = Cost of Delay ÷ Job Size. Cost of Delay is the sum of three components, each scored on a relative Fibonacci scale (1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 20): User-Business Value (the direct economic or customer benefit), Time Criticality (does the value decay if this waits), and Risk Reduction / Opportunity Enablement (does this unlock or de-risk other work). Divide that sum by relative Job Size, and the result ranks a moderately valuable two-week job above a highly valuable six-month one, because value delivered sooner compounds and value delayed decays. An AI pilot, a compliance mandate, and a technical debt paydown all get scored on the same four inputs, which is exactly what makes this usable across categories where the other four portfolio pages can't reach.

The scores are relative rather than precise measurements. Anchor the smallest, most obvious item on your list at 1 for each component, then size everything else against it. The ranking is the useful output; the individual numbers are scaffolding.
Interactive

Rank your own portfolio

List every initiative competing for capacity, regardless of category, and set the capacity you have in job-size points. The tool ranks by WSJF, draws the cut line where capacity runs out, and totals the cost of delay deferred below it. Runs entirely in your browser; nothing is sent anywhere.

Worked example

WSJF with three competing items

Cost of delay is the sum of business value, time criticality, and risk reduction or opportunity enablement, each scored on the modified Fibonacci scale. WSJF divides it by job size.

Reading it

Why the smallest item wins

C has the highest cost of delay but ranks last, because it takes four times as long as B.

What the ranking says

Do B first; it is urgent and small. Then A. Split C into increments so the first slice can compete on its own size rather than waiting behind everything.

What it does not say

WSJF ranks within available capacity. It does not tell you whether C should be funded at all; that is the capital allocation decision, made before this ranking runs.

3

Category-blind ranking vs. reserved capacity

An unresolved tension. Pure WSJF ranking can starve categories that never win on pure economic score.

Pure ranking

Every initiative competes on the same list, highest WSJF wins the next slot regardless of category. Simple, defensible, and explicit about tradeoffs. The risk: technical debt and security work, which rarely score high on Time Criticality until something breaks, permanently lose to visible, revenue-attached features.

Reserved capacity by category

A fixed share of capacity, say the debt and reserve percentages from Technology Capital Allocation, is set aside before ranking begins, then WSJF ranks within each reserved share. Protects categories that would otherwise never win, at the cost of a less pure, harder-to-defend prioritization story.

Most functioning portfolios use both: a capacity floor for the categories that lose on pure economics, WSJF ranking inside and above that floor for everything else.
4

Sequencing and dependencies

A ranked list is a different thing from a schedule. Value-order ignores what blocks what.

A high-WSJF initiative that depends on a lower-ranked one still has to wait; running it first produces rework or a stalled effort waiting on its own dependency. Before finalizing sequence, check the top ten items against each other for blocking relationships, in addition to checking them against the full backlog. The common failure: nobody maps this until two teams have already started conflicting work, which is a coordination cost the ranking itself was supposed to prevent.

5

Killing something already in flight

The hardest and most avoided move in portfolio management, and the one that matters most.

The failure mode

An initiative that scored well six months ago keeps running on inertia. Re-scoring it against today's list would rank it below several things that didn't exist when it started, but nobody re-runs the comparison because it's already funded and staffed.

The discipline

Every re-ranking cycle scores in-flight work against new proposals on the same list, with no separate "already committed" list exempt from comparison. Money and time already spent are gone regardless of the decision now; the only question is whether finishing beats every current alternative for the capacity remaining.

6

Cadence

A portfolio ranked once a year is a portfolio managed by whoever spoke last in December.

Quarterly re-ranking of the full list is the common floor, monthly for organizations moving fast enough that a quarter is a long time. Between formal cycles, a time-critical item can force an out-of-cycle re-rank, but that path should be visibly rare; if it's common, the formal cadence is too slow for the business it's serving.

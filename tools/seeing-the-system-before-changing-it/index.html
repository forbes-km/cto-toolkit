Understanding the System Before Changing It

A ten-question diagnostic for understanding a system before intervening in it.

A system can be a team, a process, an operating model, a software platform, a technology architecture, or the network of people, processes, technology, incentives, constraints, and dependencies that interact to produce an outcome.

The principle is simple: before changing a system, understand enough of its structure and behavior to anticipate how it's likely to respond. That doesn't mean understanding every detail, an impossible bar in a system of any real size. It means understanding the boundaries, actors, incentives, feedback loops, delays, constraints, and measures that could cause the intervention to behave differently from the plan.

This matters most when the system crosses organizational or technical boundaries. A change that appears local can alter behavior elsewhere, sometimes immediately and sometimes months later.

Pairs with Systems Thinking for CTOs for the underlying concepts, Decision Architecture for where this sits relative to everything else, and ADRs & Roadmapping for where the answers should get written down.
Example: operating model

Four business units each run their own technology organization, its own budget, roadmap, and technology leader reporting directly to that business unit's president. Leadership centralizes all technology under one enterprise CTO, and the four business-unit technology leaders now report through the CTO instead of their business president. No infrastructure changes, this is a people-and-process system.

Example: system of systems

Five core enterprise systems, CRM, ERP, billing, a data warehouse, and the customer-facing app, are wired together through years of ad hoc point-to-point integrations. The company replaces all of them with a central event bus, so each system publishes and subscribes to shared events instead of calling each other directly.

Phase 1 · Understand the system : what it is, and what's inside it
1

Define the system

What you're changing, stated at the level where the real behavior lives, not the level where the budget line sits.

The question

Name the system being changed, not just the artifact being removed or added. The artifact is the visible trigger. The system is everything whose behavior depends on it.

Operating model

Not "we're changing four reporting lines." The system being changed is every business unit president's direct influence over what used to be their own technology priorities, and the speed that came from a technology leader who sat in that president's own staff meeting every week.

System of systems

Not "we're adding an event bus." The system being changed is every point-to-point contract between the five systems, the implicit timing and ordering assumptions baked into each one, and every downstream report or reconciliation process that quietly depends on those assumptions holding.

The technique for finding the system instead of stopping at the artifact is root cause analysis: ask why the artifact is the proposed fix, then why that answer is true, and repeat until the answer isn't itself caused by something further upstream. Toyota's 5 Whys is the simplest version of this. Stop after one round and you've defined the artifact, not the system.
2

Define the boundary

What you're excluding, and whether that exclusion makes the analysis easier or just makes it wrong.

The question

Every analysis draws a boundary around what counts as "in scope." The boundary is usually drawn to make the planning tractable, which means it's drawn to exclude exactly the inconvenient parts.

Operating model

The boundary drawn for planning was "reporting lines and the org chart." Excluded: the commitments each business unit president already made to their own board or customers based on having direct control over technology, and the unwritten, unit-specific prioritization logic no one ever documented centrally.

System of systems

The boundary drawn for planning was "the integration layer." Excluded: the five system owners' own roadmaps, each of which assumed continued control over their point-to-point contracts, and the data warehouse's nightly batch jobs, which rely on a delivery order the event bus doesn't guarantee.

The technique for drawing a boundary that doesn't quietly exclude the inconvenient part is MECE, mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive, a McKinsey problem-structuring principle: break what's potentially in scope into pieces that don't overlap and, together, cover the whole system, then test each piece against the boundary question directly. An issue tree is the same technique on paper, one big question broken into an exhaustive, non-overlapping set of smaller ones, instead of a boundary drawn once by instinct and never checked for what it left out.
3

Identify actors

Who behaves differently because of the intervention, not just who signs off on it.

The question

Approval lists and stakeholder lists are not the same thing as the list of people whose day-to-day behavior changes. The second list is usually longer and less obvious.

Operating model

Not just the four technology leaders now reporting to the CTO. Every business unit president who loses direct authority over what used to be their own roadmap. Every business stakeholder who now routes a request through a shared enterprise queue instead of a direct relationship with an embedded technology leader.

System of systems

Not just the team building the event bus. Every system owner who now has to instrument their system to publish events instead of just responding to direct calls. Every analyst whose report assumed CRM updates arrive before billing updates, an order the bus doesn't guarantee.

Phase 2 · Understand the behavior : what drives it, and what it's already doing
4

Identify incentives

What behavior the current system rewards, which is usually not the same thing as what the org chart says it rewards.

The question

Every system rewards something whether anyone designed it to or not. Find the actual reward structure before assuming the intervention only changes what it was aimed at.

Operating model

The current system rewards each business unit's technology leader for being maximally responsive to their own president, since that's who evaluates them and controls their budget. Centralizing removes that direct incentive and replaces it with enterprise-level prioritization no single president controls, so responsiveness to any one unit necessarily drops even as total enterprise throughput rises.

System of systems

The current system rewards each system owner for shipping their own roadmap fast, since integrations are point-to-point and entirely theirs to manage. An event bus rewards owners for publishing stable, well-documented event contracts, a different skill than shipping features quickly.

5

Identify feedback loops

What gets amplified, what gets suppressed, once the change is live.

The question

An intervention rarely just adds a new behavior. It usually strengthens one existing loop and weakens another, and the weakened loop was often doing quiet, unglamorous work.

Operating model

Amplified: standardization pressure, since a centralized CTO is rewarded for reducing duplicate platforms and enforcing common tooling across units. Suppressed: the fast, informal channel that let a business unit president get an urgent fix bumped to the front of the queue without a formal enterprise-wide justification.

System of systems

Amplified: how freely owners add new integrations, since publishing an event is cheaper than negotiating a new point-to-point contract with another team. Suppressed: the friction that used to force two teams to talk before building a new dependency between their systems.

6

Identify delays

What consequences won't appear immediately, and how long the gap plausibly runs before they do.

The question

A delay is what lets a bad intervention look successful for a while. The longer the delay, the more confidently people will point to the wrong evidence when asked if it worked.

Operating model

For the first two quarters, the four technology leaders keep making the same calls they always did out of habit and relationship, so almost nothing visibly changes. The real effect, roadmap decisions increasingly reflecting enterprise tradeoffs instead of any one unit's priorities, shows up nine to twelve months later, once the centralized budget cycle runs its first full round.

System of systems

The event bus goes live and every system publishes correctly on day one. The real cost shows up months later, when the warehouse's nightly reports start silently running on stale data during any week event delivery lags by even a few minutes, a failure mode the old synchronous calls never had.

Phase 3 · Understand the consequences : what an intervention would disturb
7

Identify indirect effects

What emerges from the intervention beyond the thing it was aimed at, whether that shows up nearby, elsewhere, or later.

The question

The intended effect gets modeled because it's the point of the change. Indirect effects get missed because nobody's job was to look for them, and they don't always arrive as a single, traceable step. Sometimes it's a feedback loop producing a result much later. Sometimes it's simultaneous, showing up somewhere nobody thought to check.

Operating model

The goal was reducing duplicate technology spend across units. One indirect effect: business unit presidents who used to treat their technology leader as part of their own leadership team start making major business decisions with no technology input in the room, because that seat physically moved to a different reporting line and stopped showing up to their staff meeting.

System of systems

The goal was reducing integration maintenance burden. One indirect effect: the customer-facing app starts showing intermittent, hard-to-reproduce data inconsistencies, because two events that used to arrive in guaranteed order under a direct call now sometimes arrive out of order through the bus, a case nobody who built the original UI logic anticipated.

8

Identify constraints

Which constraints are real, physics or contract or regulation, and which are organizational habits nobody has re-examined lately.

The question

Every plan runs into "we can't do that because X." Half of those X's are load-bearing. The other half are just how it's always been done, restated as if it were a law of physics.

Operating model

Real constraint: one business unit operates under a regulatory requirement that mandates a separately governed technology function, that unit can't be fully centralized without a real compliance problem. Not real, just never questioned: the assumption that every unit needs its own full technology leadership stack rather than shared platform capability with unit-specific product ownership.

System of systems

Real constraint: billing's downstream finance reporting requires transactional consistency an eventually-consistent bus can't provide without extra work. Not real, just never questioned: the assumption that every integration needs to be real-time, when several of the five systems only ever needed daily reconciliation.

9

Identify measures

What you're optimizing, and what behavior that specific metric will produce once people start managing to it.

The question

Whatever gets measured gets managed to, including the parts of the metric you didn't intend. State the metric, then ask what the cheapest way to hit it looks like.

Operating model

If the measure is "total technology headcount reduced," the cuts land hardest on whichever unit documented its context the least, not on genuine duplication. The metric that matters is duplicate capability eliminated without loss of unit-specific delivery speed, not headcount alone.

System of systems

If the measure is "point-to-point integrations removed," teams migrate the easy ones first and leave the fragile, high-risk ones for later, the opposite of where the real risk lives. The metric that matters is risk-weighted fragility reduced, not integration count.

Phase 4 · Intervene : then close the loop
10

Design the intervention, then observe the response

The intervention that changes the system behavior you want with the least unnecessary disruption, not the largest change that feels decisive.

The question

Everything above exists to inform this design choice. A sequenced intervention that respects the loops and delays you just found usually beats a bigger one that ignores them, though a real structural problem sometimes needs a real structural fix, not a smaller version of the same mistake. Either way, the intervention isn't the last step. Watch what the system does in response, and update the answers above if it's not what questions 4 through 9 predicted.

Operating model

Not centralizing all four units in one reorg announcement. A phased centralization starting with the shared services duplicated identically across all four units, common infrastructure, security, while each unit keeps a dedicated technology lead in their president's staff meeting for at least two more budget cycles. Afterward, check whether presidents still report technology as represented at their leadership table, not just assume the org chart change was fine because nobody complained in month one.

System of systems

Not cutting all five systems over in one release. A phased migration starting with the two systems least sensitive to ordering, with the old point-to-point path kept running in parallel until event delivery is proven reliable under real load. Afterward, the warehouse's report accuracy gets checked against the old baseline, not assumed fine because the bus is technically delivering events.

This is where the loop closes back into the rest of the toolkit: ADRs & Roadmapping records the decision, and whatever gets measured afterward feeds back into the Measurement stage of Decision Architecture, which feeds back into Strategy. This diagnostic doesn't end at the decision.
Checklist

Before you sign off on the intervention

Run this against any change crossing more than one team's boundary, a shared platform, or a standing risk control.

  • You can name at least two actors, beyond the team proposing the change, who will behave differently once it ships
  • You can name the current incentive the change removes or reverses, not just the goal it's meant to achieve
  • You've written down an indirect effect you expect, not just the intended one the proposal is optimized for
  • The success metric measures the outcome you want, not the easiest thing to measure by the deadline
  • You've stated which constraints are real and which are just how it's always been done, out loud, in the room
Reference only. This diagnostic surfaces the interactions worth examining before a decision, it doesn't replace the judgment call once you've examined them. The 5 Whys technique in card 1 originates at Toyota, credited to Sakichi Toyoda; MECE in card 2 is a McKinsey & Company problem-structuring principle.

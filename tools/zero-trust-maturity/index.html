Zero-Trust Maturity Preview

A preview of the six-dimension Security & Zero-Trust Maturity Model, scored on evidence rather than stated policy: identity and access, segmentation and blast radius, detection and response, vulnerability management, third-party risk, and governance and culture.

This page covers the framework and scoring discipline. The full facilitated workbook, scored against a defined zero-trust target architecture, is available through direct engagement.
Framework

Six dimensions, scored independently

Most organizations are unevenly mature across these six; that unevenness is itself the most useful output of the assessment, not a flaw in it.

DimensionLevel 1: Ad hocLevel 3: DefinedLevel 5: Optimized
Identity & accessStanding broad access, no consistent least-privilege policy across systemsDocumented access policy applied through a defined onboarding/offboarding processJust-in-time, continuously verified access with no standing privilege by default
Segmentation & blast radiusFlat network, one compromised credential reaches most of the environmentDefined network segments with controlled points between themMicro-segmented to the workload level; a single compromise is contained by design, not by luck
Detection & responseReliant on external reports to learn about a problemCentralized logging and defined alerting on known threat patternsBehavioral anomaly detection catching novel patterns, with a tested response plan behind it
Vulnerability managementScanning inconsistent, patching reactive and undocumentedRegular scanning cadence, patching SLA defined and mostly metContinuous scanning integrated into deployment, patching SLA consistently met with evidence
Third-party riskVendor access granted with no ongoing reviewVendor risk assessed at onboarding, reviewed periodicallyContinuous vendor risk monitoring, access scoped and reviewed on the same cadence as internal identity

Sequencing

Where to start, since you can't do all six at once

Treating six dimensions as equally urgent is how effort gets spread thin everywhere and deep nowhere.

The highest-leverage starting point

Identity and access almost always comes first. Segmentation, detection, and even third-party risk all assume you can trust who's making a request in the first place; building micro-segmentation on top of standing broad access just adds complexity around a foundation that's still weak.

The trap

Spreading effort evenly across all six dimensions produces slow, shallow progress everywhere and leaves the foundational dimension exactly as weak as it started, which is worse than concentrating effort where it compounds.

Worked example

Reading a real score pattern, not just the average

What the six-dimension breakdown reveals when it's uneven, which it almost always is.

Scores: Identity & access 2, Segmentation & blast radius 2, Detection & response 4, Vulnerability management 3, Third-party risk 2, Governance & culture 3. Average: 2.7.

The diagnosis the average hides: strong detection sitting on top of weak identity and segmentation is a specific, dangerous pattern. This organization can see an attacker after they're already in, but the foundational controls that would have made getting in hard, or limited what they could reach once inside, are still weak. The instinct this score pattern usually triggers, buy more detection tooling since it's already the strongest area, is exactly backwards. More visibility into a wide-open environment doesn't reduce how wide open it is. Identity and segmentation need the next dollar, not detection.

How to use this

Scoring honestly, and what to do with the result

The assessment only has value if the scoring is honest, which is harder than it sounds when the person scoring it is also accountable for the result.

How to score it

Score against evidence, not intention. "We have a policy" is not Level 3 if the policy isn't followed; it's evidence of intent, not practice. Ask for the artifact (the actual access review log, the actual segmentation diagram, the actual vendor review record) before accepting a self-reported level.

What to do with a low score

A Level 1 or 2 in any dimension is not itself an emergency; it's a prioritization input. Rank dimensions by business impact if that dimension fails, not by how uncomfortable the low score is, and fund the highest-impact gap first rather than the easiest one to fix.

Common pitfall

Compliance certification as a false signal of maturity

A frequent gap between what a certification confirms and what an organization assumes it confirms.

What a certification confirms

SOC 2 or ISO 27001 confirms that defined controls existed and were followed during the specific audit period the certification covers, evaluated against a scope the organization itself proposed.

What it doesn't confirm

That the scope covered every system that matters, that the maturity level is sustained rather than a point-in-time snapshot assembled for the audit, or that a novel attack pattern outside the certified controls would be caught. Treat certification as a floor, not a ceiling.

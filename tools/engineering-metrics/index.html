Engineering Metrics

The four DORA metrics and their benchmark bands, why story points and lines of code persist despite being poor proxies, why distribution matters more than the average, and the trust problem that determines whether a metrics program survives contact with the org.

DORA benchmark bands summarized from the widely cited State of DevOps research; treat the specific thresholds as reference points, not hard pass/fail lines for your organization's context.
Framework

The four DORA metrics

The closest thing the industry has to a validated, research-backed set of engineering performance metrics. Not a complete picture on their own, but the right starting point before inventing something else.

Deployment frequencyHow often code reaches productionOn demand, multiple times a dayFewer than once per month

1

What not to measure, and why it persists anyway

Story points, lines of code, and commit count remain common despite being well understood as poor proxies. Worth naming why they persist.

Why they're wrong

Story points measure estimation, not output, and are calibrated differently per team, making cross-team comparison meaningless. Lines of code rewards verbosity over good design. Commit count rewards small, frequent commits over meaningful ones, and is trivially gamed the moment someone knows it's being watched.

Why they persist

They're easy to pull from existing tools with no new instrumentation, and they produce a number, which satisfies a reporting requirement even when the number means very little. Replacing them requires investing in DORA-style instrumentation that most orgs haven't built yet.

2

Distribution, not average

The single most common mistake in reading engineering metrics: reporting a mean when the distribution is what matters.

Why the average lies

A team with a median lead time of two hours and a long tail of three-week outliers has a very different problem than a team with a consistent two-day lead time across the board, but both can produce the same average. The fix rarely looks the same for both.

What to look at instead

Report the median and the 90th percentile together. The median tells you what's typical; the gap between median and p90 tells you how bad the worst case gets, and that gap is usually where the real operational risk lives.

3

Using metrics without weaponizing them

The trust problem that kills a metrics program faster than any technical flaw in the metrics themselves.

What works

Metrics owned and reviewed at the team level first, with the team using them to improve their own process, before any number rolls up to a leadership dashboard. People who feel a metric is being used to improve their work engage honestly with it.

What backfires

Individual-level metrics tied to performance review, or a leadership dashboard that ranks teams against each other. Both produce the same response: people optimize for the number instead of the outcome the number was supposed to represent, and the metric stops meaning anything.

4

Beyond DORA: what the four metrics don't capture

DORA measures delivery. It says nothing about the team's health or whether the work being delivered is sustainable.

SignalWhat it catches that DORA misses
Code review turnaround timeA team can deploy frequently while pull requests sit unreviewed for days; this is often the real bottleneck DORA's lead time number is quietly absorbing
Pages per on-call rotationElite change failure rate and time to restore can coexist with an on-call rotation that's burning people out through sheer volume of low-severity noise
Technical debt ratio (tracked debt items versus new feature work merged)Delivery metrics can look healthy for months while debt accumulates invisibly until it surfaces as a sudden change failure rate spike
Time-to-first-commit for new hiresA slow, painful onboarding process doesn't show up in any DORA number until the new hire's productivity ramp is already six weeks behind where it should be

The dashboard is not the point, the conversation is

A metrics program that produces a dashboard nobody discusses has produced nothing.

Where metrics earn their keep

In a team retro, where the team itself asks why lead time crept up this sprint and proposes a change to test next sprint, or in a 1:1 where a manager notices a specific engineer's review turnaround has quietly doubled and asks what's going on before it becomes a pattern. The number is a prompt for a conversation, not the output.

Where they don't

A dashboard displayed in a wiki that gets glanced at during a monthly all-hands and never referenced between updates. Building the dashboard is the easy 20 percent; making it something a team uses to change a decision is the hard 80 percent, and most metrics programs stop at the easy part.

A worked example

What reading distribution instead of average looks like against real numbers.

The team: eight engineers, reports a median lead time of 4 hours (comfortably in the High band) and a change failure rate of 12 percent (Elite band). On paper, a strong team.

The distribution check: p90 lead time is 11 days. Roughly one deployment in ten is taking over a week and a half, dragging against a median that looks fine. Digging in, the p90 outliers cluster around one specific service with a manual, undocumented deployment step that only two engineers know how to run.

What the average would have hidden: a leadership dashboard reporting only the median would show this team as healthy indefinitely, right up until both engineers who know the manual step are unavailable during an incident on that specific service, at which point time to restore, the metric that matters most in the moment, blows well past any benchmark band.

The fix that came out of the conversation, not the dashboard: the team automated the manual step as a two-week investment, dropping p90 lead time to under two days within the next reporting cycle. The median barely moved, because the median was never the problem.

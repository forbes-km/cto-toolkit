Innovation Portfolio Management

Structuring innovation as a managed portfolio across the same three horizons as the broader technology strategy: funding models that survive quarterly pressure, stage-gate versus continuous discovery, kill criteria set before a bet starts, and the two failure modes specific to innovation work.

A framework for structuring and governing an innovation portfolio, not a guarantee that any specific bet succeeds. The discipline is in the process, kill criteria, funding protection, honest metrics, not in picking winners in advance.
1

Portfolio structure follows the same three horizons

Innovation isn't a separate budget line floating outside the strategy, it's Horizon 2 and 3 of the same investment split covered on the Technology Strategy page, made explicit as a managed portfolio rather than a handful of side projects.

Horizon 2 bets

Adjacent opportunities with a clearer, though not yet proven, path to value. Roughly 20 to 30 percent of technology investment. These get real funding and a named owner, not a hackathon slot.

Horizon 3 bets

Genuine unknowns: unproven, high-uncertainty, high-potential-payoff exploration. Roughly 5 to 10 percent. Most of these fail, and that's the design, not a problem to fix by picking better bets.

The most common portfolio failure is disguising Horizon 1 optimization work as innovation because it's safer to fund and easier to report on, which quietly starves the two horizons that produce new capability.
2

Funding model: separate from core, not carved out of it

A dedicated innovation fund, protected from quarterly delivery pressure, is what keeps Horizon 2 and 3 bets alive past the first bad quarter.

Why separation matters

A bet funded out of a product team's normal budget gets cannibalized the moment that team hits a delivery crunch, which is often, since crunches are common and bets are inherently deferrable. A separate fund with its own governance is what survives that pressure.

Sizing

Sized against the same 20 to 30 percent Horizon 2 and 5 to 10 percent Horizon 3 split, not against whatever's left over after core delivery is funded. If the fund is sized residually, it will trend toward zero the first time the core business needs the money.

3

Stage-gate versus continuous discovery

The mechanism by which a bet gets more funding, or doesn't, and the single biggest determinant of whether a portfolio learns anything from its failures.

Stage-gate

A big upfront business case, built for something inherently uncertain, followed by a large funding decision at a small number of gates. Produces confident-sounding plans for things nobody can forecast, and makes killing a bet feel like admitting the original business case was wrong.

Continuous discovery

Small funded experiments with a defined evidence bar for the next increment of funding. Each increment is small enough that killing it isn't an admission of failure, it's the model working as designed. This is the harder model to run administratively and the one that produces learning.

4

Kill criteria, set before the bet starts

The single most skipped discipline in innovation portfolios, and the reason most of them accumulate zombie projects that never officially die.

What works

Success and failure criteria named in writing before a bet is funded: what result, by when, would justify the next round of funding, and what result would justify killing it. Set before anyone's emotionally invested, when the criteria can still be objective.

What happens without it

Kill decisions get made, or more often don't get made, based on who's willing to have the uncomfortable conversation rather than the evidence. A bet with no named end condition doesn't die, it just quietly stops being discussed while still consuming a line item.

A portfolio without a visible kill rate isn't a healthy portfolio, it's a portfolio that hasn't yet had the hard conversation. Zero kills in a year is a bigger red flag than a high kill rate.
5

Portfolio balance, measured, not assumed

What gets tracked to know whether the portfolio is real or theater.

Metrics worth tracking
  • Split of active investment across the three horizons, against the 60-70/20-30/5-10 target
  • Bet velocity: how long from initial funding to a real kill-or-scale decision
  • Kill rate: bets stopped versus bets scaled, over a rolling period
  • Diversity of bet type: adjacent-market bets versus new-capability bets versus new-business-model bets, so the portfolio isn't quietly all one type
What it's for

Not a vanity dashboard. If Horizon 3 investment has quietly drifted to zero, or the kill rate is zero, or every active bet is the same type of bet, that's the signal to act on, not a number to report and move past.

6

Common failure modes

Patterns specific to innovation portfolios, distinct from general project failure.

Innovation theater

A lab, an incubator, or a hackathon with real energy and no connection to actual funding or authority to ship. Produces enthusiasm and demos, not capability. The tell: ask what happened to last year's winning hackathon project. If nobody knows, it's theater.

Portfolio capture by Horizon 1

Improvements to the existing product get relabeled as innovation because that's where the safe, fundable work already is. The portfolio looks balanced on a slide and is 90 percent Horizon 1 wearing a Horizon 2 label.

Both failure modes are easier to prevent than to fix after the fact. A portfolio review that asks 'what did we kill this quarter, and what did that free up' catches both early.

Innovation Portfolio Management

Structuring innovation as a managed portfolio across the same three horizons as the broader technology strategy: funding models that survive quarterly pressure, stage-gate versus continuous discovery, kill criteria set before a bet starts, the two failure modes specific to innovation work, and a calculator that checks your own portfolio against the specific red flags this page names.

Innovation and transformation get used as synonyms for change, but they're different problems. Innovation is a selection problem: generate enough options, then kill the weak ones fast and cheap before they're expensive. This page is entirely about that selection problem. Getting an organization to adopt what a winning bet produces once it's ready to scale past the team that built it is transformation, and it runs on adoption mechanics this page doesn't cover.

A framework for structuring and governing an innovation portfolio. Nothing here guarantees that any specific bet succeeds. The discipline is in the process (kill criteria, funding protection, metrics that can't be gamed) rather than in picking winners in advance. See Transformation and Innovation for the full selection-versus-adoption distinction and the frameworks for the adoption side.
1

Portfolio structure follows the same three horizons

Innovation is Horizon 2 and 3 of the same investment split covered on the Technology Strategy page, made explicit as a managed portfolio rather than a handful of side projects. It is never a separate budget line floating outside the strategy.

Horizon 2 bets

Adjacent opportunities with a clearer, though not yet proven, path to value. Roughly 20 to 30 percent of technology investment. These get funding and a named owner. A hackathon slot doesn't count.

Horizon 3 bets

Unknowns: unproven, high-uncertainty, high-potential-payoff exploration. Roughly 5 to 10 percent. Most of these fail, and that's the design. Picking better bets doesn't change it.

The most common portfolio failure is disguising Horizon 1 optimization work as innovation because it's safer to fund and easier to report on, which quietly starves the two horizons that produce new capability.
2

Funding model: separate from core rather than carved out of it

A dedicated innovation fund, protected from quarterly delivery pressure, is what keeps Horizon 2 and 3 bets alive past the first bad quarter.

Why separation matters

A bet funded out of a product team's normal budget gets cannibalized the moment that team hits a delivery crunch, which is often, since crunches are common and bets are inherently deferrable. A separate fund with its own governance is what survives that pressure.

Sizing

Sized against the same 20 to 30 percent Horizon 2 and 5 to 10 percent Horizon 3 split, rather than against whatever's left over after core delivery is funded. If the fund is sized residually, it will trend toward zero the first time the core business needs the money.

3

Stage-gate versus continuous discovery

The mechanism by which a bet gets more funding, or doesn't, and the single biggest determinant of whether a portfolio learns anything from its failures.

Stage-gate

A big upfront business case, built for something inherently uncertain, followed by a large funding decision at a small number of gates. Produces confident-sounding plans for things nobody can forecast, and makes killing a bet feel like admitting the original business case was wrong.

Continuous discovery

Small funded experiments with a defined evidence bar for the next increment of funding. Each increment is small enough that killing it reads as the model working as designed rather than as an admission of failure. This is the harder model to run administratively and the one that produces learning.

4

Kill criteria, set before the bet starts

The single most skipped discipline in innovation portfolios, and the reason most of them accumulate zombie projects that never officially die.

What works

Success and failure criteria named in writing before a bet is funded: what result, by when, would justify the next round of funding, and what result would justify killing it. Set before anyone's emotionally invested, when the criteria can still be objective.

What happens without it

Kill decisions get made, or more often don't get made, based on who's willing to have the uncomfortable conversation rather than the evidence. A bet with no named end condition never dies; it quietly stops being discussed while still consuming a line item.

A portfolio without a visible kill rate is a portfolio that hasn't yet had the hard conversation. Zero kills in a year is a bigger red flag than a high kill rate.
5

Portfolio balance, measured

What gets tracked to know whether the portfolio is working or theater.

Metrics worth tracking
  • Split of active investment across the three horizons, against the 60-70/20-30/5-10 target
  • Bet velocity: how long from initial funding to a kill-or-scale decision
  • Kill rate: bets stopped versus bets scaled, over a rolling period
  • Diversity of bet type: adjacent-market bets versus new-capability bets versus new-business-model bets, so the portfolio isn't quietly all one type
What it's for

These are signals to act on rather than a vanity dashboard. If Horizon 3 investment has quietly drifted to zero, or the kill rate is zero, or every active bet is the same type of bet, something has to change.

6

Common failure modes

Patterns specific to innovation portfolios, distinct from general project failure.

Innovation theater

A lab, an incubator, or a hackathon with plenty of energy and no connection to funding or authority to ship. Produces enthusiasm and demos, and no capability. The tell: ask what happened to last year's winning hackathon project. If nobody knows, it's theater.

Portfolio capture by Horizon 1

Improvements to the existing product get relabeled as innovation because that's where the safe, fundable work already is. The portfolio looks balanced on a slide and is 90 percent Horizon 1 wearing a Horizon 2 label.

Both failure modes are easier to prevent than to fix after the fact. A portfolio review that asks 'what did we kill this quarter, and what did that free up' catches both early.
7

Innovation tournaments: sourcing the portfolio

Christian Terwiesch and Karl Ulrich, Innovation Tournaments (Harvard Business Press, 2009). Stage-gate versus continuous discovery, above, governs how a bet gets more funding once it exists. A tournament is the separate mechanism for deciding which bets enter the portfolio in the first place. See Transformation and Innovation for how this fits alongside the adoption side of the same problem.

How it works

A periodic open call, quarterly is typical, where anyone in engineering can pitch a bet against a short, consistent rubric: horizon fit, a proposed kill criterion, and a funding ask. Pitches are evaluated by a small panel that includes people outside the pitcher's own reporting line, and winners get seeded into the portfolio with funding rather than applause.

Where it goes wrong

Run as a one-time event, a hackathon, rather than a standing cadence, or winners get praise with no funding attached. Both produce the innovation-theater failure mode named above: energy with no connection to authority or budget.

A standing forum with an empty docket is a natural home for this. See Architecture Review Board for turning an unscheduled review slot into an informal salon where early-stage ideas get tested before they're ready for a formal tournament pitch.

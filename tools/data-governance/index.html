Data Governance & Privacy

The regulatory and stewardship side of data: who owns it, what protects it, and what happens when a regulator or a customer asks what's held about them. Distinct from the architecture question of how data is stored and modeled.

This covers governance and regulatory obligation. See Data Strategy for warehouse versus lakehouse versus mesh and the architectural side of the data question.

1

Data classification as the foundation

Every governance obligation downstream depends on first knowing what data exists and how sensitive it is.

How to run it

A classification scheme with a small number of clear tiers, public, internal, confidential, restricted or regulated, applied when data is created or collected, not retrofitted after an audit. Every system holding data above the lowest tier has a named owner accountable for it.

How it breaks

A classification policy that was written but never applied to the systems in production, so nobody can answer a basic question like "where does our customer PII live" without a multi-week investigation.

2

Subject access and deletion requests, operationally

The rights GDPR and similar frameworks grant individuals are only real if the organization can fulfill them within the required window.

What good looks like

A documented, at least partially automated process for locating and exporting or deleting an individual's data across every system that holds it, tested against a live request before anyone relies on it. A tracked SLA against the regulatory deadline, with an owner accountable for meeting it.

What usually happens

Fulfillment handled manually by whichever engineer is available, searching system by system with no authoritative list of where personal data lives, which routinely blows past the regulatory response window on anything beyond the simplest request.

3

A worked example: a request end to end

What the practice above looks like against an incoming request, including where it commonly breaks.

A request arrives through a web form and gets logged against the SLA clock immediately. The response process checks it against the data inventory: CRM, product database, support ticketing, and email marketing tool are all on the list, get queried, and the results get compiled into a portable export within the regulatory window.

Where it commonly breaks: the analytics warehouse isn't on the inventory, because nobody classified it as holding personal data even though it has user IDs joinable back to an identity through the product database. The request gets marked complete while a meaningful chunk of what the organization holds on that person was never touched. That is why the inventory in section 1 has to come from mapping data flows. Asking each team what they think they hold misses this.

4

Ownership versus stewardship

Two different roles that get conflated, and the conflation is where accountability gaps live.

The distinction

A data owner is accountable for the business decisions around a dataset, retention, access policy, whether it should exist at all. A data steward implements those decisions day to day, quality checks, access provisioning, pipeline maintenance. The same person can hold both roles, but the accountability should be explicit either way.

Common failure mode

Data that's used operationally by several teams but formally owned by none of them, so nobody ever decides retention or access. They just default to whatever the system did.

5

Privacy by design, not privacy as a review gate

Where privacy consideration enters the development process changes how much friction it creates and how much risk it catches.

The approach

A lightweight privacy impact assessment built into the design phase for any feature touching personal data, before implementation starts, so decisions about what to collect and how long to keep it are made on purpose instead of defaulting to "collect everything, figure out retention later."

The usual mistake

Privacy review as a gate right before launch, which arrives too late to change the data model, so findings either get overridden under deadline pressure or become expensive retrofits after the feature is already live and being used.

6

Breach notification, the mechanics

The 72-hour clock most people have heard of, and the specific way organizations usually miss it despite meaning to comply.

What has to happen

The 72-hour window starts when the organization becomes aware of a breach, well before it's fully understood. A preliminary notification with known facts, followed by updates as the investigation progresses, is compliant. Waiting for a complete picture before notifying anyone is a violation.

Common failure mode

Treating the deadline as something to hit only once the full scope is understood, which routinely blows past 72 hours since an investigation almost always takes longer than that, when a partial, updated notification would have been compliant from day one.

7

Cross-border data transfer, briefly

A specific, common gap that surfaces during customer security reviews more often than during an internal audit.

What's required

Standard Contractual Clauses, an adequacy decision (for US recipients, certification under the EU-U.S. Data Privacy Framework), or another valid mechanism for transferring EU personal data outside the EEA, documented per vendor. A vendor's general terms of service do not cover it by default.

Common failure mode

Discovering during a customer security questionnaire that data has been flowing to a vendor or a region with no transfer mechanism in place, sometimes for years, because nobody mapped where data goes once it leaves the primary system.

Industry overlay

Healthcare and financial services

What changes when the data is protected health information or consumer financial data. Use it alongside the general model above, not instead of it.

Healthcare
  • Apply the HIPAA minimum necessary standard in the access model itself. A policy that says it while every analyst can query the whole clinical warehouse does not meet it.
  • De-identification has two routes under HIPAA: remove the eighteen listed identifier types (Safe Harbor) or get a documented expert determination.[1] A limited data set is not de-identified and still needs a data use agreement.
  • Substance use disorder records under 42 CFR Part 2 now follow HIPAA-style breach notification and allow a single patient consent for treatment, payment, and operations. Compliance was due February 16, 2026.[2]
  • No vendor touches PHI without a signed business associate agreement. That includes analytics, observability, and AI vendors that only see prompts.
  • Healthcare was the costliest industry for a breach for the fourteenth year running, at an average of $7.42M in the 2025 IBM study.[3] Use it when sizing the classification work.
Financial services
  • GLBA governs nonpublic personal information. Non-bank financial institutions under the FTC Safeguards Rule must notify the FTC within 30 days of a breach affecting 500 or more consumers.[4]
  • NYDFS Part 500 expects an asset inventory and secure disposal of nonpublic information that is no longer needed, backed by an annual compliance filing.[5]
  • Amended SEC Regulation S-P requires an incident response program, customer notice within 30 days when sensitive customer information is compromised, and service provider notice within 72 hours. Larger entities had to comply by December 3, 2025 and smaller ones by June 3, 2026.[6]
  • Retention schedules carry legal weight here. Deleting under a privacy request can conflict with a books-and-records duty, so the deletion workflow needs a legal hold and retention check before it runs.
  • EU financial entities under DORA need the data inside their ICT risk framework, including the third-party register; see the financial services column on Incident Response for the reporting clock.

Checklist

Governance red flags

Patterns that show up when data governance exists as policy and stops there.

  • No single, current inventory of where personal or regulated data lives across the estate
  • Subject access or deletion requests handled manually with no tracked SLA against the regulatory deadline
  • Data retained indefinitely by default because no one made an active decision to delete it
  • Third-party vendors processing personal data with no data processing agreement or business associate agreement on file
  • Privacy consideration enters the process only as a pre-launch review, never during design
  • No documented transfer mechanism exists for personal data moving to vendors or regions outside its origin

Cloud Cost Management

Managing general infrastructure spend as a standing discipline, not a quarterly panic when finance asks why the cloud bill jumped. Distinct from AI-specific cost modeling.

This covers general cloud infrastructure cost, compute, storage, networking, data services. For token-based pricing, PTU and reserved capacity, and GPU amortization on AI workloads, see the AI FinOps Map.

Where cost hides

The categories that consistently account for the largest share of avoidable spend, in roughly the order worth checking first.

CategoryWhat to look for
Idle and oversized computeInstances provisioned for a peak load that rarely occurs, running at 10-15 percent utilization around the clock
Non-production environmentsDev, staging, and QA environments running 24/7 when they're only used during business hours
Orphaned resourcesVolumes, snapshots, and load balancers left behind after the workload that used them was decommissioned
Untagged spendCost with no owning team attached, which nobody feels accountable for reducing because nobody's dashboard shows it
Storage tieringData sitting in a hot storage tier years after it stopped being accessed regularly
Data egressCross-region or cross-cloud transfer that could be avoided with different architecture, often invisible until the bill arrives
1

Rightsizing and idle elimination

The fastest money on the table, and the easiest to let slide once the first sweep is done.

Practice

Automated shutdown for non-production environments outside business hours, utilization-based rightsizing recommendations reviewed monthly, and a default assumption that any resource untouched for 90 days gets flagged for a decommission decision rather than left alone indefinitely.

Common failure mode

Treating this as a one-time cleanup sprint instead of an ongoing practice. Six months after the sweep, utilization has drifted again and nobody's watching, because the tooling that found the savings the first time was never left running.

2

Reserved capacity and commitment strategy

Committing to reserved instances or savings plans locks in a discount and locks in a bet about future usage. Both halves matter.

Practice

Commit only on the stable baseline, the usage level that's been consistent for at least six months, and use on-demand or short-term commitments for anything still growing or shrinking. Ladder commitment terms so not everything renews at once, which avoids a cliff where the whole discount structure needs renegotiating simultaneously.

Common failure mode

Overcommitting based on a growth projection that doesn't materialize, then paying for reserved capacity that sits unused, which is a worse outcome than paying on-demand rates for the same idle resource would have been.

3

Tagging, showback, and chargeback

Cost visibility changes behavior. Cost invisibility guarantees nobody optimizes anything they can't see themselves accountable for.

Practice

A mandatory tagging policy enforced at resource creation, not applied retroactively by a cleanup script, with showback reporting that puts each team's actual spend in front of that team monthly. Chargeback, where teams' budgets are directly debited, is a stronger lever but works only once tagging discipline is already solid.

Common failure mode

Rolling out chargeback before tagging is reliable, which produces disputed, inaccurate bills that teams learn to ignore rather than a credible signal they act on. Trust in the numbers has to come before the numbers carry financial weight.

4

Governance without becoming a bottleneck

The tension every cost program eventually hits: enough control to prevent waste, not so much that provisioning something new takes a week of approvals.

Practice

Guardrails over gatekeeping: budget alerts that trigger automatically, instance-size policies enforced by infrastructure-as-code rather than a manual review, and approval requirements reserved for genuinely large or unusual spend rather than every new resource. Most cost control should be invisible to an engineer working within normal bounds.

Common failure mode

A approval queue that becomes the default path for routine provisioning, which teaches engineers to over-request once to avoid asking twice, producing the exact oversizing the governance process was meant to prevent.

Signs cost governance isn't working

Not exhaustive, but the patterns that show up consistently when a cost program looks good on paper and isn't changing behavior.

  • The last optimization effort was a one-time project, not a recurring practice with an owner
  • Untagged or misattributed spend is a growing percentage of the bill rather than shrinking
  • Reserved capacity utilization is below 80 percent, meaning committed spend is going unused
  • Non-production environments run around the clock with no automated schedule
  • No one outside the finance or platform team can say what their own team's monthly cloud spend is
Reference only. Cloud pricing models, discount structures, and vendor tooling change frequently; verify current mechanics against your provider's documentation before committing budget.

Cloud FinOps

Managing general infrastructure spend as a standing discipline rather than a quarterly panic when finance asks why the cloud bill jumped. Distinct from AI-specific cost modeling.

This covers general cloud infrastructure cost, compute, storage, networking, data services, using the FinOps Foundation's own framework. For token-based pricing, PTU and reserved capacity, and GPU amortization on AI workloads, see the AI FinOps Map. AI is a separate recognized Scope under the same Foundation framework. The Foundation itself publishes the full framework, certifications, and the FOCUS specification at finops.org.
Framework

Phases: one building block of a larger framework

These are the FinOps Foundation's own Phases. The Foundation is the industry body (under the Linux Foundation) that maintains the full FinOps Framework and the FOCUS billing specification referenced throughout this page. The complete framework also includes Principles, Personas, Domains and Capabilities, Technology Categories, and Scopes, and the Foundation's current definition centers on maximizing the business value of technology spend broadly rather than cloud alone. The 2026 framework names five Technology Categories, public cloud, SaaS, data center, data cloud platforms, and AI, and treats a Scope as a business-defined slice of spend across those categories, started because a business question needs answering. This page covers public cloud infrastructure, which is why AI cost modeling gets its own page rather than folding into this one.

Where cost hides

The categories that consistently account for the largest share of avoidable spend, in roughly the order worth checking first. This is the Optimize phase in practice.

1

Rightsizing and idle elimination

The fastest money on the table, and the easiest to let slide once the first sweep is done.

Practice

Automated shutdown for non-production environments outside business hours, utilization-based rightsizing recommendations reviewed monthly, and a default assumption that any resource untouched for 90 days gets flagged for a decommission decision rather than left alone indefinitely.

Common failure mode

Treating this as a one-time cleanup sprint instead of an ongoing practice. Six months after the sweep, utilization has drifted again and nobody's watching, because the tooling that found the savings the first time was never left running.

2

Reserved capacity and commitment strategy

Committing to reserved instances or savings plans locks in a discount and locks in a bet about future usage. Both halves matter.

Practice

Commit only on the stable baseline, the usage level that's been consistent for at least six months, and use on-demand or short-term commitments for anything still growing or shrinking. Ladder commitment terms so not everything renews at once, which avoids a cliff where the whole discount structure needs renegotiating simultaneously.

Common failure mode

Overcommitting based on a growth projection that doesn't materialize, then paying for reserved capacity that sits unused, which is a worse outcome than paying on-demand rates for the same idle resource would have been.

3

Tagging, showback, and chargeback

Cost visibility changes behavior. Cost invisibility guarantees nobody optimizes anything they can't see themselves accountable for.

Practice

A mandatory tagging policy enforced at resource creation rather than applied retroactively by a cleanup script, with showback reporting that puts each team's spend in front of that team monthly. Chargeback, where teams' budgets are directly debited, is a stronger lever but works only once tagging discipline is already solid.

Common failure mode

Rolling out chargeback before tagging is reliable, which produces disputed, inaccurate bills that teams learn to ignore rather than a credible signal they act on. Trust in the numbers has to come before the numbers carry financial weight.

4

Governance without becoming a bottleneck

The tension every cost program eventually hits: enough control to prevent waste, without so much that provisioning something new takes a week of approvals.

Practice

Guardrails over gatekeeping: budget alerts that trigger automatically, instance-size policies enforced by infrastructure-as-code rather than a manual review, and approval requirements reserved for large or unusual spend rather than every new resource. Most cost control should be invisible to an engineer working within normal bounds.

Common failure mode

An approval queue that becomes the default path for routine provisioning, which teaches engineers to over-request once to avoid asking twice, producing the exact oversizing the governance process was meant to prevent.

5

FOCUS: a shared billing language across clouds

The FinOps Foundation's second major contribution beyond the phase model, and the reason cross-cloud cost comparison stopped being a manual exercise.

What it solves

Before FOCUS (the FinOps Open Cost and Usage Specification), comparing a dollar of AWS spend to a dollar of Azure spend meant manually normalizing wildly different billing export formats, column names, and units. FOCUS is an open, vendor-neutral spec, now adopted by AWS, Microsoft, Google, and others, that puts every provider's billing data into the same shape.

What it doesn't solve

FOCUS standardizes the data format and leaves the underlying pricing and discount structures untouched. A normalized bill still requires the same rightsizing and commitment discipline covered above. FOCUS removes the translation tax from the reporting layer; the practice itself still has to happen.

6

Crawl, Walk, Run, applied practically

The FinOps Foundation's maturity model, stated concretely enough to assess against rather than claim.

What distinguishes each stage

Crawl: tagging exists but is inconsistent, cost visibility is monthly and reactive, no forecasting. Walk: tagging enforced at creation, showback running, budgets set proactively, rightsizing is a recurring practice with an owner. Run: chargeback in place, unit economics tracked (cost per customer or transaction), anomaly detection automated, cost is a first-class input to architecture decisions rather than an afterthought.

The trap

Treating maturity as a label to claim rather than a description of what's happening. An organization that calls itself "Run" but can't answer what a single team spent last month is Crawl with better branding.

Allocation methods for multi-account, multi-BU estates

Section 3 covers tagging discipline as the prerequisite. Once tagging is reliable, the question becomes which allocation method to run on top of it, and shared infrastructure that no single team's tags can attribute cleanly is where most multi-BU chargeback programs stall.

MethodHow it splits shared costWhere it fits
Direct allocationCost attributed to the team or BU whose tag is on the resource, no splitting requiredDedicated compute, storage, and services with clean tagging; the majority of spend in a mature program
Even splitShared cost (a common network egress bill, a shared database cluster) divided equally across consuming teams regardless of actual usageA fast starting point when metering shared usage isn't built yet; crude, and worth replacing with proportional splitting once the metering exists
Proportional (metered)Shared cost split by actual measured consumption, requests served, data volume, compute-seconds against the shared resourceThe target state for anything shared and material; requires usage metering per consumer, which is the real cost of running this method rather than even split
Fixed or negotiatedA pre-agreed percentage split, set by governance rather than derived from either headcount or metered usageJoint infrastructure where no consumption metric fairly represents the benefit each BU gets, decided once by the same forum that owns the funding model on Technology Capital Allocation

The FinOps Foundation's own Allocation capability, under the Inform phase, covers this same problem in more depth; treat the table above as the working version rather than a substitute for reading it at finops.org when the shared-cost picture gets complicated.

Checklist

Signs cost governance isn't working

Not exhaustive, but the patterns that show up consistently when a cost program looks good on paper and isn't changing behavior.

  • The last optimization effort was a one-time project with no recurring owner
  • Untagged or misattributed spend is a growing percentage of the bill rather than shrinking
  • Reserved capacity utilization is below 80 percent, meaning committed spend is going unused
  • Non-production environments run around the clock with no automated schedule
  • No one outside the finance or platform team can say what their own team's monthly cloud spend is
  • The organization describes itself as FinOps "mature" but can't answer what a single team spent last month
  • Cost is reported only as total spend, with no unit cost agreed with finance
  • The program still uses pre-2025 FinOps vocabulary and cannot map spend to business-defined scopes

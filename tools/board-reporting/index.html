Board Reporting

How technology, security, and AI reach a board: the calendar across a year, which committee hears what, the one page that goes to every meeting, the metrics a director can actually use, the thresholds that trigger a call between meetings, and the questions you will be asked.

This page covers the operating practice and includes a downloadable sample deck built to it. Disclosure rules referenced here were checked in October 2026. The full framework, with cadence and escalation thresholds tailored to a particular board, is available through direct engagement.

What the board is for, from where you sit

Most weak board reporting comes from a wrong picture of the audience.

A board does not run technology and does not want to. It has three jobs that touch your area: oversee risk, approve and test the use of capital, and make sure management is competent to execute the strategy. Every minute of your time in the room should serve one of those three. Status updates serve none of them, which is why they get skimmed.

Directors also see a lot of companies. Many sit on two or three boards and have heard a dozen CTOs present. They are good at spotting a presenter who is managing them instead of informing them, and that judgment, once formed, is hard to reverse. Consistency across quarters builds more credibility than any single strong presentation.

One test covers most of what follows. Before each meeting, write one sentence per section of your material: what you want the board to know, feel, or decide because of it. A section with no answer gets cut.

Cadence

A year of board meetings, planned in advance

Four regular meetings is typical. Give each one a theme so the deep dives rotate instead of competing for the same twenty minutes.

MeetingDeep-dive themeWhat you bringDecision you may ask for
Q1Plan and capitalPrior year actuals against plan, this year's technology investment ranked by return and risk, the run, grow, and transform splitApproval of a major investment or a change to the split
Q2Security and resiliencePosture trend, the last tabletop or DR test with results, third-party exposure, insurance coverage against the scenarios that matterAcceptance of a named residual risk, or funding to close it
Q3Strategy and architectureWhere the technology strategy stands against the business strategy, what changed, AI portfolio and governanceEndorsement of a strategic bet or a stop
Q4Risk appetite and next yearProposed changes to technology risk appetite, the draft plan, succession and key-person risk in the technology teamApproval of the risk appetite statement

The one-page summary below goes to every meeting regardless of theme. Public companies also need the annual 10-K disclosure of board oversight in view when planning the Q4 or Q1 session, since what the board actually did during the year is what that disclosure describes.

Structure

Which part of the board hears what

Getting the forum wrong wastes the time of the people who should hear it and bores the people who should not.

ForumTypical technology contentYour usual counterpart
Full boardStrategy, major investments, material incidents, the annual risk appetiteBoard chair, through the CEO
Audit committeeCyber risk oversight in most public companies, controls, internal audit findings on technology, capitalization of softwareCommittee chair and the CFO
Risk committee, where one existsEnterprise risk register, operational resilience, third-party concentration, regulatory exposureChief risk officer
Technology committee, where one existsArchitecture direction, modernization progress, AI, talent in the technology functionCommittee chair, often the most technical director

In a survey of the S&P 100's first 10-K filings under the SEC cyber rules, about two thirds placed primary cyber oversight with a committee, and most of those named the audit committee.[2] Audit committees are already carrying financial reporting, so their cyber time is short. Bring them trends and exceptions. Save the explanation of how the program works for an annual session or a pre-read.

The standing page

The one page that goes to every meeting

Same layout every time, so a director can compare it with last quarter in under a minute.

  1. HeadlineTwo sentences. What changed since the last meeting and whether anything needs the board's attention. If nothing does, say so plainly.
  2. Six to eight metrics with a trendThe same metrics every quarter, each with the last four values and a target. A metric that appears once and disappears tells the board you dropped it because it looked bad.
  3. Top risksThree to five, each with its trend arrow and owner. A heat map works once there are more than two.
  4. Incidents since the last meetingEvery incident above an agreed severity, one line each, with customer impact and status of the follow-up. Include the ones that were handled well.
  5. Major programsStatus against the outcome promised when the board approved the money, not against the project plan.
  6. AskWhat you need from the board, or a clear statement that you need nothing this quarter.
Metrics

Metrics a director can use

Pick from these and keep the set stable. Each one should have a plain-language definition on the page the first time it appears.

MetricWhy a board caresWhat a bad trend usually means
Availability of revenue-critical services, against targetCustomer and revenue exposure in one numberCapacity, change discipline, or an aging platform. Pair it with incident count to tell which
Critical vulnerabilities open past the agreed fix windowThe gap between policy and practicePatching capacity is short or ownership is unclear, and audit will find it before an attacker does
Time to detect and contain a confirmed security incidentWhether the security spend works when it mattersLogging gaps or alert fatigue. Ask how many incidents were found by a third party
Privileged accounts, and the share reviewed this quarterThe most common route from a breach to a disasterAccess reviews are a formality, which is also an audit finding waiting to happen
Critical vendors with a current security assessmentExposure the company does not control directlyProcurement is signing faster than security can assess
Technology cost per unit of business volumeEfficiency independent of growthArchitecture debt or unmanaged cloud spend. The total alone cannot show this
Last successful restore or failover test, and its resultWhether the recovery plan is realA plan that has not been tested in a year should be treated as untested
Regretted attrition in the technology teamExecution capacity and key-person riskSomething in leadership, pay, or workload is wrong, and delivery dates will follow

Two cautions. Averages hide the incident that matters, so for detection and recovery show the worst case next to the median. And any metric tied to someone's bonus will improve on paper first, so know which of yours are.

Between meetings

Escalation thresholds, agreed before you need them

The promise that the quarterly report never contains a surprise only holds if everyone knows in advance what triggers a call.

TierExample triggersWho hears, and how fast
Call nowSuspected material breach, ransomware with operational impact, an outage of a revenue-critical system beyond its agreed tolerance, a regulator or law enforcement contact about an incidentCEO immediately. Board chair and the relevant committee chair the same day, by phone
Notify within daysAn incident that may become material as facts develop, loss of a key technology leader, a critical vendor failure with a workaround in placeCommittee chair within two business days, written
Next regular meetingIncidents handled within tolerance, risks that moved but stayed inside appetite, program slippage that does not change the outcomeThe standing page

Write the tiers into a short protocol the CEO and the board chair have both read. The value is that nobody has to decide under pressure whether something is board-worthy, which is the moment people talk themselves out of making the call.

Public companies

The internal clock and the disclosure clock are different

Confusing them is how a company ends up disclosing to the market before its own board has the facts.

Under the SEC rules adopted in 2023, a registrant has to determine whether a cybersecurity incident is material without unreasonable delay, and then file a Form 8-K under Item 1.05 within four business days of that determination. Separately, the annual 10-K has to describe, under Regulation S-K Item 106, how the board oversees cyber risk, which committee is responsible, and how management keeps it informed.[1] That annual description is only credible if the reporting it describes actually happened, which is one more reason to keep the standing page consistent.

Regulated firms can carry their own requirements on top. For entities licensed by the New York Department of Financial Services, Part 500 expects the CISO to report in writing to the board at least annually and to bring material cyber issues to it in a timely way, and it expects the board itself to have enough understanding of cyber matters to exercise oversight.[3]

The practical rule: the board should hear about a likely-material incident well before anyone outside the company does. Aim for within a day of the first credible signal, so counsel has room to run the materiality determination with the board informed. Disclosure obligations and the materiality process are legal questions. Settle them with counsel in advance, not during the incident.

Sources
  1. SEC press release 2023-139, cybersecurity risk management and incident disclosure rules
  2. NYDFS, 23 NYCRR Part 500 as amended November 2023
Translation

Rewriting a technical risk for the board

The risk is usually diagnosed in engineering language. The board decides in business language. Somebody has to translate, and it should be you.

As the engineers wrote it

Critical (CVSS 9.1) flaw in the OAuth token validation of the customer portal. Patch requires a framework upgrade estimated at six weeks.

As the board needs it

A weakness in how customers log in could let an attacker take over accounts. About 40,000 accounts are exposed, and a breach would trigger notification duties in three states. We have put compensating monitoring in place and the permanent fix lands in six weeks. We are accepting the residual risk until then, inside the appetite you approved.

The order is consequence, exposure, what is being done, and whose decision it is. The CVSS score can sit in an appendix for the director who wants it.
Preparation

Questions directors ask, and what a prepared answer contains

These come up in some form at most boards. Have the answer before the meeting, with a number in it.

The questionA prepared answer includes
Could what happened to that other company happen to us?Whether the same weakness exists here, the evidence you checked, and what would stop or limit it. Have this ready within days of any major public incident in your sector.
Are we spending enough on security?Spend against peers as a range, but mostly what the spend buys: the risks reduced and the ones still open. A percentage alone answers nothing.
How long would it take us to recover from ransomware?The result of the last real restore test, not the plan's target, and which systems would come back first.
What are we doing with AI, and is it safe?What is in production, what it costs, who approved each use, and what would trigger switching one off. See AI Governance.
Who would run technology if you left tomorrow?A named interim leader and how ready they are. See CTO Leadership & Succession.
Why does this program cost more than we approved?Which assumption moved, by how much, and what the outcome still is. Raise it before they ask.
Worked example

Two weeks before a meeting, after a bad quarter

A mid-size SaaS company had a nine-hour outage of its billing platform in August, caused by a failed database upgrade at a managed provider. Customers were not overcharged, but invoices went out four days late. The Q3 meeting is in mid-October. Figures are illustrative.

WhenWhat happenedWhy
August, same dayCEO informed during the outage. Audit committee chair called that evening, because billing is a revenue-critical system and the outage passed its agreed toleranceThe escalation protocol made it a call, not a judgment
Two weeks beforeDraft standing page shared with the CEO and CFO. The incident appears in the incident section and drives availability below target for the first time in five quartersNo one on the management team should learn about the framing in the meeting
Ten days beforeThirty-minute call with the audit committee chair to walk through the postmortem and the proposed askThe chair can shape the discussion and will not be surprised by the ask
One week beforePre-read sent: the standing page, a two-page incident summary, and the ask. The full postmortem is an appendixDirectors who read ahead ask better questions
The meetingTen minutes. Two on the headline and metrics, five on the incident and what changed, three on the ask: funding a second provider region for billing at $380k a year, against an estimated $1.1M exposure per comparable outageMost of the time goes to the decision, not the narrative
Next dayShort note to the chair recording the decision and the date the failover test will be reportedCloses the loop and sets up the next standing page

The ask was approved with a condition: report the failover test result at the Q1 meeting. That condition is a good outcome. It means the board is overseeing the risk, which is its job, and it gives the next report a fixed point to measure against.

AI and emerging risk

Reporting AI before anyone asks

Directors are being asked about AI by investors and regulators. They need a consistent account from you to answer.

Give AI a short standing section: what is in production, what it costs, which uses touch customers or regulated decisions, and what controls are in place, described in the same terms every quarter. Use the domains on the AI Governance Map so the vocabulary does not drift.

Do not hold back a pilot because it is still experimental. If it uses customer data or informs a regulated decision, the board's oversight duty already applies, and learning about it after an incident is far worse than a brief early mention. The same goes for longer-horizon items such as post-quantum cryptography readiness: one line a year on where you stand is enough until the timeline gets closer.

Common pitfall

The deck that reports activity

Thirty slides of sprints completed, tickets closed, and projects in green.

It feels thorough and it gives the board nothing to oversee. Directors cannot tell whether the green is earned or just unmeasured, and the one real issue is buried on slide nineteen, where nobody reaches it. When something does go wrong later, the deck is evidence that the issue was technically disclosed and practically hidden, which is the worst position to be in.

The fix is the standing page plus one deep dive and one ask. Everything else goes in an appendix or a reading room. If you find you cannot fit what matters on one page, that is usually a sign you have not yet decided what matters.

Technology Decision Rights

Making decision ownership explicit with a RACI-style model, because most executive conflict is an undefined decision right wearing a personality conflict's clothes.

1

Why explicit rights beat "we'll figure it out when it comes up"

The alternative to a stated model is a fresh political negotiation every time the same category of decision recurs.

What explicit rights do

A decision made once, in advance, about who owns what. A live disagreement becomes a matter of checking the model rather than relitigating it from scratch with whoever happens to be in the room and how forcefully they argue.

What ambiguity produces

The same category of decision gets resolved differently every time depending on who's present, which erodes trust in whoever "won" and guarantees the losing side argues it again next time, since nothing was settled.

2

Accountable and Responsible are different roles

The distinction most RACI exercises blur, which is exactly where they stop working.

The distinction

Responsible executes the work. Accountable owns the outcome and has final say when consulted input conflicts. Exactly one person should be Accountable per decision; if two people are both marked A, the model has documented the ambiguity without resolving it.

The common mistake

Marking multiple people Accountable to avoid conflict in the room where the RACI gets built. This moves the conflict to the moment the decision has to be made, when the stakes are higher and the room is smaller.

3

Building the table for your organization

The example above is a starting shape rather than a template to adopt unchanged.

Build it in a room with the stakeholders rather than alone. Force explicit disagreement about at least one row before finalizing; if nobody disagrees with anything, the exercise was probably too polite, since every organization has tension over at least a few of these decisions. Revisit it annually, or after any reorg, since org changes shift decision rights even when nobody remembers to update the document.

4

When a decision doesn't map to any existing row

A gap in the RACI is information. Improvising quietly and moving on wastes it.

What to do

Decide deliberately whether this is new, add a row, on the record, with the same rigor as the original exercise, or a variant of an existing row whose language was too narrow. Either way, the document changes instead of the gap being patched over informally and forgotten.

Common failure mode

Treating each gap as a one-off exception handled quietly in the moment. The RACI slowly becomes less accurate than the day it was built, and by the second year it covers less of what's happening than it did on day one, without anyone deciding that should happen.

5

When Consulted input gets ignored

The most common source of quiet resentment in a working RACI, and it's usually a misunderstanding of what Consulted promises.

What Consulted means

The Accountable party has to hear the input before deciding, and is free to decide against it. Consulted is never a veto, and treating it like one defeats the entire purpose of having a single accountable owner who can move a decision forward.

When it's a problem

The same Consulted party's input gets dismissed without explanation, repeatedly, on the same category of decision. The RACI is working as written; the signal is that the Accountable assignment itself may be wrong, or that the underlying relationship needs the direct conversation described on Working with the CEO or Working with the CFO.

6

A worked disagreement

What the mechanics above look like resolving a live tension.

Product wants to ship a new vendor integration fast, a competitor just launched something similar and the sales team is asking daily. Security flags a data residency concern with the vendor's hosting: customer data would leave the region a key enterprise contract requires it to stay in.

Checking the table: major vendor selection has the CTO Accountable, CISO and Product both Consulted, Engineering Responsible. The CISO's flag carries no veto, but the CTO has to weigh it rather than note it and move on. In this case, the CTO goes back to the CISO for the specific requirement, then negotiates a contractual data residency clause with the vendor rather than blocking the integration outright. The integration ships two weeks later than Product wanted. Security's concern gets addressed rather than either overridden or allowed to stall the decision indefinitely. Nobody has to relitigate who had the final call, because the table already said so before the disagreement ever started.

RACI is one model among several; match the model to the decision

RACI documents who does what. It is weakest at the moment of a contested decision, which is why several alternatives exist. Most organizations use more than one.

Classify by reversibility before assigning an owner

The fastest way to cut executive decision load is to separate decisions that are expensive to undo from those that are not. Amazon popularized the one-way and two-way door framing; the idea is older and simpler than the label. Thresholds below are illustrative for a mid-size company.

DecisionReversible (two-way door)Hard to reverse (one-way door)
Default ownerThe team closest to the workA named senior owner, with the Consulted list honored in writing
ProcessDecide, record in a line or two, move onWritten options with tradeoffs, an ADR or decision memo, a review date
Spend (illustrative)Under $50K a year within approved budget: team lead. Under $250K: directorOver $1M or any multi-year commitment: CTO with CFO. Over $5M or a new strategic vendor: CEO, and the board if above its delegated limit
ArchitectureInside one team's boundary, following approved patternsNew system of record, new shared platform, or a deviation from a principle: through the Architecture Review Board, CTO accountable
Risk acceptanceMonitor-tier items within stated toleranceTemporary acceptance or anything reaching board visibility, per Technology Risk Appetite
9

The decision log

The artifact that turns a decision-rights model from a chart into a working system.

A decision log is a single, searchable list of consequential decisions: what was decided, the date, who decided, which options were considered, who was consulted, the one-paragraph rationale, and when it will be revisited. Architecture decisions live as ADRs, described on ADRs and Roadmapping; the log indexes them alongside commercial, organizational, and risk decisions.

Two practices make it pay for itself. First, link to the log entry whenever a settled question comes up again; that alone ends most relitigation. Second, review the log quarterly for decisions that were reversed or quietly ignored. A reversal is not a failure, but a pattern of reversals in one category usually means the wrong person holds the decision right, or the right person decided without the input they needed.

Checklist

Signs decision rights are still effectively undefined

Having a document and having working decision rights are different things. These patterns show up when the model exists on paper but isn't load-bearing.

  • The same category of decision gets escalated differently depending on who's involved
  • Two people both believe they're Accountable for the same decision
  • The RACI was built once and never revisited despite a reorg since
  • Decisions get made in hallway conversations that bypass the stated model entirely
  • No one can locate the document when a live disagreement happens
  • A Consulted party's input has been dismissed without explanation on the same category of decision more than once
  • No distinction is made between reversible and hard-to-reverse decisions, so both get the same slow process or the same casual one
  • Spend approval limits are not written down, so the same purchase is approved at different levels depending on who asks
Reference only. RAPID is a Bain & Company framework, DACI originated in industry practice and is widely published by Atlassian, and the advice process is described in the organizational design literature and applied to architecture by Andrew Harmel-Law.

Platform Engineering

The team and system that make good delivery metrics achievable, treating internal infrastructure as a product with users, not a shared utility maintained as an afterthought.

This covers the platform team model. See Engineering Metrics for the DORA outcomes a good platform makes achievable.
Goals

What this is for

The central goal, and the ones downstream of it. Self-service, golden paths, and everything else on this page are how these get achieved, not goals themselves.

GoalWhat it means
Reduce cognitive load on product engineering teamsThe central goal everything else serves. A team building a product shouldn't also have to be expert in Kubernetes, network policy, database operations, and compliance tagging just to ship; the platform absorbs that complexity.
Increase delivery velocityGetting from "I need X" to "I have X" in minutes or hours instead of days or weeks.
Enforce standards consistentlySecurity, compliance, cost tagging, and network policy encoded once, in a template or golden path, rather than re-implemented, and inevitably re-implemented inconsistently, by every team on its own.
Eliminate duplicated effort across teamsWithout a shared platform, every team quietly builds its own version of the same deployment pipeline, the same provisioning process, the same monitoring setup.
Make scarce expertise available broadlyA platform team's depth in networking, security, or database operations gets embedded into the platform itself, so every product team benefits without needing to hire for it directly.
Improve developer experience in its own rightNot just a side effect of the goals above. A platform can be technically capable and still fail if the people using it actively dislike it.
Framework

Platform as a product, not a shared utility

The distinction that determines whether a platform team gets adopted voluntarily or gets worked around.

A platform built as a product has users (product engineering teams), a roadmap shaped by what those users need, and success measured by adoption and satisfaction, the same discipline applied to any external-facing product. A platform built as a shared utility gets whatever capacity is left over after feature work, has no real user research behind its priorities, and gets adopted only where teams have no other choice. The first approach produces a platform teams want to use; the second produces one they route around the moment they're allowed to.

Reference

Cloud versus on-prem: what changes

The self-service and golden path guidance below was written cloud-first, since that's the more mature case. Here's what's different if some or all of the estate is on-prem or bare metal.

DimensionCloudOn-prem / bare metal
Provisioning speedMinutes, via APIHours if capacity is already racked; weeks or months if new hardware has to be ordered
ScalingElastic; spin up and tear down on demandBounded by physical capacity already on hand; scaling means ordering and racking more of it
Self-service ceilingEffectively unlimited from the requester's point of viewSelf-service against a pre-provisioned capacity pool; a request past available headroom has to queue or escalate, it can't just be self-service
Cost modelOpex, pay-per-use, elasticLargely fixed capex already spent; utilization efficiency matters more than avoiding waste, since the hardware cost is sunk either way
Tooling maturityMature; every major IaC tool has first-class cloud provider supportLess mature; bare metal is still commonly flagged in current platform engineering discussion as "artisanal infrastructure," provisioned manually and outside the platform's own tooling, even at organizations that are otherwise fully API-driven everywhere else

Most real platform teams in 2026 aren't choosing one or the other. A common pattern: steady-state, predictable workloads (databases, core services) run on-prem or on bare metal where it's cheaper at scale or required for latency and compliance reasons, while variable and spiky workloads scale in the cloud. A small number of providers now offer "bare metal cloud," single-tenant physical performance with the same API-driven provisioning cloud users expect, narrowing this gap further, but it remains the less mature option and needs to be evaluated as such, not assumed equivalent to a mainstream cloud provider's tooling.

Self-service versus ticket-driven infrastructure

Whether provisioning something new takes minutes or a multi-day queue determines how much of the platform's value reaches engineering teams.

Practice

Common infrastructure needs, a new service, a database, a deployment pipeline, provisioned through self-service tooling with sane defaults, no ticket or human approval required for the standard case. Reserve human review for genuinely unusual requests, not the routine ones that make up the bulk of demand.

Common failure mode

Every infrastructure request routed through a ticket queue regardless of how routine it is, which caps how fast product teams can move at the platform team's ticket-processing capacity rather than at the actual complexity of the request.

Worked example

Self-service provisioning, concretely

What "self-service tooling with sane defaults" looks like, rather than left as an abstraction.

A developer needs a new PostgreSQL database for a service. Through a developer portal (Backstage is the common open-source choice; Port is a common commercial alternative) or an internal CLI, they select a pre-approved "new database" template and fill in a short form: service name, environment, expected scale tier.

Submitting the form triggers an infrastructure-as-code pipeline, often a Terraform module or a Crossplane composition, that provisions the database with the organization's standards already baked in: encryption at rest, standard tagging for cost allocation, automated backups, and network policy applied without the developer having to know or configure any of it themselves. The database is ready in minutes, registered automatically in the service catalog, with ownership and cost attribution wired in from the start. No ticket, no human approval, for the standard case.

The template itself is where governance lives, not in a review step. Security, compliance, and tagging requirements get encoded once, in the template, rather than re-checked by a human on every request. A genuinely unusual request, a non-standard database engine, an exception to the network policy, is exactly the case that should still route to a human, because it's the case the template wasn't built to handle safely on its own.

On-prem, the same template pattern applies, provisioning still runs through a pipeline with standards baked in, but the pipeline also has to check available capacity before it commits, and route to a human when the request would exceed it. That capacity check has no real equivalent in the cloud version of this example above.

The database example above is self-service at the resource level, inside an environment that already exists. The same discipline applies one level up: issuing an entire new, pre-governed subscription or account to a team, Azure calls this subscription vending, AWS calls it Account Factory, GCP typically calls it a project factory. See the landing zone reference on Cloud Service Mapper for how each provider's version works.

Golden path design

A golden path is the supported, well-paved way to accomplish a common task, deliberately easier than any alternative.

Practice

Design the golden path around the most common use case, make it genuinely the fastest option available, and keep it optional rather than mandatory. Teams with unusual requirements should be able to deviate, just without platform-team support for the parts they've customized away from the standard.

Common failure mode

A golden path built for an idealized use case that doesn't match what most teams need, so adoption stays low and everyone keeps building their own version, defeating the purpose of standardizing in the first place.

3

Team funding and organizational placement

Platform teams funded as pure overhead get cut first when budget tightens, regardless of the value they're delivering.

Practice

Frame and track platform investment against a measurable outcome, deployment frequency, lead time, incident rate, so its value is visible in the same terms leadership already uses to evaluate engineering effectiveness, not treated as a cost center whose contribution is assumed rather than shown.

Common failure mode

A platform team that can't articulate its impact in outcomes anyone outside engineering tracks, which makes it the easiest line item to cut in a downturn, even when its removal would slow every other engineering team down.

4

Developer experience as a deliberate metric

What a platform team optimizes for shapes what it builds; measuring the experience, not just uptime, keeps the incentives pointed at the right outcome.

Regular, structured feedback from the engineers using the platform, time-to-first-deploy for a new service, satisfaction survey results, support ticket volume and resolution time, treated as seriously as uptime and reliability metrics. A platform can be technically reliable and still be actively disliked by the people forced to use it, and that dissatisfaction shows up eventually as workarounds and shadow infrastructure.

5

The overbuilt platform trap

Platform teams, left to their own judgment about priorities, tend toward building for imagined future scale rather than the problems product teams have today.

What works

Prioritization driven by direct input from the teams the platform serves, with roadmap decisions traceable to a specific request or a specific measured friction point, the same product discipline applied to any customer-facing roadmap.

What backfires

An elaborate multi-cluster, multi-region abstraction layer built for a scale the company is nowhere near, while product teams struggle with a basic deployment step nobody prioritized fixing because it wasn't as technically interesting to build.

Checklist

Signs the platform isn't earning its adoption

Patterns that show up when a platform team exists but isn't delivering the leverage it's meant to.

  • Product teams maintain workarounds or shadow infrastructure instead of using the platform's supported path
  • Common provisioning tasks require a ticket and a multi-day wait rather than self-service
  • The platform roadmap isn't traceable to specific requests or friction reported by the teams it serves
  • Platform investment can't be tied to any measurable engineering outcome when leadership asks what it's delivering
  • Developer satisfaction with the platform has never been measured, only assumed
Reference only. Platform team scope and investment level should scale with engineering org size; a ten-person engineering team rarely needs a dedicated platform function.

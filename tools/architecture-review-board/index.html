Architecture Review Board

How to stand up a review board that examines evidence from engineering work already done, rather than diagrams produced for the meeting, and how to keep it working as a forcing function for years after the founding energy fades.

See ADRs & Roadmapping for the record a review should produce, Architecture Principles Library for what a board is applying, and Technology Decision Rights for where the board's authority ends and a named individual's begins.
Reference

What belongs in front of the board, and what doesn't

The most common way a review board fails is reviewing everything, which turns it into a bottleneck and trains teams to route around it. The same filter used to decide when a decision needs an ADR applies here.

Goes to the boardDoesn't need the board
A new external dependency or a shared service other teams will depend onA team's fully contained internal implementation detail
A decision that affects more than one team's roadmapA reversible, single-team decision costing less than a day to undo
A new system of record or data store other systems will read fromAdding a field to a schema the team already owns exclusively
A deliberate deviation from an established architecture principleFollowing a pattern the board has already approved elsewhere
1

Evidence over drawings

The distinction that separates a review with teeth from a rubber stamp with a diagram attached.

What a working review looks at

The running system, observed traffic patterns, incident history, a working prototype or spike, existing code. Evidence produced by doing the engineering work rather than evidence produced to satisfy the meeting.

The theater version

A polished architecture diagram produced in the days before the review, disconnected from the codebase, that nobody consults again once the meeting ends. The tell: check the diagram file's last-modified date six months later. If it's the review date, it was never a working artifact.

2

Setting it up: composition and authority

Two decisions made once, at founding, that determine whether the board has standing or only a name.

Composition

A small standing group, four to six people, spanning the systems most likely to be affected by cross-cutting decisions. Rotate membership on a defined cycle so it doesn't calcify into the same five people's opinions indefinitely. The proposing team's own senior engineer is always in the room, never reviewed in absentia.

Authority, stated explicitly

See Technology Decision Rights: the board should be Consulted, rarely Accountable. A named individual, the CTO or a delegate, is accountable for architecture standards and can be persuaded by a strong board recommendation, but the board's vote is input to that person's decision and can be overridden.

3

The review itself

What makes a specific session evidence-based rather than theater, independent of how well the board was set up.

Practice

The proposing team walks through the tradeoffs they considered and rejected, the same alternatives-considered discipline covered on the ADR page, with numbers behind claims (this handles X load, this costs Y) rather than assertions. The review produces a decision and its reasoning captured in an ADR. A verbal thumbs-up disappears when the meeting ends.

Common failure mode

The review becomes a presentation the team already knows how to pass: confident language and a clean diagram calibrated to satisfy the audience rather than stress-test the reasoning. At that point the board is a rubber stamp with theater added, arguably worse than no review, since it spends time producing a false signal of scrutiny.

4

Running it long-term without decaying into theater

A board that works well in its first year and quietly stops working in its third is the most common lifecycle, and it's predictable enough to design against.

What sustains it

A standing cadence, biweekly is common, that runs whether or not there's a full docket. A public backlog of upcoming reviews so teams plan around it instead of being surprised. Periodic retrospectives on the board's own past decisions: did what it approved work out, was what it blocked right to block.

How it decays

The board approves the same rubber-stamp pattern for months. Membership never rotates, so the room's authority rests on tenure rather than current context. No one checks whether a past decision held up, so the board never learns whether its own judgment is any good, and keeps operating on the same instincts regardless of outcomes.

5

Empty slots: turn them into innovation salons instead of canceling

What to do with a scheduled review slot that has nothing queued for it, which happens regularly once a board is running well and teams aren't manufacturing proposals just to fill the docket.

The idea

Instead of canceling the slot, which trains people to deprioritize the cadence, or filling it with a low-stakes item just to have something on the agenda, open it up: anyone can bring an early-stage idea, a technical curiosity, or a "here's something I've been thinking about" for informal discussion. No funding decision required, no ADR required, just structured space for ideas that aren't ready for a formal pitch yet.

Why this works better than canceling

It keeps the room's habit and rhythm intact, the same people, the same regular slot, the same expectation of showing up prepared to think carefully, without wasting the time when nothing formal needs review. It also creates a low-stakes on-ramp: an idea that draws interest in a salon session is the kind of thing ready to become a formal pitch at the next innovation tournament.

The board and the tournament are the same room used two different ways: one evaluates evidence against a decision that needs to be made, the other surfaces ideas that don't have evidence yet. Keeping both on the same cadence, in the same room, with the same people, is what makes the salon slot feel like a natural extension rather than a separate initiative competing for calendar space.

Review tiers and turnaround commitments

A board earns trust by being fast on the routine and thorough on the consequential. Publishing turnaround commitments is what stops teams from routing around it. Times are suggested starting points.

Standards and practices worth borrowing

Most of what makes a board work is already written down elsewhere. Borrow deliberately rather than inventing a process.

SourceWhat it contributes
Architecture Decision Records (Michael Nygard, 2011)The short, immutable record of context, decision, and consequences that every review should produce
ISO/IEC/IEEE 42010:2022A precise vocabulary for architecture descriptions: stakeholders, their concerns, and the viewpoints that address them. Useful for deciding what a proposal must show
C4 model (Simon Brown)Four levels of diagram, context, containers, components, code, so reviewers see the right level of detail instead of one crowded picture
arc42A free, widely used template for documenting a system; a good default for the evidence pack on tier 2 reviews
Architecture fitness functions (Ford, Parsons, Kua, Sadalage, Building Evolutionary Architectures, 2nd ed., 2022)Turning an architecture decision into an automated check so it stays true after the meeting
The architecture advice process (Andrew Harmel-Law, Facilitating Software Architecture, 2024)A decentralized alternative in which teams decide after seeking advice, with the board acting as an advisory forum and keeper of the decision log
TOGAF Standard, 10th EditionThe formal Architecture Board and compliance review concepts, useful for regulated enterprises that need an auditable governance trail
8

Fitness functions: keep the decision true after the meeting

A decision that exists only in an ADR decays as soon as the next deadline arrives. Encode the important ones.

Practice

For each tier 2 decision, ask what automated check would fail if the decision were violated, then put it in the pipeline. Examples:

  • Dependency rules that stop one domain calling another's database directly
  • A latency budget checked in a performance test
  • A cost ceiling alarm on a new service
  • A policy-as-code rule that blocks public storage buckets
Common failure mode

Treating the board's approval as the control. Six months later the system has drifted, nobody noticed, and the next review argues about a design that no longer exists in production.

9

Measure the board itself

A board that never measures its own performance cannot tell whether it adds value or delay. These five numbers, reviewed quarterly, are enough.

MetricWhat healthy looks likeWhat it warns about
Median days from request to decisionWithin the published turnaround for each tierRising numbers mean teams will start routing around the board
Share of proposals changed by reviewSomewhere between a fifth and a halfNear zero is a rubber stamp; very high means teams arrive unprepared or the principles are unclear
Decisions with a written ADRAll tier 1 and tier 2 decisionsAnything lower means the board's reasoning is lost when members rotate
Decisions revisited after 6 to 12 monthsA sample reviewed every quarter, with a verdict on whether each held upNo review of outcomes means the board never learns whether its judgment is good
Bypass rateFew or no systems in production that should have come to the board and did notA high bypass rate is the clearest sign the board is seen as an obstacle
Checklist

Signs the board has become theater

Patterns that show up when a review board exists on the calendar but has stopped functioning as a forcing function.

  • Reviews consistently approve with no pushback or requested changes
  • No one can name a decision the board changed its mind about after seeing how it played out
  • Board membership hasn't rotated in over a year
  • Decisions aren't captured anywhere durable, no ADR, no written record, once the meeting ends
  • Teams prepare a specific "board-ready" version of their architecture that differs from what's running in production
  • Nobody can say the median time from review request to decision
  • Important decisions exist only in ADRs, with no automated check to catch drift

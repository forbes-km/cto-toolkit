Engineering Culture

What shapes how an engineering organization behaves day to day, beyond org charts and values decks. The thing every other page under building the organization quietly assumes is healthy underneath it.

Reference

Symbol versus substance

The gap between what an organization says its culture is and what determines behavior is where most culture work fails.

SymbolWhat determines behavior
Stated values on a wall or a slideWhat gets rewarded in promotion and compensation decisions
A culture deckWhat happens in the room when a deadline and a quality standard conflict
Perks and amenitiesWhether people ship work they're proud to put their name on
"We value work-life balance"Whether anyone leaves on time without guilt or a backlog of resentment
1

Culture is whatever gets rewarded

The clearest predictor of the culture you have is what a high performer gets away with that no one else would.

What shapes it

Promotion criteria, what leadership visibly celebrates in public forums, and what gets quietly tolerated from a top performer that would draw a consequence for anyone else. These three things are the culture, regardless of what the values document says.

The trap

Writing a values document and treating the exercise as complete, without ever auditing whether promotion decisions, performance reviews, and day-to-day tolerance reflect those stated values. The gap between the two is usually visible to everyone except leadership.

2

A worked example: diagnosing a gap

What the audit in section 1 looks like run against data instead of impressions.

A company's values deck states "we value work-life balance and sustainable pace." Nobody has ever written a rule contradicting that. But a look at the last two promotion cycles against work-pattern data shows every engineer promoted to senior had a visible pattern of late-night and weekend Slack activity; nobody promoted in that window kept a strictly nine-to-six pattern.

No survey would have caught this, since a pulse survey asks about stated values rather than revealed behavior. Cross-referencing promotion outcomes against a specific behavioral signal (hours of activity rather than self-reported sentiment) made the reward function visible immediately. The stated value and what gets rewarded had diverged completely, and the gap stayed invisible until someone looked at the right data instead of asking the right question.

3

Blameless is a practice

See Incident Response for the mechanics of a blameless postmortem. This is about what determines whether the practice survives contact with a bad outage.

What sustains it

Leadership visibly not punishing the engineer who caused a serious incident, in front of the team, repeatedly, until it's trusted rather than merely declared in a policy document. Trust here is built slowly, one incident at a time.

What kills it

One visible instance of blame after a sufficiently bad incident erases months of stated blameless culture instantly. The trust is asymmetric: it takes a long series of consistent restraint to build and one data point to destroy.

4

Whose culture wins in a merger or a scaling moment

Two cultures rarely blend into something new. One absorbs the other, and which one wins is more predictable than most leaders expect.

What determines dominance

The size of the surviving organization, which leadership team's members remain in place after the transition, and which systems and processes get adopted as the combined standard. All three tend to point the same direction.

The mistake

Assuming a merger or rapid scaling event will produce a neutral, blended culture through goodwill alone, rather than deliberately deciding what's worth preserving from the smaller or absorbed side and building that preservation explicitly into the integration plan.

5

Remote and hybrid culture specifically

The core mechanism doesn't change with location. What changes is how much deliberate effort it takes to make it visible.

What stays the same

Culture is still whatever gets rewarded. If anything, it becomes more visible remotely, since behavior happens in trackable channels, Slack timestamps, meeting attendance, async response times, rather than the more ambiguous signal physical presence in an office used to provide.

What changes

The blameless-culture trust described above depends on visible, repeated leadership behavior. Remote teams see far less of that by default, no hallway conversations, no visible body language during a tense moment, so leadership has to be far more deliberate about making the same restraint visible through explicit written communication rather than trusting it'll simply be felt.

6

Burnout as a distinct signal from attrition

Someone burned out and still employed is a worse leading indicator than someone who already left.

Why it's distinct

Declining output and a rising error rate in a burned-out but still-employed engineer often get misread as a performance problem rather than a culture symptom, precisely because they haven't yet generated the attrition data point that would trigger an investigation.

What to watch

A sudden change in someone's own baseline, working hours, response time, output quality, is a more reliable signal than a static threshold. A chronically overworked culture normalizes a baseline that looks fine compared to itself but wouldn't compare well to a healthy organization.

7

Measuring culture without gaming it

See Engineering Metrics for the trust problem that determines whether any metrics program survives; culture measurement has the identical problem.

What to track

Voluntary attrition, especially regretted attrition among senior or high-performing people, pulse survey trends over time rather than a single point-in-time score, and whether people internally refer friends for open roles, a strong revealed-preference signal that's hard to fake.

What doesn't work

A single annual engagement survey score treated as the complete picture, gamed the same way any metric gets gamed once it's tied to a leader's own performance review and that leader controls how the survey gets framed and followed up on.

What the research actually says

Culture is one of the few areas where the evidence is consistent across many years of studies. Leaning on it moves the conversation away from opinion.

SourceFinding worth using
Ron Westrum's typology of organizational cultures (2004)Organizations fall into pathological, bureaucratic, or generative patterns, distinguished mainly by how information flows and how messengers are treated
DORA research program (Google Cloud, annual since 2014)Generative culture, in Westrum's sense, repeatedly predicts better software delivery and organizational performance. The 2025 report found AI amplifies whatever culture and practices a team already has
Amy Edmondson on psychological safety (1999 onward)Teams that feel safe to raise problems report more errors early and learn faster, which looks worse on a dashboard in the short run and better in outcomes
Google's Project Aristotle (2015 to 2016)Psychological safety was the strongest of the factors that distinguished effective teams, ahead of team composition
SPACE framework (Forsgren, Storey, and others, 2021)Productivity has several dimensions, including satisfaction and wellbeing; measuring output alone misreads it
DevEx research (Noda, Storey, Forsgren, Greiler, 2023)Developer experience comes down to three things leaders can change: fast feedback loops, manageable cognitive load, and protected flow time
9

Measuring it: a short team-level pulse

A small, repeatable instrument beats an annual engagement survey for spotting drift. Run it quarterly at team level, report trends, and never tie it to an individual manager's rating.

Ask each engineer to rate agreement from 1 to 7 with six statements built on Westrum's dimensions, paraphrased here:

  • People on my team actively seek out the information they need, and get it
  • People who bring bad news or raise problems are not punished for it
  • Responsibilities are shared rather than pushed onto others
  • Working across team boundaries is encouraged
  • When something fails, the response is inquiry into the system, not a search for someone to blame
  • New ideas are welcomed, including from junior people

Add two DevEx items, how often work is blocked waiting on someone else and how often the engineer gets two uninterrupted hours, and pair the results with regretted attrition and internal referral rates. The single most useful reading is the item that moved most since last quarter, not the average.

Checklist

Signs culture is deteriorating beneath a healthy-looking surface

Patterns that show up before the problem is visible in a survey score or an exit interview.

  • Voluntary attrition among senior or high-performing engineers is elevated, even if overall attrition looks acceptable
  • Employees have quietly stopped referring friends for open roles
  • The stated values and the working promotion criteria have diverged, and most people already know it
  • Blameless postmortems exist as a stated policy, but the last serious incident produced visible blame anyway
  • Pulse survey trends are flat or declining while leadership continues describing culture as strong in the same conversation
  • Someone's individual work baseline has shifted noticeably and it's being treated as a performance issue rather than investigated as a possible culture symptom
  • Culture is measured only by an annual engagement score, never by team-level items on information flow and psychological safety
Reference only. Draws on Ron Westrum's typology of organizational cultures, the DORA research program including the 2025 State of AI-assisted Software Development report, Amy Edmondson's work on psychological safety, Google's Project Aristotle, the SPACE framework, and published DevEx research.

We're Cutting Costs

The number has probably already been chosen for you. What you still control is whether the cut lands on things that matter, whether the savings are real or just deferred, and whether the organization on the other side of it can still deliver. This page sequences the decisions and links to the pages that hold the detail.

Questions to ask

Questions that separate real savings from deferred cost

Most cost plans fail because nobody asked whether the saving is a reduction, a deferral, or a transfer. Ask these before any number goes to the CFO.

  • What is the actual run cost of the estate, by system, and how much of it is licensing that scales with headcount you are about to reduce
  • Which applications on the TIME map are already marked tolerate or eliminate, and what has stopped them being retired so far
  • How much of cloud spend is uncommitted on-demand, and how much of the committed spend is under-utilized
  • Which contracts renew in the next twelve months, at what value, and with what notice period
  • For every proposed saving, is it a reduction in run cost, a deferral of spend that returns later, or a transfer of cost to another team or the business
  • What is the cost of the next major incident, and does the proposed cut increase its probability
  • Which roles, if cut, remove the only person who understands a system that still makes money
  • What does the security program lose, specifically, and does that change the stated risk appetite
  • What would the team stop doing if headcount were reduced, and has anyone written that list down
The trap

The across-the-board percentage

The most common way to cut technology cost is to apply the same percentage to every team and category. It protects nothing that matters and reflects no prioritization; it just avoids the discomfort of choosing.

What it looks like

Every team loses fifteen percent. Security loses its pen test budget, the platform team loses the engineer who was automating the thing that costs the most, and the revenue-critical system's on-call rota drops to two people. Nine months later the run cost is back where it was, the deferred work is more expensive, and the best people have left because they could see nothing was being decided.

The counter-move

Decide the protected categories first, then cut deeper in fewer places. Retire whole systems rather than trimming every system. Take the vendor renewals seriously; they are usually the largest real, non-recurring saving available. Report savings only when they show up in the ledger, and keep the ranked list alive after the cut so the next round, if it comes, is already answered.

Outputs

What should exist at 30, 60, and 90 days

The announcement is the easy part. These are the artifacts that make the saving real and defensible.

CheckpointWhat exists
Day 30A protected-category list agreed with the CEO. A ranked cut list with consequences stated, already reviewed by the CFO privately. A vendor renewal calendar for the next twelve months with a strategy per contract. An explicit stop, pause, and continue decision for every in-flight initiative.
Day 60Cloud commitment coverage and rightsizing actions executed, with the saving visible in the bill rather than in a forecast. The first vendor renegotiations closed. Headcount decisions, if any, made and communicated once, not in waves. A written record of what the organization has stopped doing.
Day 90A savings tracker reconciled against actual spend, signed by finance. A restart condition recorded for every paused initiative. The security and reliability posture re-stated against the new spend level so nobody is surprised later. The next round of cuts, if asked for, answered from the same list rather than from scratch.

Technology Strategy

What a technology strategy is, as distinct from a roadmap or a tech stack decision, the three-horizon investment split, the test that ties an initiative to a real business outcome, and why tradeoffs, not initiatives, are what make a strategy usable.

A framework for thinking through strategy formulation, not a substitute for the strategic analysis specific to your business, market, and competitive position.
1

What a technology strategy is

Not a roadmap, not a list of tools, and not a tech stack decision. A frequent source of confusion worth resolving before anything else.

What it is

An explicit, prioritized set of choices about where to invest scarce engineering capacity, what capabilities to build versus buy, and what technical risk to knowingly accept versus what to remediate. A strategy is defined as much by what it says no to as what it says yes to.

What it gets confused with

A roadmap is the sequenced execution of a strategy, not the strategy itself. A tech stack decision is an implementation detail below the strategy. A wish list of every capability engineering would like to build, with nothing prioritized against anything else, isn't a strategy at all.

Framework

Three horizons

Adapted from McKinsey's Three Horizons model, applied to technology investment specifically. Most technology strategies fail by spending everything on Horizon 1 and nothing on Horizon 3, then getting blindsided by a competitor who didn't. See Innovation Portfolio Management for how Horizon 2 and 3 get run as a managed portfolio rather than a handful of side projects.

HorizonFocusTypical investment share
Horizon 1Optimize and defend the core: keep what's working reliable, fast, and cheap to runRoughly 60 to 70 percent
Horizon 2Extend into adjacent, emerging opportunities with a clearer but not yet proven path to valueRoughly 20 to 30 percent
Horizon 3Genuine bets: unproven, high-uncertainty, high-potential-payoff explorationRoughly 5 to 10 percent

The "so that" test

The single question that separates a strategy tied to business outcomes from a list of things engineering finds interesting.

How it works

Every strategic initiative should complete the sentence: "we will invest in X, so that Y business outcome becomes possible, measured by Z." If an initiative can't complete that sentence with a specific business outcome and a way to measure it, it doesn't belong in the strategy document, whatever its technical merit.

Where it gets skipped

Infrastructure and platform investments are the most common place this test gets waived, on the assumption that their value is self-evident. It rarely is to a non-technical stakeholder, and skipping the test here is exactly why platform investment is often the hardest thing to get funded.

3

Tradeoffs are the strategy

What separates a document leadership uses from one that gets written once and never referenced again.

A real strategy document

States explicit tradeoffs: we are prioritizing reliability over feature velocity this year, we are building this capability in-house rather than buying because it's a genuine differentiator, we are accepting this specific technical risk rather than remediating it now because the cost of remediation exceeds its near-term impact.

A document that gets ignored

Lists initiatives with no stated tradeoff, which means every initiative reads as equally important, which means nobody can use the document to make a prioritization call when two initiatives compete for the same engineers.

Technology Strategy

What a technology strategy is, as distinct from a roadmap or a tech stack decision, the three-horizon investment split, the test that ties an initiative to a real business outcome, and why tradeoffs, not initiatives, are what make a strategy usable.

A framework for thinking through strategy formulation, not a substitute for the strategic analysis specific to your business, market, and competitive position.
1

What a technology strategy is

Not a roadmap, not a list of tools, and not a tech stack decision. A frequent source of confusion worth resolving before anything else.

What it is

An explicit, prioritized set of choices about where to invest scarce engineering capacity, what capabilities to build versus buy, and what technical risk to knowingly accept versus what to remediate. A strategy is defined as much by what it says no to as what it says yes to.

What it gets confused with

A roadmap is the sequenced execution of a strategy, not the strategy itself. A tech stack decision is an implementation detail below the strategy. A wish list of every capability engineering would like to build, with nothing prioritized against anything else, isn't a strategy at all.

Framework

Three horizons

Adapted from McKinsey's Three Horizons model, applied to technology investment specifically. Most technology strategies fail by spending everything on Horizon 1 and nothing on Horizon 3, then getting blindsided by a competitor who didn't. See Innovation Portfolio Management for how Horizon 2 and 3 get run as a managed portfolio rather than a handful of side projects.

HorizonFocusTypical investment share
Horizon 1Optimize and defend the core: keep what's working reliable, fast, and cheap to runRoughly 60 to 70 percent
Horizon 2Extend into adjacent, emerging opportunities with a clearer but not yet proven path to valueRoughly 20 to 30 percent
Horizon 3Genuine bets: unproven, high-uncertainty, high-potential-payoff explorationRoughly 5 to 10 percent
2

The "so that" test

The single question that separates a strategy tied to business outcomes from a list of things engineering finds interesting.

How it works

Every strategic initiative should complete the sentence: "we will invest in X, so that Y business outcome becomes possible, measured by Z." If an initiative can't complete that sentence with a specific business outcome and a way to measure it, it doesn't belong in the strategy document, whatever its technical merit.

Where it gets skipped

Infrastructure and platform investments are the most common place this test gets waived, on the assumption that their value is self-evident. It rarely is to a non-technical stakeholder, and skipping the test here is exactly why platform investment is often the hardest thing to get funded.

3

Tradeoffs are the strategy

What separates a document leadership uses from one that gets written once and never referenced again.

A real strategy document

States explicit tradeoffs: we are prioritizing reliability over feature velocity this year, we are building this capability in-house rather than buying because it's a genuine differentiator, we are accepting this specific technical risk rather than remediating it now because the cost of remediation exceeds its near-term impact.

A document that gets ignored

Lists initiatives with no stated tradeoff, which means every initiative reads as equally important, which means nobody can use the document to make a prioritization call when two initiatives compete for the same engineers.

4

A worked example

A compressed real scenario showing the method end to end: strategic priority through horizon allocation through a stated tradeoff.

Situation: a mid-market SaaS company, $60M ARR, engineering team of 90, competing against two larger incumbents on feature breadth and one well-funded startup on price. The CEO wants to know what the technology organization is going to do about it this year.

Strategic priority: defend gross margin while shipping the two integrations sales says are costing the most deals, without adding headcount.

HorizonWhat's in it this yearThe "so that" test applied
Horizon 1 (65%)Infrastructure cost reduction, on-call load reduction, the two highest-defect legacy modules stabilized"We will invest in cost reduction, so that gross margin improves 4 points without a price increase, measured monthly"
Horizon 2 (25%)The two integrations sales has flagged, built on a shared integration platform rather than one-off connectors"We will invest in a shared integration layer, so that the next five integration requests ship in weeks not quarters, measured by integration lead time"
Horizon 3 (10%)A single exploratory bet: whether usage-based pricing is technically feasible on the current billing system"We will invest in a pricing model spike, so that the leadership team has a real answer before the next pricing conversation, measured by a working prototype, not a slide deck"

The stated tradeoff: no new customer-facing features outside the two flagged integrations ship this year. That line is what makes the document usable; it's the sentence a product manager points to when a stakeholder asks for a third integration in March.

5

One strategy, two audiences

The same mistake covered on ADRs & Roadmapping for the roadmap itself shows up one layer up, at the strategy document.

The board and executive version

Three to five priorities, each with the business outcome and the tradeoff stated in one sentence, no architecture detail, no technology names unless the technology itself is the competitive story. This is the version that goes in the board deck alongside the content on Board Reporting.

The engineering version

The same priorities, expanded with the technical approach, sequencing dependencies, and named owners. Engineers need to know how, not just why; a strategy document that stays at board-level abstraction throughout gives a team nothing to plan against.

Maintain one underlying document and generate both views from it, the same discipline as the roadmap split, rather than maintaining two documents that drift out of sync within a quarter.
6

Revisiting and sunsetting a strategy

A strategy document has a shelf life, and most organizations never explicitly declare when it's expired.

Practice

Revisit formally on the annual cadence described in The CTO Operating System, and out of cycle the moment a stated assumption breaks: a competitor ships the thing Horizon 3 was betting against, a key hire who owned a priority leaves, the market shifts underneath the plan. A strategy that hasn't been checked against reality in six months is being followed on faith, not evidence.

Common failure mode

Last year's strategy document quietly stays the reference point because writing a new one feels like admitting the old one failed. It didn't fail; circumstances changed, which is what strategies are supposed to respond to. An unrevised strategy is a bigger tell than a revised one.

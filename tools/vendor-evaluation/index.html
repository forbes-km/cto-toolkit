Vendor Evaluation

A preview of the Vendor Evaluation Scorecard: weighted, multi-evaluator scoring across functional fit, security posture, financial stability, integration complexity, support, and exit cost, built to produce a defensible recommendation rather than a gut call.

This page covers the scoring categories and the multi-evaluator mechanic, plus a working single-evaluator scorer below. The full weighted scorecard workbook, with multi-evaluator variance tracking, is available through direct engagement.
Mechanic

Why multi-evaluator, not single-decision-maker

The scorecard's real value isn't the criteria list, it's forcing the score itself to survive more than one perspective before it becomes a decision.

How it works

Each stakeholder category, technical, security, finance, the actual end users, scores the vendor independently against the same criteria before anyone compares notes. Independent scoring first prevents the most senior or most vocal evaluator's opinion from anchoring everyone else's.

Where it earns its keep

The interesting result usually isn't the average score, it's the variance. A vendor scored a 4 by security and a 9 by the business sponsor is a conversation the scorecard forces to happen explicitly, before a contract is signed, rather than as a surprise after go-live.

Framework

Weighted scoring criteria

The categories that belong in every vendor evaluation, regardless of what's being bought, with the weighting varying by how critical and how sticky the vendor relationship will be.

CategoryWhat to checkCommon mistake
Functional fitScore against your actual top ten requirements, not the vendor's feature listScoring against the demo instead of a trial with your own real data
Security & compliance postureCurrent SOC 2 or ISO certification, not an expired one; actual pen test summary if availableAccepting a security questionnaire response at face value with no artifact requested
Financial stabilityFunding stage or profitability, customer concentration risk, runway if it's an early-stage vendorSkipping this for anything that isn't a large, obviously stable vendor
Integration complexityActual API quality and documentation, tested against a real integration spike, not a sales engineer's assuranceEstimating integration effort from a slide deck instead of a technical spike
Support & SLAActual historical response time from reference customers, not the SLA document's stated targetTrusting the SLA document without checking whether it's ever enforced against the vendor

1

Reference calls that surface something

The single highest-value, most consistently skipped step in vendor evaluation.

What works

Asking the vendor for a reference in a similar situation to yours (similar scale, similar use case), then asking that reference one specific question: "what would you do differently if you were choosing again today." That question surfaces real friction far more reliably than "are you happy with them."

What backfires

Accepting only the references the vendor's sales team hand-picked, and asking only satisfaction questions that have an obvious socially expected positive answer. This step becomes a formality that confirms nothing.

2

The lock-in question, asked before signing

Exit cost is far easier to evaluate honestly before a contract exists than after switching costs have already accumulated.

Questions to answer now

What format can data be exported in, is there a contractual data portability clause, what's the actual notice period and any early termination penalty, and how much custom integration work exists that would need to be rebuilt for a different vendor.

The cost of skipping this

Discovering the actual exit cost during a renewal negotiation, at exactly the moment the vendor has the least incentive to make leaving easy and the most leverage to raise price, since they know switching now looks expensive.

3

Contract terms that matter beyond price

The number in the proposal is the easiest thing to negotiate. The clauses nobody reads carefully are usually what costs money later.

  • Auto-renewal terms. How much notice is required to opt out, and does the notice window open before or after the price for the next term is disclosed. A short window that closes before the new price is revealed is a structural risk to you regardless of the vendor's intent, and worth negotiating out of the contract either way.
  • Liability caps. What the vendor is on the hook for if their product causes a breach or an outage, often capped at fees paid, which can be a small fraction of the actual damage.
  • SLA penalty structure. Whether missing the SLA produces a real, automatic credit or requires the customer to notice, document, and formally claim it, which most SLAs quietly rely on nobody doing.
  • Most-favored-nation and price-lock clauses. Whether the contract protects against a price increase at renewal, and whether it's enforceable or just a stated intention.
4

Negotiation leverage, and when to use it

Leverage in a vendor negotiation is real but time-bound. Knowing when it exists changes what's worth pushing for.

Where leverage exists

The vendor's fiscal quarter-end or year-end, when a sales rep needs the deal to close on their timeline, not yours. A genuine competitive alternative already evaluated, not bluffed. A multi-year commitment offered in exchange for a real discount, since the vendor values the certainty more than the marginal revenue from staying month-to-month.

Where it doesn't

Threatening to walk with no real alternative lined up gets tested and lost quickly. A vendor negotiation team that's dealt with the bluff before will call it, and the next negotiation starts from a weaker position because the threat is no longer credible.

5

After signing: performance management, not filing it away

A vendor evaluation that stops at the signature misses the half of the relationship that determines whether the choice was right.

Practice

A standing quarterly business review for any vendor above a materiality threshold, tracking the same dimensions scored during evaluation, support responsiveness, uptime against the SLA, whether the integration is still fit for purpose. This is what turns the next renewal negotiation from a cold restart into a conversation grounded in a real track record.

Common failure mode

The scorecard gets built once, before signing, and never revisited. By renewal, eighteen months of drift in support quality or product direction is invisible, and the renewal decision gets made on inertia rather than on evidence, exactly the failure mode this whole page exists to prevent at the front end.

Transformation and Innovation

Two words that get used as synonyms for "change" and answer two different questions. Innovation asks what should exist that doesn't yet, a product, a process, a business model, and it's fundamentally a selection problem: generate enough real options, then kill the bad ones fast and cheap before they're expensive. Transformation asks how an entire organization starts operating differently, and it's fundamentally an adoption problem: the hard part was never deciding what to do, it's getting people and systems to do it differently and keep doing it after the initiative team moves on.

Most people who conflate the two are only doing one of them, usually innovation, a pilot, a proof of concept, and calling it transformation because the word sounds bigger.

Two different problems, one blurred word

The distinction that most conversations about "driving change" skip past.

InnovationTransformation
Core questionWhat should exist that doesn't yet?How does the whole organization work differently?
Fundamentally a problem ofSelection, generating enough real options and killing the weak ones earlyAdoption, getting people and systems to change behavior and keep it changed
Where it failsToo few real alternatives, or nothing ever gets killedThe kickoff gets funded, reinforcement six months later doesn't
Symptom when it's missingThe org executes reliably but keeps optimizing the same things the same wayThe org has good pilots that never make it past the flagship site
Innovation without transformation produces pilots that never scale. Transformation without innovation produces the same organization, just faster and more expensive to run. Neither failure looks like the other, which is why treating them as one problem usually means solving neither.
2

Diagnose which one you have

Before fixing anything, find out which problem is real, because the fixes don't transfer.

You have an innovation problem if

The org executes reliably, ships on schedule, runs a tight operation, and keeps optimizing the same processes the same way. Nobody's proposing anything different, or the ideas that do surface never get a real evaluation against a kill criterion. More change management won't fix this. The gap is upstream, not enough real options ever reach a decision.

You have a transformation problem if

The org has plenty of good ideas, pilots that work, proofs of concept that prove the concept, and none of them make it past the team or facility that built them. More AI pilots won't fix this. The gap is downstream, adoption, incentives, and the operating model never got built to carry a working idea past its origin point.

The innovation stack: generating and selecting

Three real, citable pieces, not an invented framework, for the selection problem.

ConceptWhat it doesCTO example
Jobs to Be Done
Clayton Christensen, Competing Against Luck, 2016		Before generating options, name the job the user is hiring a solution to do. Prevents building impressive things nobody needed.A platform team ships a slicker internal dashboard nobody asked for, while the job engineers were hiring the old one to do, tell me fast whether my deploy is safe, went unaddressed
Innovation Tournaments
Terwiesch & Ulrich, Innovation Tournaments, 2009		Treat innovation as a funnel: generate far more ideas than you need, then kill most of them fast and cheap, before they're expensive. The discipline is in the killing, not the generating. See Innovation Portfolio for how this runs as a standing quarterly mechanism.A quarterly open call where any engineer pitches against a fixed rubric, horizon fit, a kill criterion, a funding ask, judged by a panel outside the pitcher's own chain
Ten Types of Innovation
Keeley et al. (Doblin/Deloitte), 2013		Innovation isn't only new product features. It spans configuration (how you're organized, your business model), the offering itself, and experience (how people interact with it).A hospital IT org whose entire "innovation" pipeline is AI features misses that a new referral or scheduling workflow can be just as innovative, and often ships faster

The transformation stack: adoption at scale

Three real, citable pieces for the adoption problem, which is the one most technical leaders have the least formal training in.

ConceptWhat it doesCTO example
Kotter's 8-Step Model
John Kotter, Leading Change, 1996		The sequence: urgency, coalition, vision, communication, removing obstacles, short-term wins, consolidating gains, anchoring in culture. Most failed transformations skip straight to communicating the vision and never build the coalition or clear the obstacles.A platform migration gets a company-wide announcement and a town hall, but no coalition of respected engineers vouching for it inside each team, and adoption stalls exactly where the announcement didn't reach
Dual Operating System
John Kotter, Accelerate, 2014		You don't transform an organization by restructuring the org chart. Run a second, network-like structure alongside the hierarchy, specifically for the change effort, because the hierarchy is built for reliable operations, not for change.Staffing a transformation initiative with the same reporting lines and cadence as steady-state IT operations, then wondering why it moves at steady-state speed
ADKAR
Jeff Hiatt / Prosci, 2006		The individual mechanics of adoption: Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability, Reinforcement. Explains exactly where adoption dies, usually Desire or Reinforcement, rarely Awareness, since most initiatives over-invest in the kickoff announcement and under-invest in what happens six months later.A new clinical documentation tool gets a full rollout announcement (Awareness, done well) and training (Knowledge, done well), but no reinforcement once the project team disbands, so usage quietly reverts within two quarters
Awareness and Knowledge are the easy, visible parts of a rollout, an announcement and a training session. Desire and Reinforcement are the parts that determine whether the change survives contact with a busy person's existing habits, and they're the parts almost every initiative underfunds.

Checklist

Before you call it a transformation

Run this before staffing an initiative as a transformation. If most of these are unanswered, it's still an innovation effort, and that's fine, just don't fund or staff it like the bigger thing.

  • You can name the coalition, the specific people whose credibility will carry this past their own team, not just the executive sponsor who announced it
  • You have a reinforcement plan for six months after launch, not just a training plan for week one
  • The initiative is staffed and run differently from steady-state operations, not folded into the normal reporting cadence
  • You know which ADKAR stage past pilots have died at, and it's not still Awareness
  • You've distinguished whether the real gap is generating better options or getting existing good ones adopted, and you're not applying an innovation fix to an adoption problem or the reverse
Reference only. These frameworks describe patterns that show up consistently across innovation and transformation efforts; they don't guarantee any specific initiative succeeds.

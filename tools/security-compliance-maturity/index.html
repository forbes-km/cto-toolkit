Security & Compliance Maturity

A maturity ladder across the six NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 functions, Govern included, scored on evidence rather than stated policy, plus the common trap of mistaking a compliance certification for maturity.

A self-assessment framework. It does not replace an independent audit. Internal scoring is useful for prioritization; external validation is what an auditor, customer, or regulator will rely on.
Framework

Maturity ladder, mapped to NIST CSF 2.0 functions

Five levels across the six NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 functions. Score each function separately; most organizations are unevenly mature across them, which is itself useful information. Govern provides the organizational context and direction for the other five, added in CSF 2.0 because a technically mature program with no executive-level ownership or risk strategy is a common gap.

How to use this

Scoring on evidence, and what to do with the result

The assessment only has value if the scoring holds up, which is harder than it sounds when the person scoring it is also accountable for the result.

How to score it

Score against evidence rather than intention. "We have a policy" is evidence of intent, and it does not reach Level 3 unless the policy is followed. Ask for the artifact, the access review log, the tested restore record, the tabletop exercise notes, before accepting a self-reported level.

What to do with a low score

A Level 1 or 2 in any function is a prioritization input rather than an emergency in itself. Rank functions by business impact if that function fails, rather than by how uncomfortable the low score is, and fund the highest-impact gap first rather than the easiest one to fix.

Common pitfall

Compliance certification as a false signal of maturity

A frequent gap between what a certification confirms and what an organization assumes it confirms.

What a certification confirms

SOC 2 or ISO 27001 provides assurance over a defined scope and a defined audit period: that the specific controls in that scope existed and operated as described during that window. That's a narrower, bounded claim than "maturity" as this page uses the term. This ladder describes an ongoing, evolving posture across an entire function, whereas a certification is a point-in-time assurance statement about a control set the organization itself proposed for audit.

What it doesn't confirm

That the scope covered every system that matters, that the posture is sustained after the audit period ends rather than a snapshot assembled for it, or that a novel attack pattern outside the certified controls would be caught. Treat certification as evidence for one point on the ladder; the rest of it still has to be scored.

Why NIST CSF functions as a crosswalk

The practical reason to run this assessment at all, beyond the framework being well known.

What it enables

Many regulatory frameworks, industry standards, and customer security questionnaires reference or heavily overlap with CSF 2.0's six functions, Govern especially, since regulators and procurement teams increasingly ask the governance question directly: who owns this, and how is it reported to leadership. A single NIST CSF assessment gives a reasonable starting map for gap analysis against HIPAA, PCI DSS, or a customer security questionnaire, without redoing the assessment from scratch for each one.

Where the crosswalk breaks down

A healthcare or payments-specific regulation has requirements that don't map cleanly onto any single CSF function, a specific breach notification timeline, a specific cardholder data handling rule. The crosswalk gets you most of the way; the remaining gap has to be checked directly against the specific regulation's text.

How this differs from Zero-Trust Maturity

Two maturity pages that look similar in structure but answer different questions, worth running both rather than picking one.

This page scores a broad governance and lifecycle posture using a widely recognized standard, useful for board conversations, audit readiness, and mapping against specific regulatory obligations. Zero-Trust Maturity scores a specific architectural posture across six dimensions purpose-built around identity, segmentation, and blast radius, useful for engineering and security architecture decisions. The two should tell different stories about the same organization rather than duplicate the same story in different language. A team scoring well on this page's Protect function can still score poorly on zero-trust segmentation, and that gap is exactly the kind of thing worth knowing rather than averaging away.

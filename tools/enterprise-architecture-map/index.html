Enterprise Architecture

Capability modeling at a usable altitude, the three common EA operating models and where each breaks down, and how to run architecture governance without it becoming a bottleneck teams route around.

Capability model examples and operating model tradeoffs are illustrative starting points. The right granularity and structure depend heavily on organization size and industry; adapt rather than adopt directly.
Framework

Capability modeling, three levels deep

The foundation everything else in enterprise architecture sits on. If the capability model is wrong or too granular to be useful, nothing built on top of it holds up.

LevelDefinitionExample
L0: Business capabilityWhat the business does, independent of how, expressed as a noun phrase, not a processCustomer Onboarding
L1: Sub-capabilityThe distinct components a capability breaks into, still technology-agnosticIdentity Verification, Account Provisioning, Welcome Communication
L2: Capability-to-system mappingWhich actual systems currently deliver each sub-capability, where the model becomes actionableIdentity Verification delivered by a specific KYC vendor plus an internal rules engine
1

Why most capability models fail

Not from lack of effort. From being built at the wrong altitude or never maintained after the initial workshop.

The failure

A capability map with 200 or more entries, built once in a two-day workshop, stored in a document nobody opens again. Too granular to reason about at a leadership level, too static to reflect a system landscape that changes monthly.

What works instead

Roughly 20 to 40 L0/L1 capabilities, reviewed and updated quarterly as part of an existing planning cadence rather than as a standalone exercise, with the L2 system mapping treated as the layer that changes most often and the L0/L1 layer treated as comparatively stable.

2

EA operating model

How the architecture function itself is structured relative to the engineering organization it serves.

Centralized

A single EA team owns architecture decisions and review across the company. Strong consistency, but becomes a bottleneck and loses touch with implementation detail as the engineering org scales past what one team can stay close to.

Federated / embedded

Architects sit inside or alongside product engineering teams, with a lightweight central function for cross-cutting standards and review. Faster decisions, closer to implementation reality, but consistency requires deliberate effort (a shared decision log, a standards forum) rather than happening automatically.

Hub-and-spoke, the federated model with a genuinely lightweight hub, is the most common landing point for organizations past roughly 200 engineers. Purely centralized rarely survives that scale; purely embedded without any hub tends to drift into inconsistent standards within 12 to 18 months.

Architecture governance without becoming a bottleneck

The tension every EA operating model has to resolve: enough oversight to catch real risk, not so much that teams route around it.

What works

A lightweight architecture review triggered by objective criteria (a new external dependency, a new data store, anything touching regulated data, anything with a five-figure or larger ongoing cost), not by an architect's subjective judgment call applied inconsistently across teams.

What backfires

Requiring review for every change regardless of size or risk. This is the same failure mode as the HITL queue-overload problem on the Architecture Map: when the gate catches everything, reviewers start rubber-stamping, and the review stops catching anything that actually matters.

Reference only. Adapt capability granularity and operating model to your organization's actual size and structure rather than applying this as a template.

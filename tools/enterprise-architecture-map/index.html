Enterprise Architecture

What EA is and isn't, how it differs from domain and solution architecture, the structural rules a capability model has to follow to stay useful, the six-dimension operating model canvas, how the EA function itself should run, and the five ways EA functions fail.

Business Capability Model, Operating Model Canvas, EA Operating Model, Architecture Review Operating Model, Architecture Principles Library, and EA Maturity Assessment are all real, field-tested workbooks with full facilitation guides, templates, and worked examples available through direct engagement. This page previews the reasoning and structure of each, not the complete deliverable.

1

What enterprise architecture is

The discipline of shaping how an organization operates: translating strategy into the capabilities, processes, information, and technology required to execute it, and governing how those elements evolve together.

IS

The unit of analysis is the enterprise as an operating system. Business capabilities define what the organization does; the operating model defines how those capabilities are delivered; technology exists to support that operating model. When EA works, technology decisions follow from capability decisions, and capability decisions follow from strategy.

IS NOT

  • A documentation function; the goal is future-state decisions, not a current-state inventory
  • A technology review board; approving individual selections is narrow governance, not the discipline itself
  • A framework exercise; TOGAF and Zachman are vocabularies, not the work
  • A long-range planning department; EA is distinct in owning the linkage between strategic intent and operational reality across domains

Enterprise, domain, and solution architecture

Three disciplines answering different questions at different scopes. Work done at the wrong scope either stalls (too broad for the problem) or creates debt (too narrow for the implication).

ENTERPRISE

DOMAIN

SOLUTION

Scope

The organization as a whole

A specific capability or technology domain

Primary question

How should the organization operate and evolve to deliver on strategy?

When the handoffs between the three work, the organization delivers faster with less rework. When they break, EA loses credibility and solution architects reinvent foundational decisions on every project.

Business Capability Model: the structural rules

A hierarchical, business-led view of what the organization does, independent of process or technology. Violating these rules loses credibility quickly; they are not style preferences.

THE RULES

  • Capabilities are nouns, not verbs: Customer Onboarding, not Onboard Customers. Verb phrases slide toward process, which varies by business unit and time.
  • MECE at each level: child capabilities are mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive under their parent
  • Three levels is usually enough, four is the ceiling; Level 1 is 8 to 12 capabilities, Level 2 decomposes each into 3 to 8
  • The business owns the model; EA facilitates. A model that can't survive a conversation with the COO is an IT artifact with capability labels, not a capability model

COMMON FAILURE MODES

  • The model that's really an org chart, and breaks the next time the org restructures
  • The model that reads like a process map, verb phrases and sequential flow
  • The model nobody owns, drifting from reality within two reorganizations
  • The model used only for IT planning, never referenced in a business investment conversation

Method note: a defensible Level 1 and Level 2 model takes six to eight weeks for an organization of moderate complexity, most of it workshop time with business owners, not desk research.

Operating Model Canvas: six dimensions

A one-page view of how a capability is delivered. Makes an operating model that's normally invisible, living in heads and habits, explicit enough to reason about in one conversation.

DIMENSION

QUESTION

Accountability

Who is accountable for the outcomes this capability produces? Not activity, a small number of named roles.

Workflow

How does work flow from initiation to completion? Main path and significant variants.

Decision rights

What decisions get made here, and who makes them? The dimension most often left underspecified.

Information

What does the capability consume, produce, and rely on? Where is the authoritative source?

Technology

What systems enable the capability? Where are the load-bearing manual workarounds?

Locations

Where is work performed, by whom, under what arrangement? Internal vs. external, geographic distribution.

Five diagnostic patterns show up repeatedly once a canvas is complete: accountability-workflow mismatch (the accountable role doesn't control the critical path), decision rights vacuum (significant decisions have no clear owner), information authority ambiguity (two systems both claim to be the source of truth), technology-workflow inversion (process steps exist because the system requires them, not because they add value), and location-decision rights mismatch (decisions made where the work can't be observed).

What the EA function provides, and what it deliberately doesn't

Naming the boundary explicitly is what keeps a function from diluting its capacity trying to do everything asked of it.

PROVIDES

  • Decision support on significant technology choices (platform selection, build-vs-buy)
  • Target-state design for material, multi-team initiatives
  • Standards and reference material
  • Architecture review
  • Capability and portfolio guidance
  • Escalation target for cross-team architectural disagreements

DOES NOT PROVIDE

  • Operational firefighting; production incidents go to the teams that own the affected systems
  • Individual engineering decisions within an established architecture
  • Delivery or program management
  • Resolution of organizational or political problems dressed as technology questions

The test for any EA activity: name a specific decision it will improve, and how. If no decision can be named, the activity probably doesn't belong in the function.

6

Roles and capacity allocation

A rough guide for full-time architects. The specific percentages matter less than the discipline of allocating deliberately rather than letting whatever's loudest consume all of it.

ROLES

  • Head of EA: owns the operating model and the CIO/CTO relationship; still practices architecture, doesn't just lead
  • Principal Architects: own the most significant decisions; scarce, and the function's effectiveness tracks how well their time is protected
  • Domain Architects: the authoritative voice within a specific domain (data, security, integration)
  • Solution Architects: the practical interface between EA and delivery teams

CAPACITY, STEADY STATE

  • 30 to 40 percent: direct engagements
  • 20 to 25 percent: review and consultation
  • 15 to 20 percent: function-level work (standards, reference material)
  • 10 to 15 percent: learning and development
  • 10 to 15 percent: organizational overhead

Architects at 100 percent direct engagement and 0 percent function-level work means the function is cannibalizing its own future. The reverse means it isn't producing value today. Neither is sustainable.

7

Demand management

The single most common EA failure mode is unmanaged demand: every ask accepted, capacity consumed by whoever escalates loudest. A single intake channel with real triage is the fix.

TRIAGE OUTCOMES

  • Accept: within scope, staffed, given a priority and expected timeline
  • Redirect: not EA scope, pointed to the right function with a warm handoff
  • Decline: genuinely out of scope or not worth the time now, with a stated reason

PRIORITIZATION CRITERIA

  • Strategic significance
  • Reversibility, harder-to-reverse ranks higher
  • Timing criticality, is there a real window for input
  • Sponsor engagement
  • Blast radius

Notably absent from the criteria: who is asking. Prioritizing by requester seniority produces capture by the loudest voices, not the most valuable work.

Architecture review: four touchpoints

Engagement, not approval. Five short conversations during design beat one long review at the end, and the default posture is collaborative, not gatekeeping.

TOUCHPOINT

TIMING

PURPOSE

Early consult

Pre-scoping, before funding

Orientation and shared context; no formal decision

Design review

After initial design, before deep build

Pressure-test the design, surface risks, name decisions and owners

Readiness check

Before production deployment

Confirm design-to-build integrity, not a generic approval

Post-deployment

Quarterly or event-driven

Feedback loop; extract patterns for the catalog

Required engagement triggers on objective criteria: a new enterprise-shared capability, a new technology category, a standards exception request, spend above a defined threshold, or regulated data beyond ordinary scope. Small, in-pattern initiatives need no engagement at all; asking permission to use an already-reviewed pattern isn't governance, it's friction.

9

How EA functions fail

Five well-known patterns, worth naming so they can be watched for rather than discovered after the fact.

THE PATTERNS

  • Ivory tower: artifacts nobody uses; the function is respected in form, irrelevant in practice
  • Governance theater: review happens, but decisions get made elsewhere anyway
  • Engineering-by-architects: architects doing detailed engineering work while actual architecture goes undone
  • Unmanaged demand: every request accepted, best work crowded out by the urgent
  • Credibility erosion: recommendations ignored, decisions made without EA hearing about them until after

THE HONEST TEST

The function that works isn't the one with the cleanest org chart or the most complete artifact library. It's the one whose consumers, business leaders, delivery teams, engineering leadership, would actively resist losing. A function that can name five leaders who'd fight to keep it is doing well; one that can't should treat the absence as actionable information.

Reference only. Adapt capability granularity, operating model, and review thresholds to your organization's actual size and structure rather than applying this as a template.

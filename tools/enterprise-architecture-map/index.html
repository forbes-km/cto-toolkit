What enterprise architecture is

The discipline of shaping how an organization operates: translating strategy into the capabilities, processes, information, and technology required to execute it, and governing how those elements evolve together.

Is

The unit of analysis is the enterprise as an operating system. Business capabilities define what the organization does; the operating model defines how those capabilities are delivered; technology exists to support that operating model. When EA works, technology decisions follow from capability decisions, and capability decisions follow from strategy.

Is not
  • A documentation function; the goal is future-state decisions, and a current-state inventory is at most an input to them
  • A technology review board; approving individual selections is narrow governance and a small slice of the discipline
  • A framework exercise; TOGAF and Zachman are vocabularies for the work rather than the work itself
  • A tool implementation; choosing and populating a repository supports the work but will not, on its own, produce a better decision
  • A long-range planning department; EA is distinct in owning the linkage between strategic intent and operational reality across domains
Every EA output is judged by what it changes. A capability model earns its place when it moves an investment decision, a reference architecture when variance across teams falls, and a roadmap when the organization can actually absorb the sequence it proposes.

Enterprise, domain, and solution architecture

Three disciplines answering different questions at different scopes. Work done at the wrong scope either stalls (too broad for the problem) or creates debt (too narrow for the implication).

EnterpriseDomainSolution
ScopeThe organization as a wholeA specific capability or technology domainA single initiative, product, or system
Primary questionHow should the organization operate and evolve to deliver on strategy?How should this domain be structured and advanced over time?How do we design and deliver this specific solution?
Time horizonThree to five years, rollingOne to three yearsWeeks to months
Typical outputsCapability model, operating model, target state, investment roadmap, governanceReference architecture, standards, patterns, domain roadmapSolution design, implementation plan, technical specifications
Value shows up whenCross-domain investments compound and the operating model keeps pace with strategyVariance across solutions falls and the domain becomes predictable to consumeThe system ships on scope, schedule, and quality
Failure modeDrifts into abstraction, loses connection to deliveryBecomes a rubber-stamp review function rather than a design disciplineOptimizes locally at the expense of the broader portfolio
Treating all architecture as one thing fails in three predictable ways. Enterprise trade-offs get delegated to solution architects, so each team optimizes its own delivery and the portfolio fills with overlapping capabilities nobody chose on purpose. Domain architecture, with no enterprise view to anchor it, follows whichever project is loudest and writes standards after the decisions are made. And solution teams end up reasoning from first principles on every initiative, carrying risk a working architecture function should have taken off them.

Business Capability Model: the structural rules

A hierarchical, business-led view of what the organization does, independent of process or technology. Violating these rules loses credibility quickly; treat them as rules rather than style preferences.

The rules
  • Capabilities are nouns: Customer Onboarding rather than Onboard Customers. Verb phrases slide toward process, which varies by business unit and time.
  • MECE (mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive) at each level: no capability overlaps with its siblings, and together they cover everything the parent capability includes, with nothing left out
  • Three levels is usually enough, four is the ceiling; Level 1 is 8 to 12 capabilities, Level 2 decomposes each into 3 to 8
  • The business owns the model; EA facilitates. A model that can't survive a conversation with the COO is an IT artifact with capability labels
Common failure modes
  • The model that's really an org chart, and breaks the next time the org restructures
  • The model that reads like a process map, verb phrases and sequential flow
  • The model nobody owns, drifting from reality within two reorganizations
  • The model used only for IT planning, never referenced in a business investment conversation
A capability also describes what, never how, where, or who. Customer Identity Verification is a capability; verifying identity through a third-party service at the call center is an implementation that belongs in another view and traces back to it.

Building the capability model, and putting it to work

A defensible Level 1 and Level 2 takes six to eight weeks for an organization of moderate complexity. Larger or multi-segment enterprises take longer, and the extra time goes to validation, not construction.

Reference structure, three families
FamilyLevel 1 starting points
Customer-facing: what customers experience directlyMarket Development, Customer Acquisition, Customer Engagement, Product and Service Delivery, Customer Support
Operational: running the core businessProduct Management, Supply and Sourcing, Operations, Risk and Compliance
Enabling: keeping the organization itself runningFinance and Treasury, Human Capital, Information and Technology, Corporate Services

Adapt it:

  • B2B: Customer Acquisition decomposes further than Market Development
  • Regulated industries: Risk and Compliance grows at Level 2 and often needs Level 3
  • Asset-heavy businesses: Operations and Supply and Sourcing carry the weight
  • Platforms and marketplaces: Product Management and Operations effectively merge at Level 1
Level 2 example: Customer Engagement
  • Channel Management: which channels exist and how they relate
  • Interaction Handling: receiving, routing, and responding within service levels
  • Customer Identity and Profile: one coherent view of the customer
  • Communication and Outreach: transactional, relationship, and campaign contact
  • Self-Service Enablement: customers reaching outcomes without staff
  • Engagement Analytics: measuring whether the five above are working

A reference pattern, not an answer. The test is whether every activity under the parent lands in exactly one child.

WhenWorkThe rule that keeps it honest
Week 1Gather strategy documents and prior architecture work; name the sponsor and a working group with a senior business leader per segment; write a one-paragraph charterArchitects facilitate; they do not vote
Week 2Adapt a reference structure in a half-day workshop until Level 1 reads like this organizationNever attempt Level 2 in the same session
Weeks 3 to 4One two-to-three hour workshop per Level 1 area with its business ownersRun the MECE test at the end of each session; failures go to a parking list, and overlaps are resolved at the boundary
Weeks 5 to 6Walk the draft through each owner, then the sponsor; publish with a version and a named ownerChanges at this stage are language, not structure
Week 7 onwardPut it to work immediately: heat-map current pain by capability, map the top ten in-flight initiatives, map the application portfolio at Level 2 or 3Plan these three uses before publishing; an unused model loses credibility within a quarter
Maintenance is light once built: confirm Levels 1 and 2 with the sponsor annually (most years nothing changes), review proposed changes quarterly against the MECE test, and keep traceability from initiatives, applications, and data current continuously. That last part is where the model earns its keep. And if there is no executive who will cite the model in their own communication, no business owner willing to be named, and no appetite for capability-anchored funding conversations, do not build it yet. Build those conditions first, or it becomes a wall poster.

Value streams: the flow the capability map leaves out

A capability map deliberately has no order. A value stream supplies it: the stages by which an organization delivers value to a stakeholder, each one enabled by capabilities from the map. Used together, they show where a customer feels a weak capability, and how often.

Capability mapValue stream
AnswersWhat does the organization do?How does value reach a stakeholder, stage by stage?
ShapeA hierarchy with no order and no flowAn ordered set of stages, triggered by a stakeholder and ending in value they recognize
StabilityStable across strategy cyclesStable too; the processes underneath each stage change far more often
Best forInvestment, ownership, duplication, and portfolio mappingCustomer experience, bottlenecks, and scoping a transformation
NotAn org chart or a process mapA process map: no swim lanes, no systems, no handoff detail
How to build one
  • Name the stream for the value delivered from the stakeholder's side, such as Onboard a Customer or Resolve a Claim, not internal shorthand like quote to cash
  • Keep stages few and ordered: five to eight, each ending in a recognizable state change for the thing moving through
  • Cross-map capabilities to stages. When a stage underperforms, the map tells you which capabilities to look at; a capability that enables many stages is load-bearing
  • Model a handful, not all of them. The three to five streams that carry the strategy are enough; nobody maintains fifty
Common misuses
  • Drawing a process map and calling it a value stream
  • Naming stages after departments, which turns the value stream back into an org chart
  • Building value streams with no capability cross-map, so they never connect to investment
  • Confusing it with lean value stream mapping, a related but different technique for measuring flow and waste in a specific process
The cross-map is the payoff. Business architecture practice treats each value stream as one value proposition for one stakeholder, with capabilities mapped to the stages they enable, so a stage that falters points straight at the capabilities behind it[1]. The diagram uses the same shading as the heat map tool below: a weak capability used in three stages is a better investment target than a weaker one used in none.

Capability heat map

The first use this page recommends for a new capability model: rate each capability for how much it matters to the strategy and how well it performs today. The tool colors the map by where attention is needed, ranks the biggest gaps, and, if you add run cost, shows whether money is going where the strategy needs it. It starts with a sample organization; replace the rows with your own.

How it scores: attention = importance x (5 minus performance), so a critical capability performing poorly scores 16 to 20 and anything performing at 5 scores zero. Bands: 1 to 5 low, 6 to 10 medium, 11 and above high. Importance and performance of 4 or more count as high. Rate performance from evidence where you have it: incident counts, cycle times, customer complaints, cost per transaction. Ratings from a single workshop drift toward the room's mood.

Operating Model Canvas: six dimensions

A one-page view of how a capability is delivered. Makes an operating model that's normally invisible, living in heads and habits, explicit enough to reason about in one conversation.

DimensionQuestion
AccountabilityWho is accountable for the outcomes this capability produces? A small number of named roles, tied to outcomes rather than activity.
WorkflowHow does work flow from initiation to completion? Main path and significant variants.
Decision rightsWhat decisions get made here, and who makes them? The dimension most often left underspecified.
InformationWhat does the capability consume, produce, and rely on? Where is the authoritative source?
TechnologyWhat systems enable the capability? Where are the load-bearing manual workarounds?
LocationsWhere is work performed, by whom, under what arrangement? Internal vs. external, geographic distribution.
Five diagnostic patterns show up repeatedly once a canvas is complete: accountability-workflow mismatch (the accountable role doesn't control the critical path), decision rights vacuum (significant decisions have no clear owner), information authority ambiguity (two systems both claim to be the source of truth), technology-workflow inversion (process steps exist because the system requires them rather than because they add value), and location-decision rights mismatch (decisions made where the work can't be observed).

Running a canvas, and the four ways it goes wrong

Leaders with line accountability plus a few practitioners who do the work, two sessions, architecture facilitating. The canvas sits on top of process maps, RACI, and the application portfolio; it summarizes them and points to them.

Use it
  • Before a transformation, to baseline how work really happens
  • After an acquisition, one canvas per side for each capability being combined
  • Before centralizing, decentralizing, or creating shared services
  • As the unit of design in a capability-led target state
Skip it
  • For a capability no one has ever described, unless you are ready to surface that no two leaders agree on it
  • As a substitute for process or data modeling when depth is needed
  • To grade an operating model with nothing to compare it against
SessionWhat happens
Session 1, 90 minutes: draftAgree the boundary first; thirty minutes on scope saves two hours of dispute. Work the six dimensions in order. Everyone writes before anyone debates, so disagreement surfaces instead of deferring to whoever speaks first. Close by naming the three biggest disagreements and who resolves each before session 2.
Session 2, 90 minutes: stress-testClarifications only, no structural rewrites. Then three tests: trace the last real exception through the canvas; take the last real change and check which dimensions moved; describe a plausible future change and see whether the canvas predicts what would have to move. Publish with the working group named, a version, and a review date.
The four errors: a wishful current state that describes how the capability should work and fails the stress tests; overloading, where any dimension with more than five to seven elements means the scope is too broad; boxes and arrows standing in for statements, when a finished canvas should read aloud as sensible prose; and no named owner, so it goes stale within a year.

The target-state narrative: prose first, diagrams second

Executives read a target-state diagram for ten seconds, ask two questions, and move on. A narrative that stands on its own survives the meeting and becomes the reference the roadmap and investment case point back to. Four to eight pages for a capability cluster, eight to fifteen for the whole enterprise; past that, nobody reads it.

SectionLengthWhat it has to do
1. Executive summaryOne pageThree paragraphs: the target in terms the executives already use, why this target over the alternatives, and the shape of the journey. If they read nothing else, the message has landed.
2. The target stateTwo to four pagesFive dimensions and no more: what the organization does, how work is organized, how information is used, how technology serves the business, and how the organization changes. No architecture diagrams here.
3. Why this targetOne to two pagesWhat the strategy needs that today cannot deliver, what external forces make today untenable, what the target opens up. Specific beats generic: resolving a request in one interaction sticks; becoming more agile does not.
4. Trade-offs, and what we are not choosingOne pageThe directions set aside and why, what the organization gives up, and the risks stated plainly. Sophisticated readers notice when this is missing and discount everything else.
5. What changes, for whomOne to two pagesCustomers, business teams, technology teams, and leadership. The leadership paragraph is the one most often skipped, and the target operating model always implies new leadership behavior.
6. How we get thereOne pagePhases and their character, what has started, the decisions still open, and the commitment required. The detailed plan lives in the roadmap.
Writing rules
  • Written by one or two named people, then validated; committees write prose designed to avoid objections
  • Present tense, as if the target already exists; future tense signals the author is not committed
  • Active voice that names who does what
  • Delete adjectives that carry no information and any paragraph that could describe a different company
  • The opposite test: if the opposite of a claim is also a position a sensible organization might take, the claim says something; if the opposite is absurd, it is a platitude
Common errors
  • A diagram first, with prose explaining it
  • A technology stack presented as a target state
  • Trade-offs missing or softened into positives
  • Ten dimensions instead of five
  • No named author, so nobody owns the position
A narrative is harder to write than a deck and more durable. Use slides in the steering committee if that is the format; write the narrative anyway, because it forces the thinking to be complete in a way slides let you skip.

The capability-driven roadmap

Most roadmaps are lists of technology initiatives with no anchor in the outcomes they serve, so when priorities shift they are either continued out of inertia or cancelled with nothing in their place. Anchoring on capability gaps lets the instruments change while the objective holds. Eight to twelve weeks for a mid-sized organization, twelve to sixteen for a large one.

StepOutputWhere it goes wrong
1. Decompose strategyFive to seven priorities, each with a measurable outcome and the two to five capability areas it depends onMore than ten priorities means the strategy is unfocused, and the roadmap will inherit that
2. Analyze capability gapsA gap register: current state, required state, the gap in capability terms (speed, scale, fidelity, coverage, consistency, cost), and why it is hard to closeSkipped in favor of jumping to initiatives; this step is the method
3. Identify initiativesEach initiative keyed to a gap, with a sponsor, provisional scope, and rough sizeInitiatives that close no gap stay in; operating model changes left out because no system moves
4. SequenceYear 1 by quarter, years 2 and 3 by half-yearPolitical ordering instead of dependency ordering
5. Narrate and traceA business-language narrative plus a traceability view from priority to gap to initiative to deliverableTraceability treated as decoration rather than the thing consulted when replanning
6. Validate and approveAn approved roadmap with a named owner and a review cadenceRestructuring at this stage means steps 1 to 3 were rushed
Sequencing drivers, in order
  • Dependencies first. Political pressure does not move a dependent initiative ahead of its prerequisite.
  • Capacity second. Three concurrent major initiatives in one capability area is a plan to fail.
  • Strategic timing third. If a priority needs a capability by a date and the work cannot finish by then, the priority or the scope changes. The roadmap is where that truth surfaces.
  • Early wins last. Some items move earlier than dependencies require, deliberately, to build sponsor confidence.
Common errors
  • Initiatives first, capabilities retrofitted afterward
  • Built by IT and presented to the business for endorsement
  • Every gap attacked in year one
  • Sequence presented without the reasoning, so nobody can replan it
  • Published and never revisited; review quarterly in year one, twice a year after
  • Year 3 fantasy: specific projects three years out, which is false confidence
Four prerequisites, or the roadmap rests on sand: a written strategy with clear priorities, a capability model at Level 2 or better, an honest view of the current state, and business sponsors who own their gaps. Some gaps close through operating model change alone, such as moving a decision right, and those are legitimate roadmap entries. Some have no good initiative yet, and the right year-one item is discovery.

Portfolio and modernization calls: where EA turns a model into a decision

The capability model says what the organization does; the portfolio says what it runs to do it. EA is the function that keeps the two joined, so that disposition decisions are made per capability and not per system.

TIME quadrantValue and conditionUsual dispositionsWhat EA checks before agreeing
InvestHigh value, poor conditionRefactor, rearchitect, occasionally rebuildThat the capability is differentiating; rebuilding a commodity is how budgets disappear
TolerateHigh value, good conditionRetain, rehost, light refactorThat Retain carries an expiry date; without one it quietly becomes Ignore
MigrateLow value, good conditionReplace with SaaS, consolidate, rehostThat duplicates are grouped by capability rather than by technology
EliminateLow value, poor conditionRetire, with data archived to its retention ruleThat decommissioning is funded and counted as part of done
Scoring on two axes
  • Business value, five factors scored 1 to 5 and averaged: strategic importance, criticality, revenue or mission exposure, reach, and how often the business needs it changed
  • Technical condition, five factors: currency, operational health, ease of safe change, dependency footprint, and fit with the target platform
  • Documented overrides that can move an application off its quadrant: a regulatory constraint, a dependency lock, vendor end of life, talent availability, and a recent sunk commitment
  • Interview the business owner, application owner, and operations and security leads, then calibrate scores in batches of about twenty so they do not drift
Red flags in a disposition plan
  • Zero retirements. In portfolios of 60 or more applications, 10 to 20 percent usually retire. None means discovery failed.
  • Rearchitect on more than 10 to 15 percent. Enthusiasm outrunning capacity.
  • Rehost by default. Nobody made a decision, so the cheapest verb won.
  • Scoring theater. Scores assembled to justify calls already made.
  • Migration and modernization on one deadline. Two risks compounded into one date.
  • A new request 70 percent covered by an existing application. Extend the existing one; require three-year TCO and an exit plan for anything new.
Vocabulary: TIME is Gartner's portfolio model; the disposition verbs follow the "R" taxonomy, which AWS now publishes as seven strategies: retire, retain, rehost, relocate, repurchase, replatform, and refactor or re-architect[2]. The percentage ranges are practitioner heuristics from portfolio work, not industry benchmarks; treat them as a smell test. Scoring and the simulator live on Application Portfolio, execution mechanics on Modernization Execution.

Assessing an architecture you inherited

New CTOs, new heads of EA, and acquirers all face the same question: what is this architecture actually like, and what does it prevent? Diagrams describe intent. These tests find out what is true.

How to test it
  • Trace, don't read. Ask three engineers to each trace one real request end to end. Where their traces disagree with each other or with the diagram, that gap is the first finding.
  • Ask what each constraint prevents. For every load-bearing design decision, name the business move it blocks: a new market, a product line, an acquisition, a pricing model. A constraint that blocks nothing on the plan is trivia.
  • Assess against the next inflection. The question is whether the architecture survives the next step in scale or scope, not whether it handles today.
  • Two sources for serious findings. A material finding backed by one interview or one document gets downgraded until something independent confirms it.
Patterns that recur
  • A target state that has been in flight for three years is a finding, not a plan
  • Architecture style is never the finding by itself; a well-run monolith beats badly bounded microservices every time
  • Founder-era platform exhaustion: the design that got the company here cannot carry the next stage
  • A successful product on a struggling platform, with delivery slowing each quarter
  • Acquired and never integrated: roll-ups running several of everything
  • Cloud cost climbing faster than usage, which is often architecture debt showing up in the bill first
Once the findings are in, price them: carry cost, risk exposure, and remediation cost per item, on Technical Debt Economics. The acquisition version of this assessment is on Technology Diligence.

EA in mergers and acquisitions

Diligence prices the risk and the integration office runs the program. EA contributes the thing neither does well on its own: a capability-level view of what the combined organization should look like and which systems get there by which path.

What EA contributes
  • Overlay the two capability models. Duplication shows up by capability, which is where synergy actually lives; grouping by technology finds overlap that does not matter and misses overlap that does.
  • Compare operating model canvases for each capability being combined. Mismatched decision rights and information authority predict where integration will hurt long before a system is touched.
  • Set posture per capability, not per deal. Identity usually merges early because everything else depends on it; developer tooling merges quickly; ERP and HR run in parallel for a year or more; customer-facing platforms never go on a fast timeline.
  • Deliver the day-90 package: a verified inventory, a re-validated deal thesis, and an integration architecture recommendation. Execution starts after that, not before.
Integration posture, per capability
PostureWhat it meansCostTypical timelineRisk
MergeOne system survives and the other retiresHigh12 to 24 months or longerHigh
IntegrateBoth run, connected through APIs and shared dataMedium3 to 9 monthsMedium
CoexistBoth run independently, with reporting joined where neededLowImmediateLow

Integrate is the most common right answer, and Coexist is underused. Choosing Merge for everything is how six-month integration promises turn into two-year programs.

The postures line up with the classic acquisition integration typology of Haspeslagh and Jemison: Merge corresponds to absorption, Integrate to symbiosis, and Coexist to preservation[3]. Deal-side detail lives on Technology M&A Integration, Technology Diligence, and the M&A Diligence Kit.

Standing up or rebooting an EA function

The rest of this page describes a function that is running. Getting there is its own problem, and the right size depends on the organization's shape, not on a model org chart. The headcount bands below are rough practitioner guidance; the signals in the right-hand column matter more than the numbers.

Organization shapeWho does enterprise architectureThe signal that it is time for more
One product, under about 50 engineersThe CTO or a principal engineer, as part of the job. Significant decisions recorded as ADRsThe same platform decision gets made twice by different teams
About 50 to 200 engineers, or a second product lineOne enterprise or principal architect with a small council of senior engineers; review only on the triggers in section 19Teams choose overlapping platforms, or large purchases happen without anyone holding the whole picture
Several business units, 200 or more engineers, regulated data, or an acquisition programA head of EA plus domain architects where the risk sits, usually data, security, and integration; intake and review tiersNobody can say how many applications you run or which ones do the same job
Large enterprise or serial acquirerThe full function described on this page, including business architectureDuplicated capability measured in tens of millions a year

The first hire is a practicing architect who can hold a room with business leaders and has made real platform decisions, not a framework specialist or a tool administrator. Tools and frameworks can come later; a function whose first months are spent adopting TOGAF or populating a repository has usually lost the business before it produces a decision.

DaysDeliverWhy it comes first
1 to 30Interview 15 to 20 leaders across business and technology. List the significant decisions due in the next two quarters and who is making them. Draft a Level 1 capability map. Close with a two-page read-out of what you heard, including what already worksCredibility starts with evidence of listening. The decision list shows where EA can help soonest
31 to 60Support one visible, in-flight decision end to end and record it as an ADR. Open a single intake channel with weekly triage. Publish the Level 1 model with a named business ownerOne decision visibly improved is worth more than any library of standards
61 to 90Publish five to seven principles drawn from real decisions rather than a template. Set review triggers so small, in-pattern work needs no review. Run the first capability heat map with the owners. Report what EA did, what changed, and what it will not doScope is set by what the function declines as much as by what it takes on
Rebooting one that is struggling
  • Diagnose first. Name which of the five failure patterns in section 20 applies; the cure differs for each
  • Announce what stops. Retire the lowest-value reviews, documents, and meetings first, publicly, so the change is visible before anything new is asked of anyone
  • Keep what works. Most functions in trouble still do one or two things teams value; protect those
  • Earn authority back case by case. A mandate restores attendance, not trust, and usually deepens the erosion
What not to do in the first 90 days
  • Starting with framework adoption, a repository purchase, or a standards catalog
  • Reviewing everything to prove relevance, which turns EA into a queue in its first month
  • Promising a complete current-state inventory before any decision is supported
  • Hiring a team before the first architect has shown what the function is for
If you are a new CTO rather than a new head of EA, the first 30 days above fold into your own first 90; see Starting as CTO. Score where the function stands at the end of the 90 days on EA Maturity Assessment.

What the EA function provides, and what it deliberately doesn't

Naming the boundary explicitly is what keeps a function from diluting its capacity trying to do everything asked of it.

Provides
  • Decision support on significant technology choices (platform selection, build-vs-buy)
  • Target-state design for material, multi-team initiatives
  • Standards and reference material
  • Architecture review
  • Capability and portfolio guidance
  • Escalation target for cross-team architectural disagreements
Does not provide
  • Operational firefighting; production incidents go to the teams that own the affected systems
  • Individual engineering decisions within an established architecture
  • Delivery or program management
  • Resolution of organizational or political problems dressed as technology questions
The test for any EA activity: name a specific decision it will improve, and how. If no decision can be named, the activity probably doesn't belong in the function.

Engagement patterns: matching depth to the work

Using the right depth for the work matters as much as doing the work well. Embedding an architect in every program is the fastest way to run out of architects.

PatternUse it forCapacity cost
TransactionalA bounded question answered with one output: an ADR, an analysis, a recommendation. Should be the most common pattern.Low
AdvisorySignificant initiatives that need architectural input on a rhythm (weekly check-ins, decision sessions) while the team owns the work. Most material initiatives belong here.Medium
ReviewDecisions above the review threshold, where EA pressure-tests the work without doing itLow per review
EmbeddedMulti-quarter, multi-team programs; a solution architect joins the team, attends its rituals, and owns its architecture artifactsHigh; reserve it
CommunityChapters and guilds where architectural thinking spreads beyond the EA teamOngoing investment
At scale, fund the non-architect roles too: a practice manager who runs intake and scheduling, an analyst who keeps portfolio data current, and a writer who turns decisions into documents people read. Without them, architects do that work themselves at architect cost.

Roles and capacity allocation

A rough guide for full-time architects. The specific percentages matter less than the discipline of allocating deliberately rather than letting whatever's loudest consume all of it.

Roles
  • Head of EA: owns the operating model and the CIO/CTO relationship; still practices architecture as well as leading
  • Principal Architects: own the most significant decisions; scarce, and the function's effectiveness tracks how well their time is protected
  • Domain Architects: the authoritative voice within a specific domain (data, security, integration)
  • Solution Architects: the practical interface between EA and delivery teams
Capacity, steady state
  • 30 to 40 percent: direct engagements
  • 20 to 25 percent: review and consultation
  • 15 to 20 percent: function-level work (standards, reference material)
  • 10 to 15 percent: learning and development
  • 10 to 15 percent: organizational overhead
Architects at 100 percent direct engagement and 0 percent function-level work means the function is cannibalizing its own future. The reverse means it isn't producing value today. Neither is sustainable. Two further disciplines: ring-fence some capacity for strategic work nobody is asking for, such as a target-state refresh or a reference model for an emerging area, because otherwise it is always deferred; and report where capacity actually goes. If one program is consuming 60 percent of the function, leadership should know whether that is a choice or an accident.
17

Demand management

The single most common EA failure mode is unmanaged demand: every ask accepted, capacity consumed by whoever escalates loudest. A single intake channel with triage that can say no is the fix.

Triage outcomes
  • Accept: within scope, staffed, given a priority and expected timeline
  • Redirect: not EA scope, pointed to the right function with a warm handoff
  • Decline: out of scope or not worth the time now, with a stated reason
Prioritization criteria
  • Strategic significance
  • Reversibility, harder-to-reverse ranks higher
  • Timing criticality, whether there is still a window for input
  • Sponsor engagement
  • Blast radius
Notably absent from the criteria: who is asking. Prioritizing by requester seniority produces capture by the loudest voices at the expense of the most valuable work. The intake form asks five things: what is being asked, which decision or initiative it supports, who is asking, how urgent it is, and what EA should produce. A request that cannot answer those gets clarified before it is accepted. Triage weekly; an untriaged backlog grows from days to weeks faster than anyone expects and discredits the channel.

Architecture review: four touchpoints

Engagement rather than approval. Four short conversations during design beat one long review at the end, and the default posture is collaborative rather than gatekeeping.

TouchpointTimingPurpose
Early consultPre-scoping, before fundingOrientation and shared context; no formal decision
Design reviewAfter initial design, before deep buildPressure-test the design, surface risks, name decisions and owners
Readiness checkBefore production deploymentConfirm design-to-build integrity, rather than a generic approval
Post-deploymentQuarterly or event-drivenFeedback loop; extract patterns for the catalog
Required engagement triggers on objective criteria: a new enterprise-shared capability, a new technology category, a standards exception request, spend above a defined threshold, or regulated data beyond ordinary scope. Small, in-pattern initiatives need no engagement at all; asking permission to use an already-reviewed pattern is friction dressed up as governance. Composition, tiers, and turnaround commitments are on Architecture Review Board; the principles reviews test against are on Architecture Principles.

How EA functions fail, and what fixes each

Five well-known patterns, worth naming so they can be watched for rather than discovered after the fact.

PatternWhat it looks likeUnderlying causeThe cure
Ivory towerPrinciples, standards, and target states teams ignore; respected in form, irrelevant in practiceOptimizing for artifacts over real decisions; architects too far from deliveryFewer documents, more conversations; measure artifacts by use, not production
Governance theaterTeams comply with the review and ignore its output; decisions happen elsewhereReviews imposed without helping anyone decide betterFewer, better reviews; if a review would not have changed the decision, skip it
Engineering-by-architectsArchitects deep in code while the architecture goes undoneNo clear boundary between architecture and engineering workReset the boundary; architects may code, but not instead of architecting

The test that cuts through all five: the function that works is the one whose consumers, business leaders, delivery teams, and engineering leadership, would actively resist losing. A function that can name five leaders who would fight to keep it is doing well; one that cannot should treat the absence as information. Score your own function on EA Maturity Assessment.

EA tooling: when a spreadsheet is enough

Tool purchases are one of the most common ways EA functions spend money without improving a decision. The right tool depends on scale and on where the data comes from, and the order matters: model, then data, then tool.

StageFits whenLoad firstHow it fails
Spreadsheets and a diagramming toolUp to roughly 200 applications and one EA teamThe capability model, the application list mapped to capabilities, owners, run cost, and TIME statusCopies multiply and nobody knows which is current. One owner per sheet, one link everyone uses
Diagrams as code for solution architectureSolution teams need diagrams that stay currentC4 context and container diagrams kept in the repository next to the service they describe, reviewed with the codeA diagram nobody updates, which is worse than none because people trust it
A dedicated EA repository platformSeveral hundred applications, multiple business units, an acquisition program, or portfolio data that finance and risk needIntegrations before manual entry: CMDB, single sign-on logs, contract records, and cloud billing, so the data refreshes itselfBought before the model exists, then filled by hand once and left to decay: a catalog that costs six figures a year and answers no questions
Rules that keep a tool useful
  • Model before tool. If the capability model and application list do not work in a spreadsheet, a platform will not fix them
  • Integrations over data entry. Anything that depends on architects typing updates is stale within two quarters
  • One owner per data element. Application owners own their records; EA owns the model and the quality checks
  • Judge it by questions answered. Which applications support this capability, what do they cost, which are end of life. If the tool cannot answer those in minutes, the count of objects stored in it is irrelevant
The platform market

The platform category includes products such as SAP LeanIX, Ardoq, Bizzdesign, and the application portfolio modules of IT service management suites. Evaluate them against your first three questions and your integration sources rather than feature lists; the scoring method on Vendor Evaluation applies.

The 200-application line is a practitioner rule of thumb, not a standard: below it, the cost of keeping a platform current usually exceeds the value it adds; above it, or with several business units feeding one portfolio, a spreadsheet stops being trustworthy. Portfolio scoring itself is on Application Portfolio.

Business leader perceptionStructured feedback twice a year: is EA helping or getting in the way?

Activity counts, such as requests triaged, reviews held, ADRs written, and standards maintained, are hygiene: report them, never confuse them with value. Metrics are diagnostic rather than a report card; a function that treats them as a grade gets better numbers and worse results. When EA reports upward, translate every item into a risk or business statement, keep one fixed one-page format for at least a year, and put the bad news first. See Board Reporting.

Stakeholder Engagement

Influence-interest mapping and the engagement plan built on top of it: the placement mistake that breaks most plans before they start, and how to handle the quadrant that gets mismanaged most often.

A preview of the stakeholder half of the Stakeholder & Vendor methodology. The full mapping tool and structured engagement plan template are available through direct engagement.
Framework

Influence-interest mapping

The foundation the engagement plan gets built on. Placing a stakeholder wrong on this grid is the most common reason an engagement plan fails before it starts.

QuadrantDefinitionEngagement approach
High influence, high interestCan materially help or block the initiative and is already paying attention to itManage closely; regular direct engagement, involve in key decisions before they're finalized
High influence, low interestCan materially affect the outcome but isn't currently engaged with the detailsKeep satisfied; concise, infrequent updates focused on what matters to them specifically rather than a full status report
Low influence, high interestCares a great deal but has limited power to change the outcomeKeep informed; regular updates, a working channel for input, without over-indexing decisions on their preferences
Low influence, low interestNeither able nor inclined to affect the outcomeMonitor; minimal effort, don't manufacture engagement they haven't asked for

The placement mistake that breaks the plan

Where influence-interest mapping most often goes wrong, before the engagement plan itself is even built.

The mistake

Placing someone by their org chart position instead of their influence over this specific initiative. A senior title doesn't guarantee influence over a given decision, and a mid-level person who controls a critical dependency can have more practical influence than their title suggests.

The fix

Score influence against the specific decision at hand rather than general organizational seniority: who can delay, block, or accelerate this particular initiative, and who has the budget, headcount, or technical dependency that makes them load-bearing here.

2

Building the engagement plan itself

The map tells you where someone sits. The plan is what you do about it, and this is where most stakeholder work stops short.

What a working plan includes

Named owner per stakeholder (not "the team"), defined cadence matched to their quadrant, the specific message or ask for each touchpoint, and a trigger for when their quadrant placement should be re-evaluated, since influence and interest both shift as an initiative progresses.

What gets skipped

Re-evaluating placement over time. A stakeholder who started low-interest often becomes high-interest once the initiative starts visibly affecting their team, and a plan built once at kickoff and never revisited misses that shift until it becomes a problem.

3

Handling the high-influence, low-interest stakeholder

The quadrant most engagement plans get wrong in practice, since the instinct is to either over-engage or ignore them entirely.

The trap

Sending this stakeholder the same detailed weekly update everyone else gets. They tune out, and then when a decision needs their sign-off, they have no context and the ask lands as a surprise, which damages trust right at the moment you need it most.

What works instead

A short, infrequent update translated into what matters to them specifically, budget impact for a finance stakeholder, risk exposure for legal, timeline impact for a dependent team, in place of a general status report. When you do need something from them, it lands as a continuation of an existing thread rather than a cold ask.

Worked example

A platform migration with six stakeholders

The mapping exercise that finds the stakeholder the org chart missed.

The first map

A migration of the core customer platform to a new cloud environment. The initial map, drawn by the program manager from the org chart, placed the CFO and the COO as high influence, high interest; two VPs as high influence, low interest; the head of customer support as low influence, high interest; and the compliance manager as low influence, low interest. The plan was built around the two executives. Six weeks in, the migration stalled: the compliance manager had not approved the data residency change and would not be rushed, and the head of support had quietly briefed the COO that the cutover window would hit peak call volume.

The corrected map

Scored against the decision rather than the org chart, the compliance manager is high influence (a hard dependency with a veto) and needed managing closely from week one. The head of support is high influence through the COO, and needed a direct channel rather than updates. One of the two VPs turned out to have no stake in the outcome at all and was moved to monitor. The corrected plan added two named relationships and removed one. The migration cut over eight weeks late, which is what mis-placement of two people cost, and the engagement plan was re-scored at every phase gate after that.

Method

When the map changes

Influence and interest move. The plan has to say when to look again, because nobody re-scores a stakeholder map on instinct.

  • A phase gate: at each one, re-score every stakeholder against the next phase's decisions rather than the last one's
  • A reorganization, a new executive, or a change in who owns a dependency
  • The initiative starts visibly affecting a team that was previously low interest; they will move to high interest and usually to high influence through whoever they report to
  • A budget cycle: finance stakeholders move quadrant when the initiative's cost appears on a review
  • A public setback: an incident or a missed milestone raises the interest of everyone who was keeping a distance
  • A quiet stakeholder goes silent for two consecutive touchpoints; silence from a high-influence stakeholder is a signal rather than a convenience
Common pitfall

The map built once, by one person, in private

A stakeholder map is a set of judgments about people. Built alone, it reflects one person's view of the organization; kept private, it never gets corrected.

What it looks like

The program manager draws the grid at kickoff, files it in the project folder, and runs the plan from memory. Nobody with a different view of the organization ever sees it. The placements are wrong in the ways one person's placements are always wrong, and the errors surface as surprises rather than as corrections.

The counter-move

Build the map in a room with three people who see the organization from different angles: the sponsor, the delivery lead, and someone from the function most affected. Disagreement about a placement is the useful output; it means someone knows something the others do not. Keep the plan visible to the core team, re-score at every gate, and treat it as a living record of what you know about who can move this initiative. The Technology Decision Rights map is the natural cross-check: anyone with an A or an R on the relevant decisions belongs on the grid.

Reference only. Stakeholder positioning shifts as an initiative progresses; treat this as a starting map to revisit rather than a one-time exercise.

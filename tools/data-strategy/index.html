Data Strategy

Beyond the storage architecture question: the operating model behind who owns data, self-service and literacy for the rest of the organization, real-time versus batch as an economic decision, build versus buy for the data stack, and whether the data itself is a product worth monetizing, alongside warehouse versus lakehouse versus mesh, master data management, and data quality as an AI prerequisite.

General data strategy patterns, not a specific technology recommendation. The right architecture and operating model depend on organizational structure and data maturity more than on which pattern is currently trending.
Framework

Warehouse, lakehouse, or mesh

The architectural choice most data strategy conversations start with, and only one piece of the actual strategy.

PatternFits whenStruggles when
Data warehouseStructured, well-understood reporting needs, a single team can reasonably own data quality centrallyData sources are highly heterogeneous or domain ownership needs to be genuinely distributed
Data lakehouseMixed structured and unstructured data, need for both BI and ML workloads on the same underlying dataOrganizational discipline around schema and quality is weak; a lakehouse without governance becomes a swamp fast
Data meshLarge organization with genuinely independent domains, each with the capacity to own their own data productApplied to an organization too small to staff domain-level data ownership; the coordination overhead exceeds the benefit
1

Data as a product, not a project

The mindset shift underneath data mesh, applicable even in organizations not adopting the full mesh architecture.

Project mindset

A data pipeline gets built once to answer a specific question, then quietly rots as the source system changes underneath it, with no owner responsible for noticing or fixing the drift until a report breaks and someone escalates.

Product mindset

A dataset has a named owner, a defined quality bar, a consumer-facing contract (schema, freshness, meaning of each field), and a deprecation process, the same discipline applied to an internal API, applied to data instead.

2

The operating model: centralized versus federated data teams

The organizational question underneath data mesh. Mesh describes how data is structured and owned technically; this is who's staffed, and where they report.

Centralized

One team owns data infrastructure, pipelines, and quality end to end across the whole company. Consistent standards and a single point of accountability, but becomes a bottleneck as the number of data-consuming teams grows past what one team can service responsively.

Federated

Data engineers embedded within each domain team, closer to the business context and faster to respond to that domain's needs. Standards and quality drift apart across domains without a strong central platform layer underneath providing shared tooling and governance.

Most organizations land somewhere between the two: a central platform team providing shared infrastructure and standards, with domain-embedded engineers applying them locally. The failure mode is picking neither deliberately and ending up with an accidental, undocumented mix of both.
3

Data literacy and self-service, not just engineering access

Most data strategies are written for the people building the pipelines. This is about everyone else.

What works

A defined, curated set of self-service tools and pre-modeled datasets a non-technical analyst can query directly with guardrails, paired with genuine training investment, not just a BI tool license handed out and called self-service.

Common failure mode

Self-service rolled out as tool access alone, which produces a proliferation of inconsistent, ungoverned dashboards each answering the same business question slightly differently, worse for decision-making than the centralized bottleneck it was meant to fix.

4

The golden record problem

Master data management's central challenge: the same real-world entity represented differently across systems, with no single system holding the authoritative version.

Why it's hard

Customer data alone commonly lives in a CRM, a billing system, a support tool, and a product database, each with its own ID, its own update cadence, and its own partial view. Picking one as automatically authoritative is rarely right for every field.

What works

A defined source-of-truth mapping at the field level, not the entity level: billing system is authoritative for payment status, CRM is authoritative for account ownership, product database is authoritative for usage data. Merge logic follows the mapping rather than picking one system to trust for everything.

Data quality as an AI prerequisite

Why this page and the AI Governance Map's data foundation layer are describing the same underlying problem from two different altitudes.

The connection

An AI system retrieving from or trained on poor-quality, poorly owned data inherits every one of that data's problems, at scale and with less visibility into the failure than a human analyst working the same data manually would have had.

The practical implication

Data quality and ownership work is not a prerequisite project to finish before starting AI work, it's the same foundational investment either way; an organization with strong data product discipline has most of what AI governance's Layer 1 requires already in place.

Real-time versus batch, as a strategic decision

An implementation detail on the surface, and an economic decision underneath, the same shape as the argument on Reliability as an Economic Decision, applied to data freshness instead of system uptime.

When real-time earns its cost

Fraud detection, live personalization, operational alerting, use cases where the value of the insight decays to near-zero within minutes or seconds, genuinely justifying real-time infrastructure's higher operational complexity and cost.

The overengineering trap

Building real-time streaming infrastructure for a report that gets looked at once a week. The freshness nobody needed doesn't add value; it just adds an ongoing operational tax the same way an unneeded ninth of availability does.

Build versus buy for the data stack

See Build vs Buy for the general framework. Data tooling is one of the clearest places it applies.

Where buying usually wins

ETL and ELT tooling, data catalogs, and BI platforms are mature, competitive markets. Building these in-house rarely produces a genuine competitive advantage and usually just recreates a worse version of what's commercially available.

Where building can be justified

A genuinely novel data product that is itself the company's competitive differentiation, not the infrastructure underneath it. The same differentiation test from Build vs Buy applies directly: would the company lose deals or margin without owning this specifically.

8

Migrating between patterns without a rewrite

Almost no organization starts on the right pattern and stays there forever. How the migration happens matters as much as which pattern is the eventual target.

What works

Run the new pattern alongside the old one for specific domains first, proving value on a bounded scope before cutting anything over globally. The same strangler-fig discipline covered on Legacy Modernization Execution applies directly here: incremental migration, not a single rip-and-replace event.

What backfires

Announcing a full data platform migration as a single multi-quarter program with no intermediate value delivered along the way. Executive patience for a program with no visible output runs out well before a data platform migration typically finishes.

9

Data contracts, made concrete

"Data contract" gets used loosely. A contract that doesn't specify these things isn't one.

What a real contract specifies

The schema, a freshness commitment (how current the data is guaranteed to be), the meaning of each field in plain language, and a deprecation policy for breaking changes, published somewhere consumers can find and reference it, not buried in a single engineer's memory.

What passes for a contract but isn't

A schema definition with no freshness commitment and no deprecation process. Consumers build against it anyway, because it's the only documentation available, and get broken silently the first time the upstream schema changes without warning.

10

Data as a product to external customers

A question worth asking explicitly, even for organizations that will answer no.

For some organizations, the data itself, aggregated, anonymized, or offered directly, is a real monetization opportunity distinct from its internal analytical use. This isn't relevant to every company, but it's worth asking deliberately rather than never considering it at all: does this organization sit on data that would be genuinely valuable to someone else, and if so, is productizing it worth the privacy, security, and packaging investment that would require. The honest answer for most companies is no, but it should be a considered no, not a question nobody asked.

Checklist

Signs the data strategy isn't working

Patterns that show up when a data strategy exists on a slide but isn't shaping how data gets built and maintained.

  • No dataset has a named owner accountable for its quality
  • A migration between patterns has stalled in an expensive middle state, both old and new running indefinitely with no plan to finish
  • Data contracts, where they exist, specify a schema but no freshness or deprecation commitment
  • The same real-world entity holds conflicting values across systems with no field-level source-of-truth mapping to resolve it
  • AI initiatives are blocked by data quality issues that were already flagged as a data strategy risk before the AI initiative started
  • Self-service access was granted with no curation or training, producing multiple dashboards that answer the same question differently
  • Real-time infrastructure exists for a use case nobody has ever needed the data faster than daily for
  • The centralized-versus-federated data team question was never decided deliberately, it just happened by accident of hiring

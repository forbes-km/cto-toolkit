M&A Diligence Kit: Question Bank, Data Room, Sell-Side Prep

The Technology Diligence page covers the method and the red flags. This page is the working kit: the questions to ask or answer, the data room structure that organizes the answers and controls who sees what, and the preparation sequence for a technology leader whose company is about to be sold.

The question bank here is a curated subset of the full bank, chosen for what most often moves price or terms. Healthcare, financial services, and public-sector transactions carry additional regulatory questions; see Regulatory Map for which regimes apply and who owns them.
How to use

Three tools, two sides of the table

The question bank is what a buyer asks. The data room structure is how a seller answers before being asked. The sell-side guide is what a technology leader does in the months before a process starts so that the answers exist. Read the one for your side, then the other one, because the counterparty will.

Question bank

Architecture and technical debt

Priority 1 questions should be answered before a price is agreed. Priority 2 belong in confirmatory diligence. Priority 3 inform the integration plan.

PriorityQuestionAsk viaWhat a bad answer looks like
1Draw the system as it runs today, not the target architecture, and mark what fewer than three people understandInterviewA slide from the last investor deck; nobody in the room can draw the current state
1Which runtimes, databases, and frameworks are past vendor end of life, and what is the plan and cost to moveDocA list has to be compiled for the question; extended support contracts nobody mentioned
1What is the technical debt backlog, with a cost of remediation and a carry cost per itemDocA backlog of tickets with no costs, or a statement that debt is under control with no artifact
1How is the platform hosted, in which regions and accounts, and who holds the root credentialsDocA shared root account, a personal credit card on a cloud account, production in a region the buyer cannot operate in
2What is the deployment frequency, change failure rate, and time to restore for the core product over the last twelve monthsDocNumbers unavailable; deployments happen monthly by one person on a weekend
2What third-party components, open-source licenses, and embedded SDKs are in the product, and which carry copyleft or commercial obligationsDocNo software bill of materials; a GPL dependency in a commercially licensed product
2Which parts of the product would have to be rewritten to scale ten times, and has anyone estimated itInterviewEveryone agrees it will scale; the load test is from three years ago
3What does a full environment rebuild from source and backups take, and when was it last doneInterviewNever; the environment was built by hand and the person who built it left

Security, compliance, and data

Certifications tell you what was in scope for an audit. These questions tell you what is true.

PriorityQuestionAsk viaWhat a bad answer looks like
1Which systems and products are in scope for each certification held, and which revenue-bearing systems are out of scopeDocThe SOC 2 covers the corporate network and not the product that drives the valuation
1List every security incident, breach, or near-miss in the last three years, with notifications made and outcomesDocNone reported, and a customer contract on file references a credit for a breach
1Where is customer and employee personal data stored and processed, under which legal entity and jurisdiction, and what lawful basis and transfer mechanism appliesDocData in three clouds across two continents with no record of why; sub-processors not listed

2What data does the product send to AI or ML providers, under which terms, and with what retentionDocCustomer data sent to a consumer AI tool by the support team under default terms
2Which customer contracts carry security, availability, or data-residency commitments that exceed what the platform deliversDocA 99.99 percent SLA on a platform with no redundancy; residency promised in a region not used

Question bank

Operations, organization, IP, and cost

The questions that most often reprice a deal are here, and they are the ones diligence teams under time pressure skip.

PriorityQuestionAsk viaWhat a bad answer looks like
1For each system that generates revenue, name the people who can change it in production, and their tenure, compensation, and retention termsDocOne name repeated across every system; no retention agreements; that person has not been told about the deal
1Who owns the intellectual property in the product: employee and contractor assignment agreements, open-source contributions, university or prior-employer claimsDocContractors with no assignment clause built the core; a founder's prior employer has a plausible claim
1What is the fully loaded run cost of the platform by month for the last 24 months, and what drives the varianceDocCloud bill grew 60 percent with no revenue growth to match; reserved commitments expire at close
1Which contracts have change-of-control, assignment, or most-favored-customer clauses, and which vendors gain leverage at closeDocThe core database vendor's license terminates on change of control
2What is the engineering headcount by role, level, location, and employment type, and what is regretted attrition over 24 monthsDocHalf the team are contractors through one agency; attrition unmeasured
2What does the on-call rota look like, how many incidents per month, and what is the escalation path when the first responder cannot fix itInterviewTwo people on permanent call; escalation is the CTO's mobile
2Which product commitments have been made to customers or the market that are not yet built, and what would they costInterviewA roadmap sold to enterprise customers that engineering has not seen
3What tooling, licenses, and subscriptions exist, who owns each, and which would the combined entity duplicateDocNo inventory; several tools paid by individuals on expense
3For healthcare or financial targets specifically: which regulatory registrations, business associate agreements, and audit obligations transfer, and which must be re-executedDocBAAs with the wrong entity; a state licensure requirement nobody on the deal team knows about
Template

Data room structure with a four-tier access model

A well-organized technology data room signals a well-run technology function. The tiers control what a buyer sees at each stage, so that the most sensitive material is only visible to a buyer who has earned it.

TierWho sees itWhenFolders
1: OverviewAny qualified buyer under NDATeaser and first management meeting01 Technology overview narrative; 02 Architecture summary (current state, one page); 03 Team structure and headcount summary; 04 Certifications and attestations held; 05 Product roadmap summary
2: ConfirmatoryBuyers who have submitted an indicative offerConfirmatory diligence06 Detailed architecture and data flow diagrams; 07 Technical debt register with costs; 08 Engineering metrics, 24 months; 09 Security policies, last pen test summary, incident register; 10 Data inventory and privacy documentation; 11 Third-party components and license inventory; 12 Infrastructure and run cost, 24 months; 13 Vendor contract summaries with change-of-control flags
3: RestrictedFinal bidder or bidders, named individuals onlyExclusivity14 Full pen test reports and open findings; 15 Employee and contractor agreements, IP assignments; 16 Key-person list with compensation and retention terms; 17 Customer contract exceptions (SLA, residency, security commitments); 18 Open litigation, regulatory correspondence, and audit findings
4: Clean teamOutside counsel and designated clean-team members onlyPost-exclusivity, pre-close19 Source code access (read-only, escrowed, or on-site review); 20 Production access evidence (privileged account export); 21 Customer-identifiable data, where its review is legally necessary

Every folder has an index file naming its contents and its owner. Every document is dated. Nothing is uploaded that the technology leader has not read; a data room is a set of representations, and the buyer's counsel will treat it as one.

Sell-side preparation

The six months before a process

Most sell-side technology preparation is a scramble after the banker's request list arrives. The work below, done early, changes the price and shortens the process.

  1. Month 6: Answer the question bank internallyRun every priority 1 and 2 question above against your own organization, in writing, as if you were the buyer. The gaps are the preparation plan. This is a Technology Diligence exercise pointed at yourself.
  2. Month 5: Fix what is cheap and visibleRemove former employees' access. Eliminate standing privileged access. Close open critical pen test findings. Retire the end-of-life version that would appear in every buyer's report. These cost weeks and remove findings that cost millions in price.
  3. Month 4: Build the artifacts that do not existA current-state architecture diagram. A technical debt register with costs, using Technical Debt Economics. A software bill of materials. An inventory of privileged accounts and non-human identities. A run-cost history with variance explained. A buyer will produce these if you do not, and theirs will be less flattering.
  4. Month 3: Paper the people and the IPIP assignment agreements for every current and former contributor. Retention agreements for the named key people, executed before they hear about the process from anyone else. A succession answer for each critical system, using CTO Leadership & Succession.
  5. Month 2: Review the contractsEvery customer contract for SLA, residency, and security commitments the platform does not meet; every vendor contract for change-of-control and assignment clauses. Renegotiate or document the exposure. Do not let the buyer find it.
  6. Month 1: Build the data room and rehearse the narrativePopulate tiers 1 and 2. Write the technology overview narrative as prose, not slides, describing the estate as it is, with its known weaknesses and their remediation plan stated plainly. A buyer who finds a weakness you disclosed treats you as credible; one who finds a weakness you omitted reprices everything.

Common pitfall

Disclosing the target state instead of the current state

The most common sell-side failure is presenting the architecture the team is building toward as if it were the architecture that exists.

What it looks like

The overview shows microservices, event streaming, and a zero-trust network. The buyer's engineers find a monolith, a nightly batch, and a flat network with the target-state work six months from done. Every other representation in the data room is now suspect, the confirmatory window extends, and the price moves.

The counter-move

Show the current state, honestly labeled, alongside the target state, honestly dated and costed. Buyers expect debt; what they price is surprise. A credible current-state picture with a funded plan is worth more than an aspirational one, and it is the only version that survives the buyer's own engineers reading the code.

AI and the Engineering Operating Model

What AI-assisted coding is doing to how engineering work gets done: skill development, code review, and how to measure productivity when the tool itself is part of the output. Distinct from deciding which AI initiatives to build.

What changes, and what doesn't

AI-assisted coding shifts specific parts of the job. It doesn't touch others, and treating it as a uniform speedup misreads what's happening.

1

AI productivity does not automatically equal more story points

The most common measurement mistake, and the one most likely to produce a plan that breaks on contact with reality.

What happens

AI-assisted coding speeds up the mechanical parts, boilerplate, first drafts, syntax, which shifts the real bottleneck to review, architectural judgment, and testing: the parts that were never the slow part for a senior engineer, but were close to the entire job for a junior one.

The naive read

Treating a rise in lines of code or pull request volume as productivity, then setting headcount or delivery expectations against that number. The plan breaks the moment the team hits the bottleneck AI never touched: review capacity and architectural decision-making.

2

Code review changes shape

Reviewing AI-generated code is a different skill than reviewing human-written code, and most engineers were never trained on the difference.

What's different

Reviewing AI-generated code means checking for plausible-but-wrong patterns, code that looks idiomatic and compiles cleanly but embeds a subtly incorrect assumption, more than checking for the kind of mistake a human engineer is likely to make. That's a distinct reviewing skill.

The risk

Review fatigue from higher volume, combined with AI-generated code's tendency to look confident and clean, produces a false sense of safety. Reviewers approve faster because the code reads well, not because they verified it more rigorously.

3

What happens to junior engineer development

The concern worth stating honestly, and the one most tempting to dismiss because it doesn't show up as a problem until much later.

The concern

A junior engineer who leans on AI for the mechanical work skips the repetition that used to build pattern recognition and debugging intuition the slow way. That intuition doesn't obviously appear missing until a novel problem requires it and it isn't there.

What to do about it

Deliberately assign some work without AI assistance specifically for skill-building, framed as a training decision rather than a productivity one, the same way a pilot still trains on manual flight even though autopilot handles most of the actual flying.

4

Measuring productivity when the tool is part of the output

The measurement question every other section on this page eventually points back to.

What doesn't work

Raw output volume: lines of code, pull requests merged, AI suggestions accepted. All of these can rise while the team ships less real, correct, maintained value, since none of them measure whether the output was right.

What's more honest

The outcome measures already covered on Engineering Metrics, cycle time, change failure rate, and ultimately business outcomes, tracked specifically before and after AI tooling adoption, rather than assuming adoption itself is automatically the win.

Checklist

Signs AI adoption is being measured or managed naively

Patterns that show up when AI tooling gets adopted enthusiastically but without adjusting how the organization measures or trains around it.

  • Productivity claims rest on lines of code, PR volume, or AI suggestion acceptance rate rather than outcome measures
  • Code review practice hasn't been adjusted for the specific failure modes of AI-generated code
  • No deliberate mechanism exists for junior engineers to build skills without AI assistance
  • Change failure rate or defect rate hasn't been tracked before and after AI tooling adoption
  • Headcount or timeline plans assume a productivity multiplier that was asserted rather than measured
Reference only. AI-assisted development tooling and its effects on engineering work are evolving quickly; treat this as a set of questions to keep asking, not a fixed conclusion.

AI Governance Map

Four layers, from data foundation to responsible AI oversight, each with concrete controls mapped to what breaks without them and which regulatory frameworks require them. Includes the standards and credentials landscape, AIGP, ISO/IEC 42001, NIST AI RMF, and where each applies.

This is the technical and organizational governance layer. Legal review of applicable regulation for your specific jurisdiction and use case is not a substitute this page provides; treat the regulatory mapping table as a starting point for that conversation, not the conversation itself.
Layer 1 · data foundation

Data foundation

Before anything else, know what the model uses. If you cannot answer where a piece of training or retrieval data came from, nothing above this layer holds up.

Control
  • Source registry covering every system feeding the AI pipeline
  • Data dictionary defining what each field means and where it originated
  • Lineage and provenance tracking from source document to retrieved chunk
  • Clear data ownership assigned per source system, not left implicit
Driver & failure mode

NIST AI RMF's Map function starts here: you cannot assess risk in a system whose inputs you cannot trace. Without this layer, an incident investigation cannot answer "where did this bad output come from," which is usually the first question an investigation asks.

Layer 2 · governance layer

Governance layer

Once the data foundation is solid, decide who decides what. Definition locks, retrieval boundaries, input validation, and a named data steward give the system clear lines of accountability.

Control
  • Definition locks: agreed, versioned definitions for terms the system reasons about, not left to drift between teams
  • Retrieval boundaries: which sources an agent or index is allowed to draw from for a given use case
  • Input validation on both user prompts and any document entering the ingestion pipeline
  • A named data steward with actual authority to change what's retrievable
Driver & failure mode

Without a data steward, retrieval boundaries erode silently as new sources get connected for convenience, and the system starts answering from data nobody approved for that use case. This is the layer that turns "we have a RAG pipeline" into "we have a RAG pipeline someone is accountable for."

Layer 3 · trusted outputs

Trusted outputs

With governance in place, you can start defending your answers. Confidence thresholds, output guardrails, and bias checks turn "the AI said so" into something you can stand behind to a customer, auditor, or regulator.

Control
  • Confidence thresholds tied to the human-in-the-loop tiering model (see the Architecture Map)
  • Output guardrails: schema validation, citation requirements, forbidden-content checks before a response ships
  • Bias and fairness checks on outputs for any use case touching people (hiring, credit, care recommendations)
  • Explainability sufficient to answer "why did it say that" after the fact, not just at design time
Driver & failure mode

A confidence score without a documented threshold policy is not a control, it's a number nobody acts on consistently. This layer is what regulators and auditors ask to see evidence of; it needs to produce artifacts (logs, rubric scores), not just exist as a design intention.

Layer 4 · responsible AI

Responsible AI & high-risk oversight

The outer layer: autonomous agents, high-risk decisions, and regulated use cases. This only works because everything underneath it is already solid.

Control
  • Formal classification of which use cases count as high-risk under applicable frameworks
  • Documented incident response plan specific to AI failures, not a generic IT incident process
  • Human oversight for high-risk decisions, implemented as the Tier 2 gate described on the Architecture Map, not a policy statement with no technical enforcement
  • Hallucination and groundedness checks running continuously, not only at initial launch
Driver & failure mode

Credit scoring, employment screening, and medical decisioning fall within the EU AI Act's Annex III high-risk use cases when the specific system matches the listed criteria there, not automatically because it operates in one of those domains generally. Article 14 requires human oversight for systems that do qualify. Enforcement timelines for specific Annex III obligations have shifted since the Act's original schedule, some employment-related obligations were pushed from August 2026 to December 2027 under a later political agreement, so confirm the current applicable date for your specific use case rather than assuming a fixed 2026 deadline.

Cross-cutting

Model risk management

Treating the model itself, not just its outputs, as something with a lifecycle that needs governing.

Control

A model inventory listing every deployed model, its version, what it's used for, and who owns the decision to keep using it. Pre-deployment evaluation against a defined rubric before a new model version replaces an old one. Ongoing drift monitoring in production, tied to the observability stage on the Architecture Map, not a one-time evaluation at launch.

Driver & failure mode

The single most common gap: a team can name the model they deployed in January but not confirm what's running in production today after three silent provider-side updates. If you cannot answer "which exact model version produced this output," you cannot investigate an incident or defend a decision.

The standards and credentials landscape

Four different things people mean when they say "AI governance," easy to conflate and genuinely different in what they certify, who holds them, and what they cost.

NameWhat it isWho or what it applies to
AIGP (IAPP)An individual professional credential, the AI-governance equivalent of IAPP's CIPP privacy certifications. 100-question exam across four domains: AI governance foundations, applicable laws and standards, governing AI development, governing AI deployment and use.A person. Validates that someone can run an AI governance program; doesn't certify the organization or any specific system.
ISO/IEC 42001A certifiable AI management system standard, the AI equivalent of ISO 27001 for information security. Third-party audited (Stage 1 and Stage 2), valid three years with annual surveillance audits, roughly 38 Annex A controls selected and justified against your own risk assessment, not a fixed checklist.An organization's AI management system. Already held by AWS, Anthropic, and Microsoft. Doesn't by itself satisfy EU AI Act obligations, but the risk assessment and governance structure it requires feeds directly into the technical documentation the Act does require.
NIST AI RMFA voluntary US framework (Govern, Map, Measure, Manage), guidance to structure a program against, not a certification anyone audits you to.An organization's practice, self-assessed. Already referenced throughout this page's layer structure.
ISO/IEC 23894Risk management guidance specific to AI, meant to be used alongside ISO 31000's general risk management approach.An organization's risk process. Guidance, not a certifiable standard on its own.
OECD AI PrinciplesHigh-level international principles (inclusive growth, human-centered values, transparency, robustness, accountability), adopted by dozens of governments as policy language.National policy and regulation that draws on these principles; not something an organization implements or gets certified against directly.
The practical distinction that matters most: AIGP tells you something about a person you might hire or promote into an AI governance role. ISO 42001 tells a customer or regulator something about your organization, backed by an independent audit. Neither substitutes for the other, and neither substitutes for the technical controls described in the four layers above; a certified management system with no enforced technical controls underneath it is a real and common failure pattern.
Judgment call

Is ISO 42001 certification worth pursuing

A real decision with a real cost, not something to default into because the standard exists.

Worth it

Enterprise customers or regulators are asking for independent evidence of AI governance maturity, not just a policy document. Selling into procurement processes where a competitor's ISO 42001 badge is becoming a checkbox. The organization already holds ISO 27001, since the incremental cost and timeline drop substantially when the management-system muscle already exists.

Not worth it yet

A small AI footprint with a handful of internal use cases and no customer demanding third-party evidence. The four layers above aren't solidly in place yet, certifying a management system before the underlying technical controls exist produces a certificate that doesn't reflect reality, and an auditor will eventually find that gap. NIST AI RMF, applied honestly and evidenced internally, covers most of the same ground without the $20,000 to $60,000 audit cost and multi-year commitment.

Accountability

Accountability & audit trail

Who signs off on an AI system going live, and what gets logged so that decision can be reconstructed later.

Control

A named accountable owner per AI use case, not a committee with no single decision-maker. Decision logging: every promotion of a new model or index version recorded with who approved it and what evaluation evidence supported the approval.

Driver & failure mode

Diffuse accountability is a recurring AI governance failure pattern behind headline incidents. It's rarely that no one thought about the risk; it's that no single person owned the decision to accept it.

Incident response

AI-specific incident response

Different from a general IT incident: a hallucinated answer causing harm, a bias incident, or a model behaving unexpectedly needs its own triage path.

Control

A defined severity classification specific to AI failures (harmful hallucination, biased output, data leakage through a response, agent taking an unauthorized action). A rollback path that can revert to a prior model or index version quickly, which depends directly on the versioning discipline described in model risk management above.

Driver & failure mode

A general incident response process (see Incident Response) usually isn't built to ask "was this the model's fault, the retrieval's fault, or the underlying data's fault," and that triage question determines the fix. Build this as an extension of the general process, not a replacement for it.

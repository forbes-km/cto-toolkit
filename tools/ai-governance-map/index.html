AI Governance Map

AI governance organized to the four domains of the IAPP AIGP Body of Knowledge, version 2.1: the foundations of AI governance, how laws, standards and frameworks apply to AI, governing AI development, and governing AI deployment and use. Each competency carries the concrete controls that make it real, what breaks without them, and where the evidence comes from, followed by the standards landscape, when ISO/IEC 42001 certification is worth it, and a regulatory mapping.

This page uses the AIGP structure so the program, the people running it, and anyone holding or studying for the credential share one vocabulary. It is not IAPP material or exam preparation, and it does not replace legal review of the rules that apply to your jurisdiction and use case; treat the regulatory sections as the starting point for that conversation.

Domain I · I.A

What AI is, and why it needs its own governance

Scope comes first. If the program cannot say what counts as an AI system, the inventory will miss most of what the organization actually runs.

Control
  • A written working definition of an AI system, aligned to the OECD and EU AI Act definitions, so the inventory scope is consistent across teams
  • An AI inventory that covers built, bought, and embedded AI, including AI features switched on inside existing SaaS products
  • A harm taxonomy the risk process uses every time: harm to individuals (rights, safety, discrimination, privacy), to groups and society, to the organization (legal, financial, reputational), and to the environment
  • A short set of adopted responsible AI principles (fairness, safety, transparency, privacy, accountability, human oversight), each mapped to at least one control on this page
Driver & failure mode

AI earns its own governance because of characteristics ordinary software does not share: probabilistic output, opacity, drift after deployment, behavior learned from data rather than written in code, scale, and, with agents, the ability to act. The common failure is a program that governs the chatbot engineering built while the resume screener a vendor added last quarter goes unexamined. Principles with no control mapped to them are a poster, not a program.

Domain I · I.B

Roles, responsibilities, and organizational expectations

Who signs off on an AI system going live, who is consulted, and which legal role the organization is playing for each system.

Control
  • A named accountable owner per AI use case. A committee with no single decision-maker does not count
  • A cross-functional governance forum (legal, privacy, security, risk, data, product, HR, and the business owner) with a written charter and a decision log
  • For every system, a recorded answer to whether the organization is the developer, the provider, the deployer, or a user, because obligations follow the role, whatever the technology
  • Role-based AI literacy and training: what staff may and may not do with AI tools, deeper training for reviewers, owners, and anyone exercising human oversight
  • A governance approach sized to the organization's context: sector, size, risk appetite, and whether it builds or mostly buys
Driver & failure mode

Diffuse accountability is a recurring pattern behind headline AI incidents. It is rarely that nobody thought about the risk; it is that no single person owned the decision to accept it. Role confusion is the second failure. Under the EU AI Act a deployer that puts its own name on a high-risk system, substantially modifies it, or changes its intended purpose takes on provider obligations (Article 25).[2] Fine-tuning a vendor model for a new purpose can move an organization into that role without anyone deciding it should. Article 4 still expects providers and deployers to support AI literacy among their staff; the 2026 Omnibus softened it from guaranteeing a sufficient level to taking measures that support it.[3]

Domain I · I.C

Policies and procedures across the AI life cycle, including third parties

The policy set that turns principles into gates people actually pass through.

Control
  • An acceptable use policy for AI tools, covering which tools are approved and what data may go into them
  • A single intake path for every new AI use case, with risk tiering that decides how much review it gets (the calculator below; Tier 3 and 4 use cases get the human-in-the-loop design on the Architecture Map)
  • Life cycle gates: design review, pre-release review, periodic re-assessment, and retirement
  • Existing policies updated rather than duplicated: privacy, security, procurement, records retention, model and change management, incident response
  • A third-party AI risk policy: due diligence questions for AI vendors, required contract terms, and monitoring for vendor model changes after signature
Driver & failure mode

A standalone AI policy that no workflow references changes nothing. Intake is the control that keeps the inventory complete; without it, the inventory is a one-time survey that is stale within a quarter. Most enterprise AI exposure now arrives through vendors, which is why version 2.1 gives third-party risk management its own performance indicator, and why the procurement process has to ask about AI before the contract is signed. See Vendor Evaluation for the scoring and risk register.

Domain II · II.A

Existing privacy law, applied to AI

Most AI systems process personal data somewhere: in training, in retrieval, in prompts, or in logs. Privacy law applies to all of it today.

Control
  • A lawful basis and purpose-limitation check for each use of personal data in training, fine-tuning, retrieval, and inference
  • A data protection impact assessment where processing is likely to be high risk, run together with the AI impact assessment
  • Data minimization applied to prompts, retrieval indexes, and logs, with retention set for each
  • Transparency notices that say where AI is used and what it does with personal data
  • Handling rules for special category and sensitive data, and a process for automated decision rights: GDPR Article 22, and California's automated decision-making technology rules, which require compliance for significant decisions from 1 January 2027[4]
Driver & failure mode

The usual failures are data collected for one purpose reused for training without a compatible basis, and prompt and response logs growing into an unmanaged store of personal data nobody classified. Controllers stay responsible for AI processing even when a vendor runs the model. The EU AI Act allows special category data to be processed for bias detection and correction under strict conditions (Article 10(5)), and the 2026 Omnibus widened that route; it is a narrow exception, not a general basis. See Data Governance & Privacy.

Domain II · II.B

Other existing law: IP, nondiscrimination, consumer protection, product liability

Calling something AI is not a defense. These bodies of law reach AI systems now, regardless of when AI-specific rules arrive.

Control
  • Intellectual property: recorded rights to training and retrieval content, a position on ownership of generated output (US copyright protection requires human authorship), and vendor IP indemnities reviewed before signature
  • Nondiscrimination: bias testing for any system that touches employment, credit, housing, insurance, or access to services, with the adverse action and notice duties that already exist in those domains applied to model-driven decisions
  • Consumer protection: marketing and product claims about AI capabilities reviewed like any other claim, since overstating them is a deceptive practice
  • Product liability: software, including AI, treated as a product with a defect exposure where it is sold or embedded in products
Driver & failure mode

US regulators have said repeatedly that existing anti-discrimination and consumer protection law applies to automated systems, and enforcement has followed against inflated AI claims. In the EU, the revised Product Liability Directive explicitly covers software and AI systems and applies to products placed on the market after 9 December 2026, when member states must have transposed it.[5] The Commission dropped its separate AI Liability Directive proposal in 2025, so the revised product liability rules and national law carry that weight. The failure mode is treating these as future AI questions when they are current legal ones.

Domain II · II.C

AI-specific law: the main elements

The EU AI Act is the template most other AI-specific laws borrow from, so its structure is worth knowing even outside the EU.

Control
  • Risk classification: every inventoried system classified against prohibited practices (Article 5), high-risk categories (Article 6 with Annexes I and III), transparency duties (Article 50), and minimal risk
  • Provider obligations for high-risk systems: risk management, data governance, technical documentation, logging, instructions for deployers, human oversight, accuracy, robustness and cybersecurity, a quality management system, conformity assessment, and registration (Articles 9 to 17 and 43 to 49)
  • Deployer obligations: use according to instructions, competent human oversight, input data relevance, monitoring, log retention, and for public bodies and certain private deployers a fundamental rights impact assessment (Articles 26 and 27)
  • General-purpose AI models: documentation, copyright policy, and training content summaries for all providers, plus evaluation, incident reporting, and security duties for models with systemic risk (Articles 53 and 55)
  • Enforcement: fines of up to 35 million euros or 7 percent of worldwide turnover for prohibited practices, 15 million or 3 percent for most other breaches, and 7.5 million or 1 percent for supplying incorrect information (Article 99)
Driver & failure mode

Credit scoring and employment screening fall within the Annex III high-risk use cases when the specific system matches the criteria listed there; operating in one of those domains does not by itself make a system high-risk.[2] The Digital Omnibus on AI, adopted in June 2026 and in force from 27 July 2026, deferred the high-risk obligations: Annex III stand-alone systems now apply from 2 December 2027 and AI embedded in Annex I regulated products from 2 August 2028. Article 50 transparency duties have applied since 2 August 2026, with generative systems already on the market given until 2 December 2026 to meet the Article 50(2) marking requirement, and new prohibitions on generating non-consensual intimate imagery and child sexual abuse material also apply from 2 December 2026.[3] In the US, Colorado repealed its 2024 AI Act and replaced it with SB 26-189, a notice, explanation, and human-review regime for automated consequential decisions from 1 January 2027,[6] while a December 2025 executive order directed the Justice Department to challenge state AI laws the administration considers inconsistent with national policy.[7] The obligations moved; they did not go away. See Regulatory Map for the full timeline.

Domain III · III.A

Governing the design and build of AI systems

The decisions that are cheap to change on a whiteboard and expensive to change in production.

Control
  • A use case statement before any build: the business context, intended purpose, who is affected, the decision the system informs or makes, and what it must never do
  • An impact assessment before build, using ISO/IEC 42005 as the structure and combined with the privacy assessment where personal data is involved
  • Internal and external risks identified, including third-party models, open-weight components, datasets, and tools the system will call
  • Human oversight designed in from the start, using the tiering on the Architecture Map; bolting it on after launch rarely works
  • Design documentation kept as the build proceeds: intended use, known limitations, design choices and the alternatives rejected, recorded as ADRs
Driver & failure mode

Most AI incidents trace back to a purpose nobody wrote down, so the system drifted into uses it was never assessed for. The documentation is not paperwork for its own sake: under the EU AI Act a high-risk provider's technical documentation has to exist before the system is placed on the market, and reconstructing design rationale after the fact is slower and less credible than recording it during the build.

Domain III · III.B

Governing the data used for training, testing, and retrieval

If you cannot answer where a piece of training or retrieval data came from, nothing built on it holds up.

Control
  • Data governance requirements set per use case: a source registry covering every system feeding the pipeline, a data dictionary, and clear data ownership per source
  • Lineage and provenance tracked from source document to training set or retrieved chunk, with the embedding model version recorded
  • Definition locks: agreed, versioned definitions for terms the system reasons about, so they stop drifting between teams
  • Retrieval boundaries: which sources an agent or index may draw from for a given use case, changed only by a named data steward
  • Input validation on user prompts and on any document entering the ingestion pipeline
  • Training and testing planned and documented: representative and held-out test sets, bias and fairness testing for any use case touching people, adversarial and red-team testing, and the results kept as evidence
Driver & failure mode

NIST AI RMF's Map function starts here: you cannot assess risk in a system whose inputs you cannot trace. Without a data steward, retrieval boundaries erode as new sources get connected for convenience, and the system starts answering from data nobody approved for that use case. Without lineage, an investigation cannot answer where a bad output came from, which is usually the first question it asks. See Data Strategy for the data quality work underneath this.

Domain III · III.C

Governing release, monitoring, and maintenance

Treating the system as something with a life cycle, so what was true at launch is checked again after every model, prompt, or index change.

Control
  • A release readiness gate: evaluation against a defined rubric, documented confidence thresholds, output guardrails (schema validation, citation requirements, forbidden-content checks), and explainability sufficient to answer why it said that after the fact
  • A model and system inventory listing every deployed model and index, its exact version, its use, and who owns the decision to keep using it
  • Continuous monitoring for drift, groundedness, and hallucination in production, tied to the observability stage on the Architecture Map, plus scheduled maintenance and periodic re-assessment of performance, reliability, and safety
  • Decision logging: every promotion of a new model or index version recorded with who approved it and what evidence supported it
  • Incidents, issues, and risks managed and documented, with root cause worked jointly by the model, data, and platform owners
  • Public disclosures where required: transparency notices, system or model cards, and, for high-risk providers in the EU, serious incident reporting to the authorities
Driver & failure mode

The gap that shows up most often: a team can name the model they deployed in January but not confirm what is running in production today after three silent provider-side updates. If you cannot answer which exact model version produced an output, you cannot investigate an incident or defend a decision. A confidence score without a documented threshold policy is a number nobody acts on consistently, and no auditor will accept it as a control; this stage has to leave artifacts behind (logs, rubric scores, approvals). Intentions in a design doc don't count.

Domain IV · IV.A

Key factors and risks in deployment

The same model carries very different risk depending on where and how it is deployed.

Control
  • Use case context: who is affected, how significant the decision is, whether a human reviews the output, and whether the outcome is reversible
  • Model type: classical machine learning, generative, general-purpose, or agentic; proprietary or open-weight; and what each implies for testing, transparency, and control
  • Deployment option: an AI feature inside a SaaS product, a managed model API, a cloud-hosted platform, self-hosted open weights, or on-device, with the control surface each one leaves you
Driver & failure mode

A model summarizing internal meeting notes and the same model drafting adverse credit decisions are different risks, and the review should differ accordingly. Deployment choice decides how much you can control: a vendor feature gives you configuration and contract terms, a managed API adds prompt and guardrail control, and self-hosting gives full control along with full responsibility for patching, supply chain, and evaluation. Agents raise the stakes because they act rather than advise; see Identity & Access Architecture for scoping the identity they act with.

Domain IV · IV.B

Assessing the AI system before deployment

A deployer's due diligence: the system's impact, the vendor's terms, and the trade-offs of the model it runs on.

Control
  • An impact assessment performed or reviewed by the deployer, including a fundamental rights impact assessment where the EU AI Act requires one
  • Vendor agreements reviewed for the terms that matter for AI: whether your data trains their models, retention of prompts and outputs, IP indemnity, notice of model changes, audit and evidence rights, sub-processors, incident notification, output ownership, and liability caps
  • The risks and opportunities of proprietary model deployment weighed out loud: vendor support and indemnity against opacity, lock-in, and pricing exposure, and whether fine-tuning or rebranding shifts the organization into a provider role
Driver & failure mode

Most organizations deploy far more AI than they build, so this is where most of their exposure sits. The failure is a contract signed on standard terms because the AI feature arrived as part of a renewal nobody reviewed for it. The clauses that fix this are the same ones that protect any renewal; see SaaS Contract & Renewal Negotiation and the exit terms on Build vs Buy.

Domain IV · IV.C

Governing deployment and use

The outer layer: autonomous agents, consequential decisions, and regulated use cases, governed for as long as the system is live.

Control
  • Policies, procedures, and ethical considerations applied to each deployment, including who may use it and for what
  • Human oversight for high-risk decisions, implemented through the human-in-the-loop design on the Architecture Map and enforced in code rather than by policy statement alone
  • Continuous monitoring and scheduled maintenance, with periodic performance, reliability, and safety assessments in production
  • Incidents, issues, risks, and post-market monitoring documented, with a severity scale specific to AI failures (harmful hallucination, biased output, data leakage through a response, an agent taking an unauthorized action)
  • Controls against secondary and unintended use: usage limits, technical scoping, and monitoring for use drifting beyond the assessed purpose
  • An external communication plan: disclosure that people are interacting with AI, customer notices, and what is said if the system fails publicly
  • Controls to deactivate or localize a system: a tested kill switch, feature flags, geographic or tenant scoping, and a rollback path to a prior model or index version
Driver & failure mode

A general incident process (see Incident Response) usually is not built to ask whether a failure was the model's, the retrieval layer's, or the underlying data's, and that triage question determines the fix; extend the general process instead of building a second one. The rollback path depends directly on the versioning discipline in III.C. A system that cannot be switched off quickly cannot be governed, and secondary use is how a tool assessed for drafting internal notes ends up making customer-facing decisions nobody reviewed.

Judgment call

Is ISO 42001 certification worth pursuing

A decision with a cost attached. Don't default into it because the standard exists.

Worth it

Enterprise customers or regulators are asking for independent evidence of AI governance maturity and a policy document no longer satisfies them. Selling into procurement processes where a competitor's ISO 42001 badge is becoming a checkbox. The organization already holds ISO 27001, since the incremental cost and timeline drop substantially when the management-system muscle already exists.

Not worth it yet

A small AI footprint with a handful of internal use cases and no customer demanding third-party evidence. The controls in Domains III and IV aren't solidly in place yet. Certifying a management system before the underlying technical controls exist produces a certificate that doesn't reflect reality, and an auditor will eventually find that gap. NIST AI RMF, applied seriously and evidenced internally, covers most of the same ground without the audit cost, commonly $20,000 to $60,000 depending on scope, and the multi-year commitment.

Industry overlay

Healthcare and financial services

Sector rules that sit on top of the AIGP domains above. Any use case touching these should screen as Tier 3 or higher in the calculator below.

Healthcare
  • The FDA finalized its guidance on predetermined change control plans for AI-enabled device software in December 2024. A plan describes the planned modifications, the protocol for making them, and the impact assessment, so an authorized device can change within it without a new submission.[12]
  • Covered entities must identify and mitigate discrimination risk in patient care decision support tools, including AI, from May 1, 2025.[13] That maps to III.B bias testing and IV.C monitoring.
  • Prompts and outputs that contain PHI are PHI. The model provider needs a BAA, and logs that hold prompts inherit HIPAA retention and access rules.
Financial services
  • On April 17, 2026 the Federal Reserve, OCC, and FDIC replaced SR 11-7 with revised model risk guidance (SR 26-2). It is risk-based, aimed at banking organizations above $30 billion in assets, and leaves generative and agentic AI outside its scope.[14][15] Govern those with the controls on this page instead of assuming the model risk program covers them.
  • ECOA and Regulation B still require specific principal reasons for adverse credit decisions, however complex the model. The CFPB withdrew its 2022 and 2023 circulars on the topic in May 2025, but the underlying rule did not change.[16]
  • The EU AI Act lists creditworthiness assessment and life and health insurance risk pricing as high-risk uses in Annex III[17], with the provider and deployer duties described under II.C.

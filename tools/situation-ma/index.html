We're Going Through M&A

Whether you are buying, being bought, or merging, the technology work splits into two halves that most people run as one: the diligence that changes the price and terms, and the integration that determines whether the deal thesis survives contact with two real estates. This page sequences both and links to the pages that hold the detail.

Decisions with a date

What has to be decided, and by when

The clock runs from before the letter of intent through Day 100. Most of the value is decided before close and most of the damage happens in the first thirty days after it.

Questions to ask

Questions that reprice or stop a deal

Ask these in diligence, of the target's engineers and their cloud bill, not of their slides.

  • What percentage of the target's revenue depends on systems that fewer than three people understand
  • What does the target's cloud bill look like by service and by month, and how does it compare to what was presented
  • How many production incidents in the last year, how were they detected, and how long did recovery take
  • Which of the target's certifications are in scope for the systems that actually matter to the deal thesis
  • Where does customer and employee data physically live, under which entity, and which regulations follow it across the transaction
  • Which contracts have change-of-control clauses, and which vendors gain pricing leverage the day the deal closes
  • What is the target's technical debt, as a scored backlog rather than a paragraph of reassurance
  • Who on the target's side has been told the truth about their role post-close, and who has not
  • If the acquired product is to be replaced, what is the honest migration cost and timeline, and who has done one of these before
The trap

Diligence that stops at the slides

The most expensive M&A technology failure is a diligence exercise that reads the target's own description of itself and moves on, because the deal team is under time pressure and the technology findings are not seen as price-moving.

What it looks like

The target presents a clean architecture diagram, a SOC 2 Type II, and a roadmap. Post-close, the platform turns out to depend on a single contractor, the SOC 2 scope excluded the product that drove the valuation, and the integration cost is three times the model. None of this was hidden. Nobody asked for the artifact.

The counter-move

Treat diligence as an evidence exercise using the same discipline as Epistemology & Research Methods: for every claim, ask for the artifact, and treat the absence of one as the finding. Insist that specific technology findings appear in the price and the terms, not only in the integration plan. Then run Day 0 like an incident and the first 100 days like a program with a tracker, not a set of good intentions.

Outputs

What should exist at diligence close, Day 30, and Day 100

The deal model is a set of assumptions. These are the artifacts that turn it into a plan and then into a result.

CheckpointWhat exists
Diligence closeA scored technology diligence report with red flags classified as stop, reprice, or integration cost. A TIME map of the target's estate. A named key-person list with retention actions. A per-system converge, coexist, or replace decision with cost and date. Specific technology findings reflected in the price, the reps and warranties, or the escrow.
Day 30Day 0 completed without a customer-facing or identity outage. The combined executive role and decision-rights map published. Retention conversations held with every named individual. Change-of-control vendor contracts triaged. A single security monitoring view across both estates, even if crude.
Day 100An integration tracker reporting synergy realization against the deal model, honestly, with variance explained. The first replace or converge migration either underway with a plan or explicitly re-scoped. The board told which synergy assumptions were wrong and what replaces them. Nobody critical lost by surprise.

The reading list is sequenced. The role isn't a sequence.

Working the list above gets you moving. It isn't the full shape of what you're now responsible for.

The CTO Operating System maps the complete set of responsibility areas, technology strategy, architecture, team and organization, capital allocation, risk and security, executive relationships, delivery oversight, hands-on technical work, plus how the balance of time across them shifts as the organization scales. The epistemic lens

Reading what you're told, not just what you're shown

Every new CTO inherits a set of claims, stated with total confidence by people who may or may not have checked. See Epistemology & Research Methods for the full framework; this is what it looks like applied to your first thirty days.

What you'll hear What to check What silence tells you
"We don't have a technical debt problem" Ask for the artifact: an APM score, a tracked debt backlog No artifact means nobody's measured it, which is itself the finding
"Our deployments are reliable" Ask for the actual change failure rate and MTTR trend A confident claim with no number behind it is an opinion wearing a fact's clothing
"The team's in good shape" Check attrition, specifically regretted attrition, and referral rate Revealed preference beats a manager's felt sense every time
"This vendor's solid, never an issue" Ask when it was last tested: a real outage, a security review Untested reliability isn't verified reliability, it's just unlucky so far
"Security posture is strong" Ask for the artifact: last pen test, actual access review log A policy document is evidence of intent, not evidence of practice

None of this is about assuming bad faith. Most people repeating these claims believe them, and often inherited the belief from someone else who never checked either. The habit worth building in the first month isn't suspicion, it's asking for the artifact before accepting the sentence.

Outputs

What good looks like at 30, 60, and 90 days

The reading list above tells you what to study. This is what should exist by each checkpoint, not just what you should have read about.

Day 30 A real technology and application inventory, not the org chart's idea of one. A first-pass decision-rights map, even in draft. Key-person and succession risk named specifically, not assumed to be fine. A standing weekly cadence already running with both the CEO and the CFO.
Day 60 The application portfolio scored, not just inventoried. The Architecture Review Board holding its first real review. An honest read on what the culture rewards, distinct from what the values deck says. A first draft of where the organization's risk appetite sits, even where it's never been stated out loud.
Day 90 A stated technology strategy, not a set of quiet intentions in your own head. A first board narrative delivered, not just survived. A first real pass at capital allocation priorities, ranked, not just discussed.
Worth considering

The Wharton CTO Program

Not everything in the first year is solvable by reading. Some of it is solved by having peers going through the same thing at the same time.

Most of what's hard about the first year in this role isn't technical, it's the judgment calls nobody else in the building has faced before. A structured peer cohort helps precisely because everyone in it is working through the same category of problem in parallel, at a different company, which makes their notes worth comparing against your own. Wharton's Chief Technology Officer Program is a real, substantive program if you can make the time for it: cohort-based, built around applying the material to your own organization rather than abstract case studies, and the peer network it builds tends to outlast the program itself. Worth a look: Wharton Executive Education, Chief Technology Officer Program.

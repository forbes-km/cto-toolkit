Technical Debt Economics

Technical debt gets discussed in adjectives and funded by whoever argues best. This page replaces that with three numbers per item and a sequencing method simple enough to defend to a CFO: what it costs each year to leave it, what it costs once to fix it, and therefore which items to fund first within a real budget.

The calculator is a transparent, simplified version of the debt carry-cost optimizer in the private methodology. It ignores dependencies, team capacity, and partial remediation on purpose, so every ranking it produces can be explained. Inputs are estimates; the value is in the ranking, not the decimals.
Framework

Three numbers per item, or it is not a debt conversation

Technical debt discussions fail because they are conducted in adjectives. An item is not fundable until it has a carry cost, a remediation cost, and a risk exposure, each stated in dollars per year. Rough is fine. Absent is not.

NumberWhat it isHow to estimate it in an hour
Carry costWhat the debt costs every year it stays: slower delivery, extra operational toil, licensing for the thing you should have retired, the workaround everyone maintainsEngineering hours per month spent working around it, times a loaded hourly rate, times twelve, plus any hard costs (licenses, hosting, support contracts) that exist only because of it
Risk exposureThe expected annual cost of the incident, breach, or outage the debt makes more likelyProbability of a material event in a year, times the cost of that event (revenue, recovery, credits, regulatory). A 20 percent chance of a $500k event is $100k a year. Precision is not the point; the order of magnitude is
Remediation costWhat it costs to make it go away, onceEngineering hours to fix, migrate, or retire, times the loaded rate, plus any one-time hard costs. Include the cost of the parallel run or cutover if there is one

Carry cost plus risk exposure is the annual cost of doing nothing. Divided into remediation cost it gives the payback period. Items with a payback under a year are not debt decisions; they are operating decisions that have been avoided. Items with a payback over five years are usually not worth doing unless the risk exposure is dominated by a tail event that the organization has said it will not accept, which is what Technology Risk Appetite is for.

Method

How the sequencer decides

The logic is deliberately simple enough to explain to a CFO in one sentence: fund the items that remove the most annual cost per remediation dollar, within the budget, and carry the rest.

  1. Annual cost of doing nothingCarry cost plus risk exposure, for each item.
  2. RatioAnnual cost of doing nothing divided by remediation cost. This is the return on the remediation dollar. Items are ranked by it.
  3. Greedy allocation by yearWalk the ranked list. If an item fits in the current year's remaining budget, fund it that year. If it does not fit, carry it to the next year and keep walking so smaller items can use the remaining capacity. Repeat for the horizon.
  4. Carry cost paid meanwhileEvery year an item is not yet remediated, its annual cost of doing nothing is paid. The total across the horizon is reported so the sequence can be compared against a different budget.
  5. What it does not doIt does not model dependencies between items, capacity constraints per team, or the option of partial remediation. The private version does; this is the transparent core. A sequencing decision that cannot be explained is one the CFO will not fund twice.
Worked example

Reading a ranked portfolio

Load the sample portfolio above. The ranking usually surprises people in the same way.

What the ranking shows

The item everyone talks about, the large monolith rewrite, ranks low: a large remediation cost against a carry cost that is real but diffuse. The item nobody talks about, an unsupported database version with a meaningful breach probability, ranks first because its risk exposure dominates and its remediation is cheap. A small integration workaround that costs one engineer a week a month ranks second. Two of the top four items cost less than $50k to fix.

What to do with it

Fund the top of the list this quarter without a further business case; the numbers are the case. Take the monolith to the capital allocation conversation as a transform investment, not a debt item, because that is what it is. Report the annual cost of doing nothing for the unfunded remainder to the board as a number, once a year, so nobody can later say the risk was hidden.

Common pitfall

The debt budget that funds nothing in particular

Allocating twenty percent of capacity to debt without a ranked list produces twenty percent of capacity spent on whatever each team finds annoying.

What it looks like

Every sprint has a debt allocation. Engineers use it to refactor code they touch anyway. The unsupported database, which nobody enjoys working on, is untouched for three years. The allocation is visible in the roadmap, so leadership believes debt is being managed. The breach, when it comes, is on the database.

The counter-move

The debt allocation funds the ranked list, top down, and nothing else. Local refactoring is part of normal delivery, not the debt budget. The list is reviewed quarterly, new items need the three numbers to get on it, and the annual cost of doing nothing for the unfunded tail is reported alongside the items funded. See Engineering Metrics for the delivery signals that tell you whether the carry cost estimates were right.

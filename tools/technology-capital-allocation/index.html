Technology Capital Allocation

The standing discipline of allocating scarce capital across run, grow, and transform, security, technical debt, headcount, vendors, and reserve, and being able to answer what gets cut or funded first without re-deriving the answer from scratch each time.

Allocation categories

The buckets a technology budget breaks into, distinct enough that lumping them together hides tradeoffs that should be explicit.

1

The "$10M less" test

A question every CTO eventually gets asked, and the quality of the answer depends entirely on whether it was worked out in advance.

How to answer it well

Have a pre-ranked list, before the question is asked, of what gets cut first, second, third, with the real consequence of each cut stated plainly, not "we'll do more with less." This list should already exist; deriving it live under budget pressure produces worse decisions and reads as unprepared.

How it goes wrong

An across-the-board percentage cut applied uniformly to every team and category, which protects nothing that matters and reflects no actual prioritization, just avoids the discomfort of choosing.

2

The "$10M more" test

The less common but equally revealing version of the same question.

How to answer it well

A similarly pre-ranked list of what gets funded next, tied to the three-horizon split and stated expected outcomes, so a windfall gets deployed deliberately instead of triggering a scramble of proposals assembled after the fact.

How it goes wrong

The money gets absorbed by whichever team made the loudest case that quarter, evaluated against no consistent criteria, and disconnected from whatever priorities the rest of the portfolio was supposedly organized around.

3

What we'd stop if deciding again

The question almost no organization asks explicitly, and the one most likely to surface a real reallocation opportunity.

Why it matters

Sunk cost is the most common distortion in capital allocation. An investment that made sense eighteen months ago, under assumptions that have since changed, doesn't automatically still make sense, and few organizations ever explicitly re-ask the question rather than just continuing to fund what's already funded.

How to run it

Annually, as part of the operating cadence's yearly review, walk the active portfolio and ask of each item: knowing what we know now, would we start this today. Anything that gets a no needs a stated wind-down plan, not just an uncomfortable silence that lets it keep running by default.

4

Zero-based versus incremental budgeting

Two real methodologies for building next year's number, with different effort and different discipline built in.

Incremental

Start from last year's number and adjust up or down by category. Fast, low-effort, and the default almost everywhere. The real cost: an investment that stopped earning its place three years ago keeps getting funded because nobody re-examines the base, only the delta.

Zero-based

Every line starts at zero and has to be justified from scratch each cycle, the same discipline as "what we'd stop if deciding again" applied to the entire budget, not just flagged items. Genuinely more rigorous and genuinely more expensive in time; most organizations can't sustain it annually, but running it once every few years catches what incremental budgeting has been quietly protecting.

5

Allocating for uncertainty: options, not full commitments

Not every Horizon 3 bet needs a full-year commitment on day one. Staging the commitment is itself a capital allocation decision.

Practice

Fund the next decision point, not the whole initiative. A small spike that answers "is this technically feasible" preserves the option to scale up without committing the full budget before the uncertainty is resolved. This is real-options thinking applied to technology investment: the cost of the option is small, and it buys the right, not the obligation, to invest further.

Common failure mode

Approving a full-year budget for an unproven bet because a partial commitment feels indecisive. It isn't; it's how you avoid discovering in month nine that the bet was wrong after spending twelve months of budget finding out.

A worked allocation, illustrative

What the categories above look like assembled into a single year's number for a mid-size technology organization.

CategoryShareWhat's in it this year

Grow20%Two validated adjacent-market initiatives with a stated business case each

Security10%Evaluated on its own terms, not benchmarked against Grow's ROI bar
Technical debt8%A dedicated allocation, not whatever's left over after the categories above are funded
Vendors4%Reviewed against the criteria on Vendor Evaluation, renewal timing staggered deliberately

The specific percentages are illustrative, not a template to copy. What matters is that every category has a number and a reason, and the reserve line exists at all.

Checklist

Signs allocation discipline is missing

Patterns that show up when capital allocation is happening by inertia rather than by explicit decision.

  • No standing, pre-ranked list exists for what gets cut first under a budget reduction
  • Technical debt remediation gets whatever capacity is left over rather than a dedicated allocation
  • Security investment is evaluated against the same ROI bar as a feature, rather than as its own category with its own logic
  • An unplanned budget increase gets absorbed by whoever asks first rather than evaluated against the standing portfolio
  • No investment currently in the portfolio has been explicitly re-justified in the past twelve months
  • Every budget cycle is built incrementally off last year's number with no zero-based review, ever
Reference only. Category weights and typical splits vary enormously by company stage, industry, and competitive position; there is no universal correct ratio.

Epistemology & Research Methods

How we know what we know, and how to know enough, fast enough, to make a real decision. This is the discipline sitting underneath every diligence finding, postmortem root cause, and portfolio score elsewhere on this site, made explicit here instead of left implicit.

This borders on academic epistemology, but it isn't taught as theory here. It's the applied version already embedded in this toolkit: the "Confidence: High/Medium/Low" tags in Application Portfolio Management's technical debt scoring, the evidence-requirements lists in Technology Diligence, the "what was assessed, what wasn't" sections in every diligence memo template. This page names the pattern and shows where else it applies.
Reference

Academic concept, translated into practice

Epistemology is the study of what counts as knowledge and how it's justified. None of these ideas require a philosophy background to use; they're already implicit in good discovery work.

1

Why the research window should be bounded, not exhaustive

The instinct that more research is always better is wrong for organizational decisions in a way it isn't for a dissertation.

The actual question

Not "have we learned everything," but "have we learned enough to decide better than we would with no research at all, relative to what more research costs in time and opportunity." Confidence gains follow a diminishing curve: the fifth independent data point usually moves a conclusion more than the fiftieth does, once the pattern has already stabilized.

Where open-ended research fails

Academic research optimizes for near-total certainty over a timeframe measured in years, because the currency is being right, not being timely. A business decision has a clock the underlying situation doesn't wait for. A diligence report finished after the deal has already closed has close to zero value, no matter how thorough it is.

This is why confirmatory diligence runs 2-4 weeks and exclusivity diligence runs 4-8 weeks elsewhere on this site, not because more time wouldn't produce marginally more certainty, but because the decision has a deadline, and every framework here separates "what was assessed" from "what wasn't" rather than pretending the boundary doesn't exist.

Research methods, the actual toolkit

None of these require formal training. They're habits of checking a claim before treating it as settled.

MethodWhat it doesWhere it applies
TriangulationCross-check a claim against at least two independent sources before treating it as factA claim made in one interview versus the same claim confirmed by system access or a second interviewee who wasn't in the room for the first
Primary versus secondary evidenceDirect observation outranks a secondhand account of the observationReviewing the actual codebase versus accepting a description of the codebase's quality
Sampling logicA convenience sample, whoever happens to be available and willing, is not a representative sampleInterview subject selection; reference customers a vendor hand-picks versus a broader, independently chosen set
Disconfirmation-seekingActively look for evidence against the working conclusion, not just more evidence for itTesting whether an alternative explanation survives contact with the same evidence
Correlation versus causationA pattern in the data isn't automatically an explanation of the patternDistinguishing a triggering event from the underlying condition it merely exposed, in incident root-cause analysis

Confirmation bias is structural in discovery work, not a personal failing

It isn't a character flaw that shows up occasionally. The shape of discovery work invites it by default.

Why discovery invites it

A working hypothesis usually forms early, often within the first interview or the first system walkthrough, and every subsequent finding then gets unconsciously filtered through it. A compressed collection window raises the temptation to stop looking the moment a plausible story has assembled, since a plausible story feels like an answer.

Practice

Before writing the finding down, state the working conclusion explicitly and deliberately argue the opposite for a few minutes, even alone. It's a small, cheap step compared to the cost of a wrong root cause or a wrong severity rating that resurfaces the same problem next quarter.

4

Calibrated confidence beats false precision

A number presented as fact when it's actually a best estimate is a small dishonesty that compounds into bad decisions downstream.

Practice

Pair every material number with a confidence tag and a one-line reason: what would need to be true, or what evidence would need to arrive, for that confidence to move up or down. A range with a stated confidence level is more useful and more honest than a single unqualified figure.

Common failure mode

Dropping the confidence tag because an unqualified number feels more authoritative in a report or a board deck. This trades honesty for the appearance of certainty, and the decision built on top of it inherits precision the underlying research never actually supported.

Applied

Where this already lives across the toolkit

None of this is new to this site; it's the pattern underneath several pages, named explicitly here for the first time.

  • Technology Diligence: evidence-requirements lists and the discipline of separating what was confirmed from what was taken on management's word
  • Technical Debt Assessment: a five-level severity scale and a confidence rating (High/Medium/Low) attached to every dollar figure, so a report never travels with more certainty than the underlying work supports
  • Application Portfolio Management: the explicit caution that Business Value scores drift toward inflation when the scorer is the system's own owner, a direct application of source bias
  • Incident Response: the distinction between a triggering event and the underlying condition it exposed, which is the correlation-versus-causation problem applied to postmortems
  • Vendor Evaluation: the caution against accepting only the references a vendor's sales team hand-picked, a direct sampling-bias correction
Checklist

Signs research quality is weak, regardless of how long it took

Time spent is not the same thing as rigor. These patterns show up in reports that took weeks and reports that took an afternoon alike.

  • Findings state conclusions with no evidence source attached
  • A single interview or a single document is treated with the same confidence as a cross-referenced, triangulated finding
  • No visible effort was made to find evidence against the working conclusion before it was written down
  • Numbers are presented without a confidence range or a stated basis
  • The research window kept extending without anyone asking whether the additional time was actually buying more confidence, or just more pages

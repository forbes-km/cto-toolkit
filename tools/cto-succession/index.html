CTO Leadership & Succession

Who runs things if the CTO disappears for ninety days, and more broadly, where institutional knowledge and leadership readiness sit versus where the org chart says they sit.

This is the internal-organization counterpart to the key-person risk assessment on Technology Diligence, applied to your own team rather than an acquisition target's.
Reference

The questions a real succession plan has to answer

Not a document exercise. If any of these has no honest answer, that's the finding.

  • Who can run engineering if the CTO is out for ninety days?
  • Who can represent technology to the board?
  • Who can run a major incident from start to finish?
  • Who owns architecture, in practice rather than by title?
  • Who is willing to challenge the CTO's thinking, and does the CTO listen to them?
  • Where is institutional knowledge concentrated, and what happens to it if that person leaves?
1

The bus-factor question, applied to leadership

The same discipline already applied to code ownership, extended to people in leadership roles.

Practice

Name the specific individuals whose departure would create a multi-month gap in a specific capability, not a generic sense that "the team is strong." Bus-factor analysis works the same way for leadership as it does for a codebase: identify where there's exactly one person who can do something critical.

Common failure mode

Assuming seniority or title implies succession readiness. A VP title doesn't mean that person could credibly represent technology to the board tomorrow if asked to do it without warning.

2

Building the bench deliberately

A bench that exists by accident usually turns out not to exist at all when it's tested.

Practice

Give a successor candidate real exposure before they need it: board attendance, incident command rotation, ownership of an architecture review. The first time someone does something this consequential shouldn't be during an actual emergency.

Common failure mode

Protecting a strong deputy from visibility because delegating feels slower than doing it yourself in the moment, which guarantees the organization has no real bench when it's tested, exactly when a real bench would matter most.

3

The leadership succession heatmap

A small artifact that makes the honest state of succession visible instead of assumed.

For each critical leadership function, name the current owner, the identified successor, and their readiness: ready now, six to twelve months out, or not yet started. An honest "none identified" in several rows is the finding the exercise exists to surface, not a sign the document was filled out incorrectly.

Checklist

Signs succession is assumed rather than real

Patterns that show up when succession planning exists as an idea but not as a tested plan.

  • No one has been identified who could run engineering for ninety days without the CTO
  • The people who could represent technology to the board have never done it
  • Succession candidates have title and tenure but no real exposure to the responsibilities they'd inherit
  • The heatmap, if it exists, hasn't been updated since the last reorg
  • A single individual's departure would create a gap the organization has no plan for
Reference only. Succession depth should scale with organizational risk tolerance and the actual cost of a leadership gap, not a fixed checklist.

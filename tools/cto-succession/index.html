CTO Leadership & Succession

Who runs things if the CTO disappears for ninety days, and more broadly, where institutional knowledge and leadership readiness sit versus where the org chart says they sit.

This is the internal-organization counterpart to the key-person risk assessment on Technology Diligence, applied to your own team rather than an acquisition target's.
Reference

The questions a succession plan has to answer

If any of these has no answer, that is the finding, and it matters more than the document.

  • Who can run engineering if the CTO is out for ninety days?
  • Who can represent technology to the board?
  • Who can run a major incident from start to finish?
  • Who owns architecture, in practice rather than by title?
  • Who is willing to challenge the CTO's thinking, and does the CTO listen to them?
  • Where is institutional knowledge concentrated, and what happens to it if that person leaves?
1

The bus-factor question, applied to leadership

The same discipline already applied to code ownership, extended to people in leadership roles.

Practice

Name the specific individuals whose departure would create a multi-month gap in a specific capability. A generic sense that "the team is strong" names nobody. Bus-factor analysis works the same way for leadership as it does for a codebase: identify where there's exactly one person who can do something critical.

Common failure mode

Assuming seniority or title implies succession readiness. A VP title doesn't mean that person could credibly represent technology to the board tomorrow if asked to do it without warning.

2

Building the bench deliberately

A bench that exists by accident usually turns out not to exist at all when it's tested.

Practice

Give a successor candidate exposure before they need it: board attendance, incident command rotation, ownership of an architecture review. The first time someone does something this consequential shouldn't be during an emergency.

Common failure mode

Protecting a strong deputy from visibility because delegating feels slower than doing it yourself in the moment, which guarantees the organization has no bench when it's tested.

3

The leadership succession heatmap

A small artifact that makes the state of succession visible instead of assumed.

For each critical leadership function, name the current owner, the identified successor, and their readiness: ready now, six to twelve months out, or not yet started. A "none identified" in several rows is the finding the exercise exists to surface. It does not mean the document was filled out incorrectly.

5

Emergency versus planned succession are different playbooks

Confusing the two produces a plan that works for neither scenario.

Emergency, no notice

The interim owner named on the heatmap steps in immediately. Communication goes out within 24 to 48 hours, framed around continuity. The board is looped in the same day. The goal is stability. Naming a permanent replacement can wait weeks; the stability signal can't.

Planned, with notice

Use the notice period deliberately. The successor takes on visible responsibility gradually across the transition window rather than all at once on the last day. The departing CTO makes introductions, to the board, key customers, key vendors, personally, rather than leaving the successor to build those relationships cold after the fact.

6

What the board needs to know, and when

Enough to avoid surprise. The detailed heatmap stays internal.

Standing awareness

The board should know a succession plan exists and who the interim contact would be, discussed at least annually as part of a broader risk conversation, the same cadence as the risk appetite review, rather than sprung on them during a departure.

Common failure mode

The board's first exposure to "who runs technology if the CTO leaves" is the day the CTO leaves, which is exactly the worst moment to be building consensus on an interim leader while also managing the departure itself.

8

The emergency succession pack

Prepared in advance, held by the CEO and general counsel, reviewed annually. It exists so the first 48 hours of an unplanned absence are execution, not discovery.

  • Interim authority letter. The named interim leader and the scope of decisions they can make, pre-approved by the CEO
  • Access continuity. How break-glass credentials are recovered, with at least two authorized holders and a tested procedure
  • In-flight commitments. The top ten commitments with owners and dates: launches, contract renewals, audits, regulatory deadlines
  • Relationship map. Key customers, vendors, auditors, and board members, with who should contact each and what they need to hear
  • Top risks. The current top risks from the risk register and their owners
  • Communication drafts. A holding message for the team, key customers, and the board, framed around continuity

Checklist

Signs succession is assumed rather than planned

Patterns that show up when succession planning exists as an idea but not as a tested plan.

  • No one has been identified who could run engineering for ninety days without the CTO
  • The people who could represent technology to the board have never done it
  • Succession candidates have title and tenure but no exposure to the responsibilities they'd inherit
  • The heatmap, if it exists, hasn't been updated since the last reorg
  • A single individual's departure would create a gap the organization has no plan for
  • The board has never heard the succession plan discussed outside of a departure
  • Break-glass or organization-owner credentials have a single holder
  • No emergency succession pack exists, so an unplanned absence starts with a search for passwords and commitments
Reference only. The truck factor method is from Avelino, Passos, Hora, and Valente, A novel approach for estimating truck factors (2016). Succession depth should scale with organizational risk tolerance and the cost of a leadership gap rather than follow a fixed checklist.

Incident Response

A blameless, repeatable incident process: detection through postmortem follow-through, with the specific failure modes that turn a manageable incident into a chaotic one.

General incident response practice, not incident response for AI-specific failures. See the AI Governance Map for the AI-specific incident triage extension, which builds on this process rather than replacing it.
Reference

Severity classification

Defined ahead of time, not decided in the moment of an actual incident when judgment is already under pressure.

SeverityDefinitionResponse
SEV1Full outage or data breach affecting all or most customersImmediate all-hands, incident commander assigned within minutes, executive notification immediate
SEV2Significant degradation or a subset of customers fully affectedIncident commander assigned, core responders paged, executive notification within the hour
SEV3Minor degradation, workaround available, limited customer impactOwning team handles it, tracked but no incident command structure invoked
SEV4No customer impact, internal-only issue caught before it matteredLogged, addressed in normal workflow, no incident process needed
1

Detection & triage

The first few minutes determine whether the rest of the response is calm or chaotic.

Practice

A single, unambiguous entry point for declaring an incident, one command or one button, not a debate about which Slack channel to post in while the outage continues. Whoever notices first declares; severity gets assigned or corrected within minutes, not negotiated at length before response starts.

Common failure mode

Multiple people independently investigating the same issue in different channels, duplicating effort and fragmenting the picture of what's actually happening, because there was no single place established ahead of time to converge on.

2

Incident command

One person owns the incident's coordination. They are explicitly not the person fixing it.

Practice

The incident commander's job is coordination and communication, not debugging. They track the timeline, decide when to escalate, and are the single point deciding when the incident is resolved. This role rotates and is trained in advance, not improvised by whoever happens to be senior and available.

Common failure mode

The most senior engineer in the room becomes both incident commander and lead debugger simultaneously. Both jobs suffer: the fix takes longer because they're context-switching to answer status questions, and communication suffers because they're heads-down in logs.

3

Communication cadence

What gets said, to whom, and how often, especially while the cause is still unknown.

Practice

A fixed cadence (every 15 to 30 minutes for a SEV1) with an update even when the update is "still investigating, no new information." Silence is worse than an uninformative update, since stakeholders fill silence with worse assumptions than reality usually warrants.

Common failure mode

Going quiet while "actually working the problem," which is defensible engineering instinct and terrible stakeholder management. The absence of updates reads as either incompetence or a bigger problem than what's actually happening.

4

Resolution & recovery

Declaring an incident over is a decision, not a moment that happens automatically when the graphs look normal again.

Practice

Resolution requires the incident commander's explicit call, based on a defined bar (metrics back to baseline for a sustained period, not just a momentary dip back to normal), and a documented rollback or mitigation, not just "it seems to have stopped."

Common failure mode

Calling it resolved the moment the immediate symptom disappears, then having it recur an hour later because the underlying cause was never actually addressed, only its most visible symptom.

5

Blameless postmortem

The written record that turns an incident into an organizational asset instead of just a bad memory.

Practice

A timeline of what happened, what was known at each decision point (not what's known now in hindsight), contributing factors framed as systemic rather than individual, and concrete action items with owners and due dates. "Blameless" means analyzing the system that allowed the error, not avoiding naming what happened.

Common failure mode

A postmortem that either assigns individual blame (which teaches people to hide problems rather than surface them next time) or is so carefully blame-free it avoids naming any specific decision point, producing generic action items nobody's accountable for actually finishing.

6

Follow-through

The step most postmortem processes skip, which is why the same class of incident recurs.

Practice

Action items from postmortems tracked in the same system as normal engineering work, with a review cadence (monthly is common) checking which ones actually shipped versus which quietly died in a backlog. An incident review process without a follow-through audit is theater.

Common failure mode

Action items live only in the postmortem document itself, never entered into the team's actual work tracking system, and six months later nobody remembers they existed when the same failure mode recurs.

Reference only. Adapt severity definitions and cadence to your organization's actual scale and customer commitments before treating this as a runbook.

Org Design

Team topology patterns and interaction modes for structuring engineering organizations, plus the span-of-control question that comes up in nearly every reorg conversation.

Team Topologies concepts adapted and condensed here; the original book by Matthew Skelton and Manuel Pais goes considerably deeper and is worth reading directly for a full reorganization, not just this summary.
Framework

Four team types

Adapted from the Team Topologies model. Most organizational dysfunction traces back to a team playing a type it wasn't set up to play, not to the people in it.

Stream-aligned

Owns a full slice of a business capability end to end, from code to production, with minimal dependency on other teams for a normal change.

Anti-pattern: a "stream-aligned" team that still needs three other teams' sign-off to ship, which means it isn't actually stream-aligned yet.

Platform

Provides internal services (infrastructure, deployment tooling, shared data platforms) that stream-aligned teams consume as a product, reducing their cognitive load.

Anti-pattern: a platform team that requires a ticket and a two-week wait for anything, which just relocates the bottleneck instead of removing it.

Enabling

Temporary, specialist expertise (security, a new technology adoption) that works itself out of a job by teaching stream-aligned teams the skill, then moves on.

Anti-pattern: an enabling team that becomes a permanent dependency because it never actually transfers the capability, just keeps doing the work itself.

Complicated subsystem

Owns a piece of the system that genuinely requires deep specialist knowledge (a pricing engine, a matching algorithm) that most stream-aligned engineers shouldn't need to hold in their heads.

Anti-pattern: labeling a team "complicated subsystem" to justify a silo that formed for organizational-politics reasons rather than genuine technical complexity.
Interaction modes

How teams actually work together

The team type tells you what a team owns. The interaction mode tells you how two teams should work together, and mismatched modes are where coordination actually breaks down.

Collaboration

Two teams working closely together for a bounded period, typically to discover something neither could figure out alone. High communication overhead by design; should have a defined end date, not become the permanent operating mode.

X-as-a-Service

One team consumes another's output through a clean, well-documented interface with minimal ongoing communication needed, the way you'd consume a well-run internal API. This is the default target state for a platform team's relationship with stream-aligned teams.

A team stuck permanently in collaboration mode with another team it should be consuming as a service is a strong signal the interface between them was never actually built. That's usually a platform maturity gap, not a people problem.
Span of control

How many people report to a manager, and when that number stops working

Not a single right number, but the failure modes on either side are predictable.

Too wide

A manager with 12 or more direct reports typically cannot do meaningful one-on-ones, career development, or real performance calibration for each person. Reports get triaged rather than actually managed, and the manager becomes a bottleneck for any decision requiring context on a specific person's work.

Too narrow

A manager with 2 or 3 reports for an extended period is usually a sign of an org chart built around a person rather than a function, and it adds a coordination layer that produces slower decisions without a corresponding benefit in depth of support.

6 to 8 direct reports is a reasonable default for a working manager who still does some individual contribution; 8 to 10 for a manager who doesn't. Adjust down for teams with junior engineers needing more development time, up for a team of senior staff who mostly need to be unblocked, not developed.
Reference only. Organizational structure decisions depend heavily on company stage, culture, and specific team composition; treat this as a starting vocabulary, not a template to apply mechanically.

Build vs Buy

The criteria that decide a build-versus-buy debate, the differentiation test people misapply most often, and the maintenance cost that rarely makes it into the initial proposal.

1

The differentiation test

The single most misapplied criterion, usually stretched to justify a decision already made for other reasons.

Differentiation

A capability customers explicitly cite as why they chose you over an alternative, or one that materially changes your unit economics versus using a vendor. If you removed it, you'd lose deals or margin specifically because of its absence.

Preference dressed as differentiation

"We want more control" or "the vendor's roadmap doesn't match ours exactly" are legitimate preferences. They aren't differentiation, and building an entire internal system to solve a preference is usually a worse trade than living with 90 percent fit from a vendor.

2

The maintenance question people skip

Build decisions are evaluated on initial build cost far more often than on the ongoing cost of owning it for the next five years.

What gets counted

Initial development time, initial infrastructure cost, time to first working version, the numbers that show up in a project proposal and are easy to estimate.

What gets missed

Security patching indefinitely, keeping pace with a changing regulatory or integration landscape, the bus-factor risk when the one engineer who deeply understands it leaves, and the opportunity cost of the team's attention being split across build-and-maintain instead of focused on the differentiator.

Maintenance is the dominant cost of owned software. The research summarized by Robert Glass in Facts and Fallacies of Software Engineering puts it at 40 to 80 percent of lifecycle cost, around 60 percent on average.[1] A build proposal whose five-year view shows maintenance as a small line has left out most of the cost; the worked example below shows the effect.

3

The middle option nobody names: partner or extend

Build-versus-buy is usually presented as binary. A frequently better third option gets skipped.

What it looks like

Buying a vendor platform for the undifferentiated 80 percent and building a thin, differentiated layer on top of it via the vendor's API or extension points, rather than building the whole stack from scratch or accepting 100 percent of the vendor's opinion.

When to reach for it

When the differentiation is narrow and specific (a particular workflow, a particular integration, a particular scoring model) rather than the whole domain. This is very often the right answer and gets dismissed early because the binary framing never surfaced it as an option.

4

Reversing the decision later

Build-versus-buy isn't necessarily a permanent choice. The sequencing of which comes first matters as much as the eventual answer.

Buy first, build later if it proves out

Start with a vendor to get to market fast. If the capability proves out as differentiation worth owning, build a deliberate replacement once you understand the requirements from having lived with the vendor's version, rather than guessing at them upfront.

The trap of building first to keep optionality

Building something in-house "to stay flexible" before you know whether it's a differentiator usually produces a worse version of what a mature vendor already built, at a cost that vendor already amortized across many customers instead of just you.

5

Vendor risk beyond lock-in

The framework above asks whether you can leave a vendor. It's an incomplete question on its own.

The fuller question

Lock-in asks "can I leave." It's worth also asking what happens if the vendor leaves you: acquisition risk, a pricing change once you're dependent, a wind-down of the specific product you rely on. See Vendor Evaluation for scoring this directly.

Where this bites

A string of individually reasonable buy decisions, each cleared on its own lock-in and exit-cost merits, that quietly concentrate several important capabilities behind the same vendor. A single vendor's bad quarter becomes every one of those capabilities' bad quarter at once.

Exit terms to negotiate before signing

The cost of leaving is set at signature, when you have the most leverage and the least motivation to think about it. See SaaS Contract & Renewal Negotiation for the full clause set.

TermWhat to ask for
Data exportComplete export in a documented, machine-readable format, on request and at termination, at no additional fee
Transition assistanceA defined period after termination, commonly 30 to 90 days, with continued access and support at existing rates
Renewal price protectionA cap on renewal increases, tied to an index or a fixed percentage
Product end of lifeWritten notice well in advance, 12 months is common, if the product or a feature you depend on is discontinued

Checklist

Signs the decision wasn't tested

Patterns that show up when a build-versus-buy call was made to justify a preference rather than worked through.

  • Differentiation was asserted rather than tested against "would we lose deals or margin without this"
  • No five-year total cost of ownership estimate exists for the build option, only the initial build cost
  • The partner-or-extend middle option was never evaluated, only a binary build-or-buy framing
  • No plan exists for reversing the decision later if the underlying assumption turns out wrong
  • Vendor concentration across other decisions wasn't considered, only this one decision's lock-in risk in isolation
  • The buy option was evaluated without negotiating exit terms: data export, transition assistance, and renewal caps
Reference only. The maintenance range is from Robert Glass, Facts and Fallacies of Software Engineering (2002), summarizing earlier studies. Apply the criteria against your own cost and capability data rather than the illustrative numbers on this page.

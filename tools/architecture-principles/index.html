Architecture Principles Library

Fifteen principles with the reasoning, the trade-off it forces, a worked example of how it shows up in a decision, and the signs it's being misapplied.

Fifteen principles that have held up across real engagements. Adopt half, adapt some, reject others, a smaller set held seriously is worth more than a longer list held loosely. Track exceptions: zero exceptions means a principle is unused or undertested, too many means it's no longer a principle.
Framework

What makes a principle worth having

Most architecture principles are bad: generic ("we will be agile"), self-serving ("we prefer our favorite technology"), or untestable ("we will deliver business value"). A useful principle has four properties.

  • Names a specific trade-off the organization has chosen
  • Is defensible against its opposite
  • Produces different decisions than its absence would
  • Comes with examples of when it was applied and what was given up as a result
Strategic
P1

Buy before build, build only where differentiation justifies the cost

Rationale: Most software the organization needs has already been built by someone else. Building it again produces ongoing maintenance costs for software that doesn't differentiate the business.

Trade-off: Loss of control over roadmap and feature set. Accepted for commodity capabilities, rejected for differentiating ones.

Worked example: Customer identity management is commodity, adopt a commercial platform. Fraud detection for a specific transaction pattern is differentiating, build it.

Signs of misapplication: Everything gets classified as differentiating to justify building. The cure is making someone name the differentiation concretely.

P2

Pay down technical debt continuously, not in campaigns

Rationale: Debt accumulates continuously. Campaigns to address it are always disruptive, under-scoped, and insufficient.

Trade-off: Feature velocity appears lower in the short term because capacity is visibly allocated away from new work.

Worked example: Delivery teams allocate 15 to 20 percent of sprint capacity to debt items, owned by engineering, informed to product.

Signs of misapplication: The allocation becomes theoretical; sprint reviews consistently show 100 percent feature work.

P3

Make reversible decisions quickly; reserve slow deliberation for the irreversible

Rationale: Treating all decisions with the same process produces either dangerous speed on the irreversible or pointless bureaucracy on the reversible.

Trade-off: Some decisions are harder to classify than the principle implies; the organization has to develop judgment about what's reversible in its context.

Worked example: A logging library choice is reversible, the team picks and moves. A primary data platform is not reversible within a reasonable timeframe, it goes through review with ADR documentation.

Signs of misapplication: Teams classify inconvenient decisions as irreversible to defer them, or reversible to avoid oversight. The test: what does it cost in time and money to change this in 18 months?

P4

Prefer loose coupling between autonomous teams over tight integration

Rationale: Tight coupling between systems produces tight coupling between the teams that own them, and destroys both the system's and the team's autonomy over time.

Trade-off: Loose coupling costs more to build initially; interfaces take design effort that a shared database doesn't.

Worked example: The Order team calls the Customer service's API rather than reading the Customer database directly. The Customer team can refactor storage without coordinating with Order.

Signs of misapplication: Teams paper over coupling with facades that still expose the underlying structure. Test: if the upstream team changes its implementation, does the downstream code break?

Technical
P5

Failure is the default state; design for it

Rationale: At any non-trivial scale, something is always failing. Systems designed assuming components work experience their first real failure as an outage.

Trade-off: Upfront complexity; designing for failure takes real thought and adds code paths.

Worked example: The order placement service, when the fraud service is unavailable, falls back to rule-based scoring rather than refusing orders. Flagged orders get deferred review.

Signs of misapplication: Retries and fallbacks added without thinking through cascade effects; retry storms that take down dependencies are worse than the original failure.

P6

Observability is a first-class concern, not an afterthought

Rationale: Systems not designed to be observed are systems whose behavior is a mystery in production, and the cost of instrumenting retroactively is higher than instrumenting from the start.

Trade-off: Instrumentation is real code that must be maintained; noisy instrumentation is worse than none.

Worked example: Every service produces request logs with correlation IDs, metrics on the operations that matter most, and traces covering cross-service flows, specified alongside functional requirements.

Signs of misapplication: Teams log everything, measure nothing, and produce dashboards nobody reads. The cure: for each piece of instrumentation, ask what decision it supports.

P7

Prefer convention over configuration; prefer explicit over implicit

Rationale: Configuration without convention produces infinite surface area. Convention without explicit seams produces invisible magic.

Trade-off: The organization must invest in establishing and evolving conventions, ongoing work that drifts into inconsistency without it.

Worked example: All services log in a common format and expose health/metrics on conventional paths. A service deviating for regulatory reasons documents why, explicitly.

Signs of misapplication: Conventions are stated but not enforced; new services do whatever their authors prefer.

P8

Automate what gets done more than twice

Rationale: Manual work is error-prone, non-repeatable, and invisible. The person doing it is a bottleneck and a knowledge concentration point.

Trade-off: Automation costs more than doing the thing once; the 'more than twice' rule calibrates when that cost is worth paying.

Worked example: New developer environment setup was a 3-day manual process. After the second hire it became a script; after the fifth, a one-click pipeline.

Signs of misapplication: Automation becomes an end in itself; teams automate one-off work and produce scripts no one else uses.

Data
P9

Data has an authoritative source; other copies are derived, not parallel

Rationale: Parallel sources of truth always diverge, usually not discovered until a reconciliation problem surfaces in production.

Trade-off: The authoritative source becomes a hard dependency for every downstream consumer; its availability matters more than in a distributed-truth design.

Worked example: Customer contact information is authoritative in the CRM; Marketing receives it via streaming replica; Billing queries CRM directly. Nobody has their own editable copy.

Signs of misapplication: Two teams claim authoritative ownership of overlapping data; reconciliation scripts appear. The cure is naming a single owner and migrating the other to consumer status.

P10

Data models reflect domain reality, not system convenience

Rationale: Data outlives systems. A model shaped by today's storage technology will be wrong when the technology changes, which it will.

Trade-off: Domain-driven models are sometimes less efficient for the current storage technology than technology-optimized ones would be.

Worked example: The order model captures business concepts, order, line item, party, event, because those concepts are stable, regardless of the underlying storage engine.

Signs of misapplication: The model has fields named for technical conventions (is_deleted, legacy_status_code) that pollute the domain. The cure is separating domain model from storage model.

P11

Classify data by sensitivity before any other decision

Rationale: Retrofitting sensitivity controls is more expensive than building them in; systems that move data without awareness of sensitivity produce the gaps that show up in breach postmortems.

Trade-off: Classification requires ongoing organizational discipline, every system handling data must know what classification applies.

Worked example: Customer purchase history is classified Restricted. A log entry including purchase history is subject to Restricted-level controls even if the producing system wouldn't otherwise be.

Signs of misapplication: Classification exists on paper but isn't wired into actual controls. The cure is instrumenting classification into specific enforcement (encryption, access logging).

People & process
P12

The team that builds runs, or the runbook doesn't exist

Rationale: Build-only teams produce systems that are hard to operate because they don't experience the operational cost; run-only teams receive systems they can't confidently change.

Trade-off: Asking build teams to operate adds real ongoing work; the alternative cost of operational-ownership confusion is larger, but this isn't a free lunch.

Worked example: A product team building a customer-facing service is on-call for it. If responsibility transfers to a central ops team, the transfer includes validated runbooks and paired on-call for 90 days.

Signs of misapplication: Teams claim to operate but delegate incidents upward constantly. The cure is staffing and training to the operational responsibility, not softening the principle.

P13

Decisions are recorded; rationale is part of the decision

Rationale: The reasoning behind a decision is usually the most valuable artifact, and the one most frequently lost.

Trade-off: Writing a good rationale takes real effort, most teams underestimate how much.

Worked example: An event-driven integration decision is recorded as an ADR including alternatives considered, why the chosen path won, and what would trigger revisiting it.

Signs of misapplication: ADRs exist but are written as announcements rather than reasoning. 'We will use X' with no explanation of what was rejected is a memo, not an ADR.

P14

Small, stable teams outperform large, dynamic ones for complex work

Rationale: Architectural work compounds on team context. A team that's operated a system for two years makes better decisions about it than one assembled three weeks ago.

Trade-off: Stable teams are harder to scale up rapidly for new initiatives; the organization has to plan for this rather than throwing people at problems.

Worked example: The team owning customer identity has a core of four engineers with two-plus years on it. New engineers onboard over three to six months, not three weeks.

Signs of misapplication: Team composition changes frequently, no one's been on the team more than six months. The cure is slowing the churn, not asking teams to document more.

P15

Measure what matters; do not optimize what is merely easy to measure

Rationale: Goodhart's law applies to architecture: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. Easy proxies produce organizations that look healthy while failing at what matters.

Trade-off: Measures that matter are harder to collect and slower to move; the temptation to substitute easy proxies is constant.

Worked example: Instead of 'reviews conducted,' measure 'percentage of significant initiatives with early architecture engagement,' which requires judgment but reflects reality.

Signs of misapplication: Dashboards show green metrics while the function is widely considered unhelpful. The cure: ask what consumers of the work would say is getting better or worse.

When principles conflict

Conflict is a feature, not a bug

Principles conflict. The conflict surfaces a genuine trade-off; three approaches help resolve it in practice.

  • State the specific conflict. "These sometimes conflict" is useless; "P1 conflicts with P4 here because the commercial product creates tight coupling with our existing systems" is resolvable.
  • Identify which principle the context prioritizes. A regulatory system might privilege data classification over decision speed; a greenfield product might privilege the reverse. Neither is wrong, the context matters.
  • Record the resolution in the ADR. Future decisions in similar contexts have precedent to draw on.
Reference only. Adopt what's defensible in your specific context; a principle nobody references in a real decision is wall decoration, not a principle.

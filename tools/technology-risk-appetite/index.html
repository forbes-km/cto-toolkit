Technology Risk Appetite

Explicitly defining which risks the organization won't accept, will accept temporarily, will monitor, and requires board or CEO visibility for, rather than leaving risk tolerance implicit and rediscovering it after something has already gone wrong.

This is the cross-cutting frame. Category-specific detail lives on Security Program, Disaster Recovery, Data Governance, and Vendor Evaluation. See Governance, Risk & Compliance for how risk acceptance connects to a broader control and compliance program.
Reference

The five tiers

The same tiers apply regardless of category, security, resilience, architecture, technical debt, vendors, AI, regulatory exposure, or concentration risk.

TierDefinitionWho can accept it
Will not acceptDownside unacceptable regardless of likelihood: unencrypted regulated data at rest, no tested recovery plan for a revenue-critical systemNo one; this is a standing prohibition, not a judgment call
Will accept temporarilyKnown, tracked, with a remediation date and a named ownerThe accountable owner from the decision-rights model, with the remediation plan documented
Will monitorAcceptable at current severity, tracked in case that changesThe team or function closest to the risk, without escalation, as long as it stays within stated bounds
Requires board visibilitySeverity or category crosses a stated materiality thresholdSurfaced regardless of whether it's currently being actively managed well
Requires CEO approvalAcceptance could affect company viability or a public commitmentThe CEO specifically, not delegated downward regardless of technical familiarity
1

Why implicit risk tolerance is worse than a strict written one

A conservative appetite stated clearly beats a permissive one left unstated, because the unstated version isn't a tolerance, it's an accident waiting to be discovered.

What an explicit appetite does

Gives engineers and managers a standing answer to "is this acceptable" without escalating every individual judgment call, and gives the CTO a defensible basis for a decision rather than an after-the-fact justification assembled once something has already gone wrong.

What implicit tolerance produces

Risk tolerance gets set retroactively, by whoever made the loudest case for or against a specific risk at the time, and the same category of risk gets treated inconsistently across teams depending entirely on who happened to be asking.

2

The board-visibility threshold

The line that determines whether a risk stays a team-level judgment call or becomes something the board has to know about.

What crosses it

A materiality standard stated in advance: dollar exposure above a specific threshold, any risk with potential regulatory or public-disclosure consequence, anything that could affect a stated company commitment. The threshold has to be a number or a specific condition, not a feeling.

The trap

Defining materiality vaguely enough ("significant risk") that it becomes a judgment call made under pressure in the moment, right when the incentive to under-report is highest and the person making the call has the least distance from the situation.

3

A worked example, applying the tiers

What the five-tier model looks like against a real, common scenario rather than an abstract description.

The finding: a known vulnerability in a legacy authentication library, requiring a multi-month migration to remediate properly, discovered during a routine dependency audit.

Will not accept? No. Exploiting it requires a specific, unlikely combination of internal network access and a second, unrelated compromise; it doesn't meet the "unacceptable regardless of likelihood" bar on its own.

Will accept temporarily. Yes, with a remediation date tied to the actual migration timeline, a named owner (head of security), and interim compensating controls (additional network segmentation around the affected service) documented alongside the acceptance, not just the acceptance itself.

Board visibility? Depends on whether the affected system handles regulated data. If it does, the materiality threshold is crossed regardless of how contained the practical exploit path looks, because the threshold is about data category, not the CTO's confidence in the mitigation.

4

When the remediation date passes

The moment that reveals whether "will accept temporarily" was a real tier or just a way to defer a hard conversation indefinitely.

What should happen

The accepted risk gets reviewed again with the same rigor as the original acceptance. If the date passes without remediation and without a re-justified new date, it escalates automatically to the next tier up, board visibility, rather than quietly receiving a new date stamped on it by the same person who missed the last one.

Common failure mode

A risk accepted a year ago, now on its third quietly extended remediation date, still sitting in the same tier with no escalation and no accountability for the pattern of missed dates itself, which is a worse signal than the original risk.

5

Revisiting the appetite as the company changes

The appetite itself has a shelf life, distinct from any individual risk decision made under it.

Practice

What was reasonable to tolerate at a five-person startup with no customers often becomes "will not accept" the moment the company signs its first enterprise customer with a security addendum, or crosses into handling regulated data for the first time. Revisit the appetite itself, not just individual risk decisions made under it, on the same annual cadence as the strategy reset.

Common failure mode

The appetite document was written once, early, by whoever was CTO at the time, and nobody revisits the actual thresholds as the company's regulatory exposure, customer base, or public profile changes around it, so the document quietly stops matching the business it's supposed to govern.

Checklist

Signs risk appetite is undefined despite feeling settled

An organization can feel like it has a consistent approach to risk while having none written down anywhere.

  • Risk acceptance decisions aren't documented anywhere, so no one can show why a given risk was judged acceptable six months later
  • The same category of risk gets treated differently by different teams with no stated reason
  • No materiality threshold exists for what triggers board visibility
  • "We'll accept this for now" risks have no remediation date or owner attached
  • The CTO has never stated, in writing, what the organization will not accept regardless of cost or convenience
  • A remediation date has slipped more than once on the same accepted risk with no automatic escalation
Reference only. Risk thresholds and escalation requirements should reflect your organization's actual regulatory exposure and board expectations, not a generic default.

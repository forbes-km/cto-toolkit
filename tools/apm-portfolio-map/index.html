Application Portfolio Management

How to look at an entire application estate at once, rather than one system at a time, and decide where to invest, where to hold, where to modernize, and where to retire. Built on the Gartner TIME model, layered with risk scoring and a technical debt backlog.

This page covers the scoring logic at a conceptual level. The full simulator is a working three-sheet instrument (debt backlog, risk register, application portfolio) with a live dashboard, dropdown-governed scoring, and an M&A-specific variant for scoring a target company's portfolio during diligence, available through direct engagement.

3 linked sheets · 1 live dashboard · standing portfolio and M&A variants

Technical debt backlog

Every known debt item scored on severity, business impact, confidence, and remediation effort, rolled up to a priority ranking.

  • Direct remediation, annual carry, and contingent risk cost per item, each carrying its own confidence rating
  • Status and aging tracked against a target remediation window, so stale items surface instead of hiding
  • Rolls into portfolio-level totals: active items by severity, cost by confidence tier, overdue count

Application risk register

A five-factor risk score per application, independent of how valuable or well-built the system is.

  • Cybersecurity, compliance, operational resilience, vendor, and knowledge-concentration risk scored separately, then composited
  • Surfaces the application that's fine on paper but one departure or one vendor lapse from a bad quarter

Portfolio classification (TIME)

Every application placed on the Gartner TIME grid using a Business Value score and a Technical Health score.

  • Business Value built from five factors: strategic alignment, business criticality, user reach, differentiation, regulatory necessity
  • Technical Health built from five factors: technology currency, architecture quality, integration complexity, data architecture fit, technical debt
  • Composite Portfolio Score, cost score, and risk score sit alongside the classification as informational overlays and are kept out of its inputs

M&A-specific variant

The same instrument, repointed at a target company's application estate during diligence or the first 100 days.

  • Same scoring mechanics, framed around acquisition-specific questions: what's redundant with the acquirer's stack, what's a hidden Eliminate candidate the seller never flagged
  • Feeds directly into the Integration Architecture Decision Tree's merge, integrate, or coexist call per system
1

The Gartner TIME model, applied

Every application lands in one of four quadrants based on two independent scores: how much the business needs it, and how healthy it is technically.

The four quadrants

Invest: high value, high health. Fund it. Tolerate: low value, high health. Leave it alone and don't over-invest. Migrate: high value, low health. This is the modernization backlog, ranked by value at risk. Eliminate: low value, low health. These are the retirement candidates most portfolios carry more of than anyone admits.

Where it breaks down

Business Value gets inflated when the scorer is the system's own owner. Score it with someone outside the owning team in the room, or the whole portfolio drifts toward Invest and the model stops doing its job. Technical Health is the opposite failure: engineers score their own systems harshly relative to peers they haven't had to maintain, which skews Migrate counts up.

2

Risk as an independent axis, kept out of the TIME inputs

A system can be a clean Invest on the TIME grid and still be the one that takes the company down. Risk is scored and reported separately on purpose.

Why it's kept separate

Folding risk into Technical Health hides exactly the applications that need visibility: strategically critical, well-funded, actively developed, and sitting on a single unreplaceable engineer or an end-of-life dependency no one has scheduled work against. Reporting risk on its own axis keeps that combination visible instead of averaging it away.

What to do with a high-risk Invest

These get remediation budget ahead of Migrate-quadrant systems, even though they score well overall, because the downside is asymmetric: a breach or a key-person departure on a high-value system costs more than slow modernization debt on a low-value one.

3

Turning individual scores into a portfolio signal

One application's score is a data point. The value is in the aggregate: how many Eliminate candidates are still costing money, how concentrated the high-risk applications are, whether Migrate is growing or shrinking quarter over quarter.

What to track over time

Average Composite Portfolio Score by quadrant, count of high-risk applications, count of high-cost applications relative to their Business Value, and the ratio of Eliminate-classified applications still consuming a meaningful share of total run cost. Movement in these over two or three quarters tells you whether governance is changing behavior or only producing a nicer chart.

The trap

Treating this as a one-time inventory exercise. A TIME classification from eighteen months ago is closer to fiction than to data; technical health decays continuously even when business value hasn't moved, so the cadence matters as much as the scoring model itself.

Reading it

The portfolio signal, not the single score

The useful output is the share of run cost sitting in Migrate and Eliminate.

In this example

$1.7M of $2.85M, about 60 percent of run cost, sits in applications the organization has decided not to keep as they are. That figure belongs in the budget conversation with the CFO.

Common mistake

Treating the quadrant as the decision. A Migrate label with no funded plan and no date is a list of worries, and the same application appears on it next year.

Disaster Recovery & Business Continuity

What happens when a primary system, region, or data center becomes unavailable. Distinct from day-to-day incident response: this is about surviving the loss of something whole, not degrading gracefully around a partial failure.

General DR/BC framework. See Incident Response for the process that handles ordinary outages; this page is about recovery from the loss of an entire system, region, or facility.
Reference

RTO and RPO, defined honestly by tier

Recovery Time Objective is how long the business can tolerate a system being down. Recovery Point Objective is how much data loss is acceptable. Both should be set by business impact, not by what's technically easy.

TierExample systemsTypical RTOTypical RPO
Tier 1: Revenue-criticalPayment processing, core transaction systems, customer-facing platformMinutes to under 1 hourNear-zero, seconds to minutes
Tier 2: Operationally importantInternal tools, reporting, secondary customer channels4 to 24 hoursMinutes to hours
Tier 3: Deferred impactArchival systems, non-urgent internal toolsDaysUp to 24 hours
1

Setting RTO and RPO honestly

The single most common DR failure isn't a bad recovery, it's a target that was never realistic in the first place.

Practice

Ask the business owner what happens at 1 hour, 4 hours, and 24 hours of downtime for each system, in dollars or customer impact, then set the target from that answer. The target should hurt to miss; if missing it wouldn't matter, the system is probably a lower tier than it's currently classified.

Common failure mode

An RTO of 4 hours written into a policy document, never tested, while the actual infrastructure would take 2 days to genuinely recover. The number on paper and the number that's true are different, and nobody finds out which one is real until an actual disaster.

2

Backup testing discipline

A backup that has never been restored is a hypothesis, not a safety net.

Practice

Scheduled, automated restore tests, not manual ones someone remembers to run occasionally, with the actual restore time measured and compared against the stated RTO every time. A backup that restores successfully but takes 3x longer than the RTO promises is a finding, not a pass.

Common failure mode

Backups that run successfully and are never restored until the day they're needed, at which point corruption, missing dependencies, or forgotten configuration steps turn a routine recovery into an extended outage.

3

Tabletop exercises

Rehearsing the decision-making, not just the technical recovery steps.

Practice

A scheduled exercise, at least annually for Tier 1 systems, that walks a realistic scenario end to end: who declares the disaster, who has authority to fail over, how customers get notified, and how the team knows recovery is genuinely complete rather than just visually normal again.

Common failure mode

A DR plan that's technically sound but has never been rehearsed by the humans who'd execute it, so the actual event surfaces confusion about authority and sequencing that the document assumed away.

4

Multi-region versus single-region, honestly costed

Multi-region resilience is expensive, and the expense is often invisible until someone prices it out.

Practice

Cost the actual multi-region architecture, data replication, cross-region networking, the operational overhead of maintaining two live environments, against the quantified cost of the downtime it prevents. For genuinely revenue-critical systems, this usually clears the bar. For everything else, it often doesn't.

Common failure mode

Multi-region architecture applied uniformly across the whole estate because it feels like the responsible default, without anyone pricing whether the systems getting that treatment warrant it.

5

Answering the board question

"What's our recovery time if we lose a region" is a standard board question. The credible answer is specific, not reassuring.

A credible answer names the tier, states the tested RTO with the date it was last verified, and is honest about which systems don't yet meet target. "We're covered" without those specifics is the answer of someone who hasn't tested it recently, and board members who've heard that answer before know the difference.

DR red flags

Patterns that show up consistently when a disaster recovery program looks complete on paper and isn't.

  • RTO and RPO targets exist in a document but have never been tested against actual recovery time
  • The last full-scale restore test or tabletop exercise was more than a year ago, or has never happened
  • Recovery depends on a single person's institutional knowledge rather than a documented, rehearsed runbook
  • Backup and primary infrastructure share a failure domain, same region, same provider account, same network path, undermining the recovery they're meant to enable
  • Tier classification hasn't been revisited since the systems it covers changed significantly
Reference only. RTO and RPO targets, testing cadence, and regulatory DR obligations vary by industry; treat this as a starting framework, not a compliance checklist.

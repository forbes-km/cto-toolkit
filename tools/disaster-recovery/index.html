Disaster Recovery & Business Continuity

What happens when a primary system, region, or data center becomes unavailable. Distinct from day-to-day incident response: this is about surviving the loss of something whole rather than degrading gracefully around a partial failure.

General DR/BC framework. See Incident Response for the process that handles ordinary outages; this page is about recovery from the loss of an entire system, region, or facility.
Reference

RTO and RPO, defined by tier

Recovery Time Objective is how long the business can tolerate a system being down. Recovery Point Objective is how much data loss is acceptable. Both should be set by business impact rather than by what's technically easy.

TierExample systemsTypical RTOTypical RPO
Tier 1: Revenue-criticalPayment processing, core transaction systems, customer-facing platformMinutes to under 1 hourNear-zero, seconds to minutes
Tier 2: Operationally importantInternal tools, reporting, secondary customer channels4 to 24 hoursMinutes to hours
Tier 3: Deferred impactArchival systems, non-urgent internal toolsDaysUp to 24 hours
1

Setting RTO and RPO

The most common DR failure is a target that was never realistic in the first place; the bad recovery only exposes it.

Practice

Ask the business owner what happens at 1 hour, 4 hours, and 24 hours of downtime for each system, in dollars or customer impact, then set the target from that answer. The target should hurt to miss; if missing it wouldn't matter, the system is probably a lower tier than it's currently classified.

Common failure mode

An RTO of 4 hours written into a policy document, never tested, while the infrastructure would take 2 days to recover. The number on paper and the number that's true are different, and nobody finds out which one is real until a disaster.

2

Backup testing discipline

A backup that has never been restored is a hypothesis.

Practice

Scheduled, automated restore tests, rather than manual ones someone remembers to run occasionally, with the restore time measured and compared against the stated RTO every time. A backup that restores successfully but takes 3x longer than the RTO promises is a finding.

Common failure mode

Backups that run successfully and are never restored until the day they're needed, at which point corruption, missing dependencies, or forgotten configuration steps turn a routine recovery into an extended outage.

3

Tabletop exercises

Rehearsing the decision-making as well as the technical recovery steps.

Practice

A scheduled exercise, at least annually for Tier 1 systems, that walks a realistic scenario end to end: who declares the disaster, who has authority to fail over, how customers get notified, and how the team knows recovery is complete rather than just visually normal again.

Common failure mode

A DR plan that's technically sound but has never been rehearsed by the humans who'd execute it, so the event itself surfaces confusion about authority and sequencing that the document assumed away.

4

Multi-region versus single-region, costed

Multi-region resilience is expensive, and the expense is often invisible until someone prices it out.

Practice

Cost the multi-region architecture (data replication, cross-region networking, the operational overhead of maintaining two live environments) against the quantified cost of the downtime it prevents. For revenue-critical systems, this usually clears the bar. For everything else, it often doesn't.

Common failure mode

Multi-region architecture applied uniformly across the whole estate because it feels like the responsible default, without anyone pricing whether the systems getting that treatment warrant it.

5

Answering the board question

"What's our recovery time if we lose a region" is a standard board question. The credible answer is specific rather than reassuring.

A credible answer names the tier, states the tested RTO with the date it was last verified, and states which systems don't yet meet target. "We're covered" without those specifics is the answer of someone who hasn't tested it recently, and board members who've heard that answer before know the difference.

6

Communicating DR investment as insurance

See Working with the CFO for the general argument about when ROI framing doesn't fit. DR spend is one of the clearest examples.

Practice

Frame DR investment the way an insurance purchase gets framed: an expected-value calculation across many possible years rather than a promised return in any single year. The probability input in the calculator above is an estimate, and saying so out loud is more credible than pretending it is precise.

Common failure mode

Presenting a DR business case with the same confident ROI language used for a revenue-generating feature, which invites exactly the wrong question, "did we get that return this year," when the true answer is that a well-run DR program often means the disaster didn't happen to test it.

Ransomware changes the recovery design

Most DR plans assume the failure is an outage. Ransomware assumes the attacker has already reached the backups.

Design for it

Keep at least one backup copy immutable or offline, restore into an isolated clean environment, and rebuild identity systems first, since a compromised identity provider re-infects everything restored after it. The 3-2-1-1-0 rule summarizes it: three copies, two media, one offsite, one immutable or offline, zero errors on test restores.

Test for it

A tabletop that assumes backups are encrypted, and a timed restore of a critical system from the immutable copy alone. The time measured there, not the replication lag, is the realistic recovery time.

Checklist

DR red flags

Patterns that show up consistently when a disaster recovery program looks complete on paper and isn't.

  • RTO and RPO targets exist in a document but have never been tested against measured recovery time
  • The last full-scale restore test or tabletop exercise was more than a year ago, or has never happened
  • Recovery depends on a single person's institutional knowledge rather than a documented, rehearsed runbook
  • Backup and primary infrastructure share a failure domain, same region, same provider account, same network path, undermining the recovery they're meant to enable
  • Tier classification hasn't been revisited since the systems it covers changed significantly
  • DR investment gets pitched with certain-return ROI language rather than framed as insurance against a probabilistic event
Reference only. RTO and RPO targets, testing cadence, and regulatory DR obligations vary by industry; treat this as a starting framework rather than a compliance checklist.

Working with the CEO

The relationship that determines whether everything else in this toolkit gets funded, listened to, or quietly ignored. The single most consequential relationship a CTO manages, and the one least often discussed explicitly.

What the CEO needs from a CTO

1

Telling the CEO no

The moment that defines the relationship more than any status update ever will.

How it works

Arrive with the tradeoff already worked out, not just the refusal: "we can hit that date if we cut X, or hit full scope two weeks later, which do you want." A no with a stated alternative reads as judgment. A no with nothing behind it reads as an obstacle.

Where it goes wrong

A flat no delivered late, often after the CEO has already committed to the date externally, which turns a technical disagreement into a credibility problem for both people, and makes the next no much harder to deliver and to hear.

2

A worked example

What the mechanics above look like against a real, common scenario.

The CEO wants to announce a launch date publicly at a conference six weeks out. The CTO's honest engineering estimate is ten weeks for the full feature set.

Rather than a flat "we can't hit that," the CTO brings the tradeoff already worked out: a limited beta with three core features ships in six weeks, the full launch stays on the real ten-week timeline. The CEO announces the beta at the conference instead of the full launch. Engineering hits the beta date. The full launch ships on the timeline that was true from the start. Nobody's public credibility gets damaged, because what got promised matched what was deliverable, and the CEO had a real, positive announcement to make either way.

3

Founder CEO versus professional CEO

The same relationship, shaped very differently depending on who's in the role.

Founder CEO

Often has deep intuitive product and technical instincts even without a formal technical background, and personal identity tied closely to the company. Disagreements can feel personal in a way they don't with a professional CEO. Technical credibility gets earned through demonstrated judgment over time, not through a title.

Professional or hired CEO

Usually more process-oriented, expecting the same structured decision-rights model and reporting cadence used at previous companies. The relationship starts more transactional and earns trust faster through consistent, predictable execution rather than through personal rapport built up over years.

4

When technology should be invisible, and when it's the strategy

Getting this posture wrong in either direction costs credibility.

Invisible

For most companies, most of the time, technology is an enabler. The goal is for the CEO to rarely have to think about it, and that quiet is a sign of health, not neglect, as long as it isn't masking a risk building underneath.

The strategic differentiator

When the technology is the competitive edge, what customers are buying is the technical capability, invisibility is the wrong posture. The CTO needs to be actively pushing technology into the CEO's strategic conversations, not staying quietly out of them.

5

Handling a CEO who's miscalibrated in either direction

Two opposite failure modes, requiring opposite corrections.

Over-indexes on technology

Treats every technical detail as something worth personally weighing in on. Give them a small, explicit set of decisions that warrant their attention, rather than either blocking them out entirely or letting them relitigate implementation details that don't.

Treats technology as a cost center

This is a translation failure, not a values failure. Find the specific business metric technology investment moves, and lead every conversation with that number, not with technical merit the CEO has no framework to evaluate.

6

Building trust before you need it

The high-stakes moments described above draw on a trust balance that has to be built somewhere else first.

Practice

Regular, low-stakes contact, the weekly touchpoint described below, builds the trust that a high-stakes "telling the CEO no" moment later draws on. A CEO who's watched a CTO be right about smaller things repeatedly extends more benefit of the doubt when it matters most.

Common failure mode

The CTO only appears in the CEO's calendar when something's wrong, which means every interaction the CEO has with the CTO is colored by a problem, and trust never gets the chance to compound the way it does with regular, unremarkable contact.

7

Escalation and cadence, stated explicitly

What should reach the CEO, and on what rhythm, shouldn't be reinvented every time something comes up.

Escalate: a material change in risk, anything crossing a stated board-visibility threshold, and any commitment at risk of slipping, as early as it's known. Handle independently: implementation approach, internal architecture decisions, and day-to-day engineering management. A regular weekly touchpoint covers the practical items; a deeper monthly or quarterly conversation, tied to the broader operating cadence, covers strategy and portfolio; anything crossing an escalation threshold gets raised immediately, not saved for the next scheduled meeting.

Signs the relationship is unhealthy

Patterns that show up when the CEO-CTO relationship has drifted, regardless of how well either person would describe it individually.

  • The CEO learns about a material technology risk from someone other than the CTO
  • Every technology conversation with the CEO is either a routine status update or a crisis, nothing in between
  • The CTO can't name the one or two business metrics the CEO tracks
  • Disagreements get resolved by whoever escalates loudest, not by a stated decision-rights model
  • The CEO has stopped asking the CTO's opinion before making technology-adjacent decisions
  • Most contact between the two only happens when something is already wrong
Reference only. The specifics of this relationship depend heavily on company stage, founder background, and board composition; adapt accordingly.

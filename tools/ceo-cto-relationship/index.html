Working with the CEO

The relationship that determines whether everything else in this toolkit gets funded, listened to, or politely ignored. It is the most consequential relationship a CTO manages, and the one people talk about least.

The defining moment

Telling the CEO no

This shapes the relationship more than any status update ever will.

A no with nothing behind it reads as an obstacle. A no that arrives with the tradeoff already worked out reads as judgment: we can hit that date if we cut this, or ship everything two weeks later, which do you want? The CEO still makes the call. You have just made sure it is a real choice.

Timing matters as much as framing. A no delivered after the CEO has committed to a date externally turns a technical disagreement into a credibility problem for both of you, and it makes the next no harder to give and harder to hear. If a date looks wrong, say so the day you see it.

A typical case. The CEO wants to announce a launch at a conference six weeks out. The engineering estimate for the full feature set is ten weeks. Instead of saying it can't be done, the CTO offers a limited beta with three core features in six weeks and the full launch on the ten-week plan. The CEO announces the beta. Engineering hits it, the full release ships when it was always going to, and nobody's public credibility takes a hit, because what got promised matched what could be delivered.

The trust account

The monthly one-page technology narrative

The hard moments above draw on trust built somewhere else first. This is where most of it gets built.

A CEO who has watched you be right about small things, month after month, gives you the benefit of the doubt when something big comes up. A CTO who only appears on the CEO's calendar when something is wrong never builds that balance, because every interaction is colored by a problem.

The note is one page, written, sent before the monthly one-on-one, with no acronyms. Five sections, always in the same order:

  • What changed in the business that affects technology. A new market, a lost deal, a regulator's letter, and what it means for the plan
  • Three commitments, on or off track. Each with a date and a number, and for anything off track the recovery option already worked out
  • Risk movement. What moved relative to the stated risk appetite, in either direction
  • Decisions needed from the CEO. Two at most, each with a recommendation and the tradeoff stated plainly
  • What I am not worried about, and why. The section CEOs value most, because it tells them where they can stop spending attention

The format matters less than the consistency. After three months the CEO knows where to look, and a surprise is rare enough that when it does come, it is taken seriously.

New CEO or new CTO

The first 90 days

A new CEO, or a new CTO joining an existing CEO, resets the relationship to zero. The first quarter decides whether it builds or stalls.

WindowWhat to doWhat to avoid
Weeks 1 to 2Learn their top three priorities, how they like to receive information, and what they were told about technology before arrivingPresenting the technology strategy before understanding theirs
Weeks 3 to 6Agree the escalation rules and cadence in writing. Send the first one-page narrativeAssuming the previous CEO's norms carry over
Weeks 7 to 12Bring one well-framed recommendation with a real tradeoff. Review the risk appetite togetherSaving every hard conversation until trust feels established. Trust is built by having them well
Warning sign

The one that matters most

Most strain in this relationship shows up in one place first.

The CEO hears about a material technology risk from someone other than you: a board member, a customer, the CFO, a vendor. Once that happens, the CEO starts building a second channel of information about technology, and your account becomes one input among several. Other symptoms usually follow: they stop asking your view before technology-adjacent decisions, and conversations swing between routine status and crisis with nothing in between.

The repair is the same machinery on this page, started now: the escalation rules written down, the monthly narrative sent without fail, and the next risk raised by you, early, before anyone else can.
